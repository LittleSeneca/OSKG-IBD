# Evidence Brief: What Justifies Treat-to-Target Over Symptom-Based Management?

**Question:** What is the evidence justifying treat-to-target (objective, endoscopic/imaging-based targets) over traditional symptom-based management in IBD?

**Date:** 2026-08-01
**Sources consulted:** 476-claim OSKG-IBD graph, focused on symptom-inflammation disconnect, mucosal healing targets, and progressive disease framework. Guideline claims from ACG (2018, 2019), AGA (2020, 2021), ECCO (2019, 2020, 2022), BSG (2019).

---

## Core Finding

**Treat-to-target is the consensus standard across all major guidelines, but the evidence for specific targets is graded as low to very low quality.** The justification rests on three pillars, each operating at a different level of evidence:

### Pillar 1: Symptoms Do Not Track Inflammation (Strong Evidence)

[[claim-summary-statement-symptoms-crohns-disease-correlate-well|acg-cd2018-2]] states the foundational premise: symptoms of Crohn's disease do not correlate well with active mucosal inflammation. This is the most clinically transformative concept in modern IBD management. The implication is clear: treating symptoms alone risks undertreating patients with silent inflammation, leading to progressive bowel damage. This claim underlies the entire treat-to-target paradigm and is consistently endorsed across ACG, AGA, ECCO, and BSG guidelines.

**Evidence quality:** Well-documented in multiple studies. A convergence point in the graph: 3+ independent sources converge on this claim.

### Pillar 2: IBD Is a Progressive Disease (Moderate Evidence)

[[claim-progressive-disease-requiring-early-intensive-monitoring-individualized|ecco-cd2020-1]] frames CD as a progressive disease where chronic untreated inflammation results in cumulative bowel damage -- strictures, fistulas, surgical resection -- even when the patient feels well. This natural history provides the rationale for preemptive, objective-monitoring-driven therapy. ECCO 2020 formalizes this into risk-stratified individualized treatment, going beyond ACG's simpler low/moderate/high-risk framework.

**Evidence quality:** Moderate. The progressive damage trajectory is documented in natural history studies. The benefit of preemptive escalation in altering that trajectory is less well-established.

### Pillar 3: Mucosal Healing Is a Validated Therapeutic Target (Low-Quality Evidence for Hard Outcomes)

[[claim-mucosal-healing-mayo-endoscopic-subscore-treatment-target|acg-uc2019-3]] establishes mucosal healing (Mayo 0-1) as the treatment target, but the confidence rating is only medium and the evidence grade is low. The STRIDE consensus (2015) set composite endpoints; STRIDE-II (2021, postdating the ACG 2019 guideline) elevated histologic healing from "not a target" to "an adjunctive measure." [[claim-mucosal-healing-important-therapeutic-goal-both-validated|ecco-diag2019-6]] adds the emerging endpoint of transmural healing (assessed by MRE/IUS), which is particularly relevant for CD where mucosal-only assessment may miss deep disease.

**Evidence quality:** The link between mucosal healing and reduced hospitalization/surgery is documented in multiple cohort studies. However, no prospective trial has used mucosal healing as a randomized target, and the ACG 2019 guideline explicitly notes "very low quality of evidence" for FC as a treatment surrogate.

## What Justifies the Paradigm Shift Despite Weak Direct Evidence?

The treat-to-target paradigm is justified by the convergence of three lines of reasoning, none of which is individually conclusive:

1. **The symptom-inflammation disconnect is settled science.** Treating symptoms without objective inflammation data is demonstrably insufficient.
2. **The progressive damage model provides biological urgency.** Waiting for symptoms to declare themselves means waiting for irreversible structural damage. This is a precautionary principle argument, not a RCT-proven strategy.
3. **Biologic-era therapies make the targets achievable.** Before anti-TNF agents, mucosal healing was aspirational. With biologics, deep remission is attainable for a substantial proportion of patients, making treat-to-target feasible.

The graph reveals that **no guideline claims that treat-to-target is supported by high-quality direct evidence from randomized trials comparing treat-to-target against symptom-based care.** The confidence ratings are uniformly medium or low, and the evidence grades are level 3-5 (cohort studies, expert opinion). The paradigm shift is consensus-driven rather than evidence-driven -- a judgment that the observational data and biological rationale outweigh the absence of definitive trials.

## Guideline Alignment

The major guidelines converge on treat-to-target but differ on specific targets:

| Guideline | Target | Histologic Endpoint | Transmural |
|-----------|--------|---------------------|------------|
| ACG UC 2019 | Mayo 0-1 | Not validated | Not addressed |
| ACG CD 2018 | Endoscopic remission | Not addressed | Not addressed |
| AGA UC 2020 | Mucosal healing | Not a target | Not addressed |
| ECCO 2022 | Mucosal healing | Adjunctive in select patients | Not addressed |
| BSG 2019 | Mucosal healing | Not addressed | Not addressed |

The European guidelines (ECCO) are ahead of the US guidelines (ACG, AGA) on transmural and histologic endpoints but still stop short of mandating them.

## Confidence Assessment

**Medium for the paradigm, low for specific targets.** The justification for treating to an objective target rather than symptoms is strong at the level of clinical reasoning and observational evidence. The justification for any specific target (Mayo 0 vs. 1, endoscopic vs. histologic, mucosal vs. transmural) is weak and evolving. STRIDE-II (2021) partially closed the gap on histologic healing; more data is accumulating on transmural healing. The graph reflects a field in methodological transition: the targets are getting more ambitious faster than the evidence base is maturing.

## Unresolved Research Questions

1. Would a randomized trial of treat-to-target vs. symptom-based management in biologic-naive patients show a difference in bowel resection, hospitalization, or quality-adjusted life years -- and would that trial be ethical to conduct given the current consensus?
2. What is the incremental benefit of histologic over endoscopic healing in UC? At what cost?
3. Does transmural healing (MRE/IUS) in CD predict outcomes better than mucosal healing alone -- and can it be operationalized into a clinical target?
