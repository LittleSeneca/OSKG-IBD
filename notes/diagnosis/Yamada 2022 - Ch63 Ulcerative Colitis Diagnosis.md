# Yamada 7E -- Ch 63: Ulcerative Colitis: Clinical Manifestations and Management

This chapter covers the full clinical spectrum of ulcerative colitis: epidemiology, clinical presentation, natural history, diagnosis (laboratory, endoscopic, histological, imaging), differential diagnosis, disease activity and severity scoring, medical management, acute severe UC, complications, and UC in special populations. Authored by Singh and Dulai (UC San Diego). For this knowledge graph, the diagnostic portions (endoscopy, biomarkers, histology, imaging, scoring systems, differential diagnosis) are extracted in detail; the treatment sections are noted but not deeply mined (treatment notes will be written separately from dedicated therapeutic sources).

---

## Claim 1: Diagnosis of UC rests on a clinical-endoscopic-histological triad; no single test is diagnostic

**Author's claim:** "Diagnosis of ulcerative colitis is based on clinical, endoscopic, and histological findings. In a patient with symptoms suggestive of ulcerative colitis (rectal bleeding, diarrhea and/or urgency), the diagnostic approach typically involves laboratory tests (serum and stool), endoscopy, and histology."

**Evidence presented:** The chapter structures the diagnostic approach around this triad. Laboratory investigations (CBC, liver enzymes, albumin, CRP/ESR) assess inflammatory burden and complications but are not diagnostic. Stool testing rules out infection. Endoscopy visualizes the mucosal pattern. Histology confirms architectural distortion. No single finding is pathognomonic -- the diagnosis requires concordance across all three domains.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH. This is the universal standard of care. Every major guideline (ACG 2019, AGA 2020, ECCO 2022, BSG 2019) endorses this triad.

**What's at stake:** Because no single test is diagnostic, misdiagnosis risk is highest when one domain is relied upon in isolation. A patient with rectal bleeding and endoscopic inflammation could still have infectious colitis if histology shows no chronic architectural changes. Conversely, histological architectural distortion in an asymptomatic patient does not establish UC. This triad approach protects against overdiagnosis.

**Who disagrees:** No disagreement. This is settled clinical practice.

**My assessment:** The triad model is sound but practically challenging in resource-limited settings where histopathological expertise may be unavailable. The reliance on histology also means diagnosis is inherently delayed (biopsy processing takes days), which is clinically acceptable for a chronic disease but creates a window of diagnostic uncertainty.

## Claim 2: Stool testing to exclude infection is mandatory before diagnosing UC, and C. difficile co-infection portends a worse prognosis

**Author's claim:** "Microbiological testing using stool cultures for bacterial pathogens, panel-based nucleic acid amplification tests for potential bacterial, viral, and parasitic pathogens and, most importantly, polymerase chain reaction for Clostridium difficile toxin are indicated in all patients with diarrhea with suspected ulcerative colitis."

**Evidence presented:** Approximately 10-15% of UC patients with diarrhea test positive for C. difficile, and another 15-20% may test positive for other organisms. C. difficile infection in UC patients is associated with higher disease severity, hospitalization risk, colectomy risk, and mortality. Multiplex gastrointestinal pathogen panels offer advantages (shorter turnaround, consolidated workflow, increased sensitivity) but cannot distinguish viable organisms from remnant nucleic acid, leading to potential overdiagnosis and inappropriate antibiotic use. Non-C. difficile infections do not necessarily portend a poorer prognosis but often lead to treatment delays.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The prevalence data and prognostic implications are from multiple cohort studies.

**What's at stake:** Missing C. difficile in a UC flare leads to corticosteroid escalation when the patient actually needs antibiotics -- a dangerous clinical error. Conversely, attributing a UC flare to a non-pathogenic PCR finding delays UC-directed therapy. The 10-15% C. difficile prevalence means this is not rare -- it should be tested in every suspected UC presentation and every flare.

**Who disagrees:** Debate about whether universal multiplex PCR panels are superior to targeted C. difficile testing plus selective bacterial culture. The multiplex panels' inability to distinguish colonization from infection is the main argument against universal adoption.

**My assessment:** The chapter's recommendation to test all patients is correct. The yield (10-15% for C. difficile alone) justifies universal testing. The caveat about multiplex panel overdiagnosis is important and well-stated.

## Claim 3: Fecal calprotectin is the most useful non-invasive biomarker for UC, with threshold-dependent sensitivity and specificity

**Author's claim:** "A metaanalysis of 2499 patients observed a pooled sensitivity and specificity of 49% and 92% for C-reactive protein, and 88% and 73% for fecal calprotectin in IBD. Fecal calprotectin is a more sensitive biomarker of mucosal inflammation than C-reactive protein, and is more sensitive in ulcerative colitis compared to Crohn's disease."

**Evidence presented:** The 2,499-patient meta-analysis (Mosli et al. 2015) provides pooled test characteristics. Threshold optimization: sensitivity is maximized at <50 microg/g (rules out inflammation), specificity is maximized at >250 microg/g (rules in moderate-severe inflammation). In patients with normal stool frequency and no rectal bleeding, FC <50 rules out moderate-severe inflammation with >95% accuracy. In patients with increased stool frequency and rectal bleeding >50% of stools, FC >250 rules in moderate-severe inflammation with >95% accuracy. Practical limitations: variability with time of collection, temperature-dependent degradation, and patient access to lab facilities.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The meta-analytic data is robust. The threshold-dependent clinical interpretation (rule-out at <50, rule-in at >250) is supported by pooled analyses from clinical trial data.

**What's at stake:** Fecal calprotectin is the closest thing to a non-invasive "endoscopy surrogate" in UC. Its proper use can reduce unnecessary colonoscopies (when FC is low in low-risk patients) and trigger timely treatment escalation (when FC is high despite symptomatic improvement). However, the chapter cautions that even when clinical + biochemical targets are met, "secondary assessments of mucosal inflammation are still required" -- calprotectin is adjunctive, not a replacement for endoscopy.

**Who disagrees:** Some clinicians argue for tighter calprotectin targets (e.g., <100 rather than <250) for treatment escalation decisions, particularly in post-operative or high-risk patients. The chapter's data supports 50/250 as the evidence-based thresholds.

**Alternative reading:** Fecal lactoferrin is an alternative neutrophil-derived biomarker with similar performance characteristics, though less widely studied than calprotectin.

**My assessment:** The 88% sensitivity vs 73% specificity trade-off is important. Calprotectin is better at ruling OUT disease than ruling it IN -- a negative calprotectin in a low-risk patient effectively excludes IBD. But a positive calprotectin (>250) still has a ~27% false positive rate for moderate-severe inflammation. The clinical algorithm in Figure 63.12 (combining symptoms + calprotectin + endoscopy) is well-designed and represents current best practice.

## Claim 4: Endoscopic diagnosis of UC requires continuous, circumferential inflammation beginning at the rectum, with specific mucosal features aiding distinction from Crohn's

**Author's claim:** "The disease presents as diffuse, continuous inflammation beginning in the rectum, with proximal extension that varies among individual patients. For initial evaluation, however, it is advisable to perform a full colonoscopy with ileal examination, to rule out Crohn's disease and to determine the proximal margin of disease."

**Evidence presented:** Table 63.1 (adapted from Hogan et al. 1980) provides a specificity index for endoscopic features distinguishing UC from CD. Features favoring UC: contiguous distribution (+++), circumferential (+++), rectal involvement (+++), friability (+++), granularity (+++), overt colitis (+++). Features favoring CD: cobblestoned (+++), ileal involvement (++++), discrete lesions (+++), ulcers >1cm (+++), linear ulcers (+++), serpiginous ulcers (+++). In partially treated patients, UC mucosal changes become more focal, potentially mimicking CD. In pancolitis, the ileocecal valve is patulous, and backwash ileitis (distal terminal ileum involvement) can occur, which must be distinguished from CD ileitis. Periappendiceal or cecal patch inflammation may be present in patients with distal disease without indicating CD.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The endoscopic features described are the standard teaching in gastroenterology training.

**What's at stake:** The distinction between UC and CD has massive therapeutic implications (surgery is curative for UC but not CD; certain biologics are approved for one but not the other). Approximately 2% of initial UC diagnoses are reclassified as CD or indeterminate colitis. The most common diagnostic error: treating partially treated UC as CD because the resolving inflammation appears patchy.

**Who disagrees:** The traditional teaching that UC always spares the anus and always involves the rectum has exceptions. Rectal sparing can occur in treated or pediatric UC. Backwash ileitis challenges the "UC never involves the small bowel" rule.

**My assessment:** Table 63.1, though based on a 1980 paper, remains clinically useful because it codifies what experienced endoscopists recognize intuitively. The key takeaway: no single feature is diagnostic; the overall pattern (continuous, circumferential, rectal, superficial) is what matters. The caution about partially treated UC mimicking CD is particularly important -- this is a common source of misdiagnosis.

## Claim 5: Histological architectural distortion distinguishes chronic UC from acute self-limited colitis, and histological remission predicts lower relapse risk

**Author's claim:** "While there are no pathognomonic histological criteria for the diagnosis of ulcerative colitis, a primary distinction between ulcerative colitis and acute self-limited (infectious) colitis is architectural distortion."

**Evidence presented:** In UC, the normal vertical ("test tube") alignment of glands is distorted, branched, and irregularly shaped. The lamina propria is expanded by lymphocytes, plasma cells, eosinophils, and neutrophils. Neutrophilic infiltration produces crypt abscesses -- characteristic but not pathognomonic (seen in other inflammatory and infectious conditions). In quiescent UC, architectural distortion persists but acute inflammation (neutrophils, crypt abscesses) is absent. Regenerating epithelium produces cellular atypia that must be distinguished from dysplasia (stratification of nuclei, loss of polarity = neoplasia). A meta-analysis of 10 studies (757 patients in endoscopic remission): those with histological remission had 63% lower risk of clinical relapse vs persistent histological activity (annual relapse risk: 5.0% vs ~14% for endoscopic remission without histological remission).

**Confidence:** HIGH. Architectural distortion as the chronicity marker is standard histopathology teaching. The histological remission meta-analysis provides strong evidence for the prognostic value of histological healing.

**What's at stake:** The distinction between acute colitis (infection, self-limited) and chronic colitis (IBD) hinges on histology. If biopsies show acute inflammation without architectural distortion, the diagnosis is infectious colitis regardless of the endoscopic appearance. Conversely, architectural distortion in an asymptomatic patient establishes chronicity. The histological remission data raises the question of whether "histological remission" should become a formal treatment target -- currently it is not (STRIDE-II recommends endoscopic remission as the target), but the 63% risk reduction is compelling.

**Who disagrees:** Some pathologists argue that architectural distortion can occur in severe or prolonged infectious colitis, making the acute/chronic distinction less absolute than traditionally taught. The clinical context (duration of symptoms, stool studies, treatment response) should always be integrated.

**My assessment:** The histological remission data (5% annual relapse with histological remission vs 14% with endoscopic remission alone) is one of the most clinically important findings in this chapter. It suggests that the current treatment target (endoscopic improvement, MES 0 or 1) may be insufficient -- patients with MES 0 who still have histological activity are at meaningfully higher relapse risk. This supports the trend toward "targeting histological remission" in clinical trials.

## Claim 6: Disease activity is assessed by composite indices combining symptoms, endoscopy, and biomarkers; disease severity is a longitudinal prognostic construct

**Author's claim:** "For all patients with ulcerative colitis, two aspects are vital for decision making: disease activity assessment, which is the cross-sectional assessment of symptomatic and endoscopic activity at that time point, and disease severity assessment, which involves evaluating the longitudinal disease course and prognosticating patients as being at low or high risk of complications."

**Evidence presented:** The Mayo Clinic score is the most widely used activity index, incorporating stool frequency (0-3), rectal bleeding (0-3), physician global assessment (0-3), and endoscopic subscore (0-3). The PRO2 (patient-reported outcome) uses only stool frequency and rectal bleeding -- resolution of rectal bleeding (RBS 0) plus normalization of stool frequency (SFS 0-1) is the current recommended clinical treatment target. The UCEIS (Ulcerative Colitis Endoscopic Index of Severity) scores vascular pattern, bleeding, and erosions/ulcers (0-8). The ACG activity classification: remission (MES 0-1), mild (<4 stools/day, intermittent bleeding), moderate (4-6 stools/day, frequent bleeding, urgency), severe (>6 stools/day, systemic signs), fulminant (>10 stools/day, severe pain, systemic toxicity). For disease severity, a Delphi panel identified the most important prognostic factors (in order): active ulcers/erosions on endoscopy, impact on daily activities, elevated CRP, lack of response to prior biologics/immunosuppressives, hospitalization in last 12 months, corticosteroid use in last 12 months, anemia, increased stool frequency, low albumin, extensive colitis, anorectal symptoms, and rectal bleeding.

**Confidence:** HIGH for the activity indices (validated in multiple clinical trials). MEDIUM-HIGH for the severity Delphi -- it is expert consensus, not derived from a prediction model.

**What's at stake:** The activity/severity distinction is clinically essential but often conflated. A patient can have severe disease activity (hospitalized with acute severe UC) but low disease severity (first flare, rapid response to steroids, no prognostic risk factors). Conversely, a patient can have mild current activity but high severity (extensive colitis, prior biologic failure, low albumin). Treatment decisions should be driven by both: activity determines the urgency of intervention; severity determines the choice of maintenance therapy.

**Who disagrees:** Different clinical trials use different indices (Mayo, UCEIS, modified Mayo, partial Mayo), making cross-trial comparison difficult. The FDA and EMA have different standards for what constitutes clinically meaningful improvement.

**My assessment:** The PRO2 (stool frequency + rectal bleeding) is a practical clinical tool because it doesn't require endoscopy. The finding that RBS 2-3 has >90% positive predictive value for moderate-severe endoscopic inflammation is clinically useful -- persistent rectal bleeding despite therapy is a reliable signal that endoscopic disease is active. The converse finding (RBS 0 only has ~50% chance of MES 0) is equally important -- absence of bleeding does not guarantee endoscopic remission, which is why confirmatory endoscopy is still needed before de-escalating therapy.

## Claim 7: The differential diagnosis of UC is broad and requires systematic exclusion of infectious, ischemic, medication-induced, and other inflammatory colitides

**Author's claim:** "Ulcerative colitis should be considered in any patient who presents with rectal bleeding or chronic diarrhea... The most common problem in the differential diagnosis of an acute presentation of ulcerative colitis (including flare-ups of quiescent disease) is ruling out infectious colitis."

**Evidence presented:** The differential diagnosis organized by the chapter:
- **Infectious colitis:** Shigella, amoeba, Giardia, E. coli O157:H7, Campylobacter. Endoscopic findings can be identical to UC. Key distinction: infectious diarrhea is typically days to weeks; UC is typically weeks to months. Amebiasis produces 5-15mm scattered ulcers with yellow exudate, most common in cecum/ascending colon.
- **IBS:** Generally lacks rectal bleeding, nocturnal symptoms, incontinence, or weight loss. 3-6x higher risk of IBS symptoms coexisting with IBD.
- **Microscopic colitis (collagenous/lymphocytic):** Chronic watery diarrhea, normal or mildly edematous endoscopy, diagnosis by histology. Associated with NSAIDs, PPIs, autoimmune conditions.
- **Ischemic colitis:** Elderly patients or vasoconstrictive drug users. Typically spares the rectum, involves the splenic flexure "watershed" area.
- **Diverticular colitis / SCAD:** Patchy erythema around diverticula in sigmoid. Inflammation extending to rectum or proximal to diverticular segment suggests UC.
- **Diversion colitis:** In excluded colon segments. Due to SCFA deficiency. Treatable with SCFA enemas.
- **Solitary rectal ulcer:** Young patients with constipation/straining. Circumferential ulceration with heaped-up margins on first rectal fold. Histology shows mucosal prolapse.
- **Immune checkpoint inhibitor colitis:** Anti-CTLA4 (ipilimumab) incidence 10%, anti-PD1 2%, combination 14%. Onset 5-15 weeks after starting. Endoscopic and histological findings identical to UC. Pre-existing UC flares in ~50% of patients.
- **Sexually transmitted proctitis:** N. gonorrhoeae, Chlamydia (LGV), HSV, T. pallidum. HIV risk factors should prompt specific testing.

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is the standard differential taught in gastroenterology. The checkpoint inhibitor colitis section is especially timely (2022).

**What's at stake:** The most dangerous differential is missing ischemic colitis (can progress to infarction) or infectious colitis (inappropriate immunosuppression). The most common error is attributing new UC symptoms to IBS without ruling out active inflammation. The checkpoint inhibitor colitis is increasingly relevant as immunotherapy indications expand.

**Who disagrees:** The distinction between diverticular colitis and UC can be genuinely difficult and some cases of "segmental colitis associated with diverticular disease" (SCAD) may represent a forme fruste of IBD rather than a truly separate entity.

**My assessment:** This differential diagnosis section is comprehensive and clinically practical. The emphasis on ruling out infection before diagnosing UC (or attributing a flare to UC) is correctly stated as the first step. The checkpoint inhibitor colitis section is forward-looking and important for oncologists and gastroenterologists alike.

## Claim 8: Serological markers (pANCA, ASCA) have suboptimal diagnostic performance and should NOT be used to screen for or diagnose UC

**Author's claim:** "The diagnostic performance of these serological tests is suboptimal and they should not used to screen for, or diagnose, ulcerative colitis (or Crohn's disease)."

**Evidence presented:** pANCA is present in 50-80% of UC patients but also in up to 31% of CD patients (especially colonic CD with UC-like endoscopic appearance). ASCA is present in 20% of UC patients. Antinuclear antibodies present in 40-50% of UC but also in healthy subjects and other autoimmune diseases. The combination of pANCA+/ASCA- may help distinguish UC from CD or indeterminate colitis, but "this is rarely necessary in clinical practice." Antimicrobial antibody panels may have prognostic value in CD but prognostic utility in UC "has not been examined."

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is consistent with every major guideline. The chapter is explicit that serology should NOT be used diagnostically.

**What's at stake:** Commercial IBD serology panels (Prometheus, etc.) are still marketed and used. Patients with positive pANCA are sometimes labeled as having UC on the basis of serology alone -- this is incorrect and potentially harmful. The chapter's clear language ("should not be used to screen for, or diagnose") is a necessary corrective.

**Who disagrees:** Some clinicians use serology in the setting of indeterminate colitis to guide surgical decision-making (pANCA+/ASCA- favors UC and thus IPAA surgery; ASCA+/pANCA- favors CD and thus cautions against IPAA). The chapter acknowledges this limited role.

**My assessment:** This is one of the most practically important claims in the chapter. Serology for IBD diagnosis is a persistent source of misuse in community practice. The chapter's unambiguous recommendation against diagnostic use is well-supported by the evidence.

**Strongest section:** The integrated diagnostic framework (Claims 1-5). The chapter builds from clinical presentation through laboratory testing to endoscopy and histology in a logical sequence, with quantitative performance characteristics for each diagnostic tool. The calprotectin threshold data is particularly well-presented with clinical decision-making implications.

**Weakest section:** The differential diagnosis (Claim 7) is comprehensive but necessarily shallow for each entity. A reader trying to distinguish ischemic colitis from UC based on this chapter alone would need to consult additional sources for the ischemic colitis diagnostic criteria.

**Cross-reference opportunities:** The endoscopic features in Table 63.1 should be cross-referenced with Ch 64 (CD clinical, which covers the CD-specific endoscopic features in more detail). The fecal calprotectin data connects to the clinical guidelines (AGA, ACG, ECCO) which provide more specific cut-point recommendations. The histological remission data (63% risk reduction) is an emerging concept that will likely feature in future treatment guidelines.
