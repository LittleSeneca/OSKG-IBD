# Yamada 7E -- Ch 64: Crohn's Disease: Clinical Manifestations and Management

This chapter covers the full clinical spectrum of Crohn's disease: history (from Burrill Crohn's 1932 case series to modern genetic understanding), clinical presentation, extraintestinal manifestations, diagnostic methods (endoscopy, histology, imaging, biomarkers), disease classification (Montreal), differential diagnosis, disease activity assessment, medical and surgical management, and complications. Authored by Ha, McGovern, and Melmed at Cedars-Sinai/UCLA. For this knowledge graph, the diagnostic and classification portions are extracted in detail; treatment sections are noted for cross-reference.

---

## Claim 1: The clinical presentation of CD is heterogeneous -- symptoms depend on anatomic location, inflammatory phenotype, and presence of complications

**Author's claim:** "The clinical presentation of patients with CD is diverse and heterogeneous, with symptoms that can range from silent, insidious progression of mid-small bowel inflammation that might be misdiagnosed for years as 'irritable bowel syndrome' to a more acute presentation of diarrhea and/or weight loss, GI hemorrhage, perianal abscess/fistula, or intestinal obstruction."

**Evidence presented:** Disease distribution: terminal ileum most common (60% have at least some ileal involvement); about half of ileal patients also have colonic involvement (usually right colon); ~20% isolated colonic involvement; ~30% confined to small bowel; ~5% upper tract (esophagus, stomach, duodenum, jejunum). Perianal disease in ~25% of patients, usually associated with distal colonic or small bowel inflammation. Symptoms correlate with location: dysphagia/odynophagia/nausea with upper tract; right lower quadrant pain and diarrhea with ileal disease; rectal bleeding and urgency with colonic involvement; perianal pain and drainage with fistulizing perianal disease. Up to 20% of CD patients are obese (not all have cachexia). In 5-15% of isolated colonic cases, UC vs CD cannot be initially distinguished (IBD-U/indeterminate colitis). Key differentiating features from UC at presentation: nonbloody diarrhea and weight loss favor CD; endoscopic/histological patchiness or rectal sparing favors CD; family history of CD favors CD.

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is the standard clinical teaching and is consistent with population-based cohort data.

**What's at stake:** The insidious onset means diagnostic delay is common -- patients may have symptoms for months or years before diagnosis. Misdiagnosis as IBS is especially common in patients with non-stricturing, non-penetrating small bowel CD without rectal bleeding or weight loss. The chapter emphasizes: "there are no hard and fast 'rules'" -- CD can present at any age, in any ethnicity, and at any BMI.

**Who disagrees:** No meaningful disagreement about clinical heterogeneity. Debate exists about whether IBD-U is a distinct entity or simply an early stage of either CD or UC.

**My assessment:** The clinical heterogeneity of CD is both its defining feature and its diagnostic challenge. The chapter's emphasis on obtaining a detailed chronological history is well-placed -- many CD diagnoses are missed because clinicians stop at "IBS" without asking whether symptoms predated the IBS label.

## Claim 2: Cross-sectional enterography (CTE/MRE) has replaced barium follow-through as first-line imaging for CD; MRE is preferred for perianal disease

**Author's claim:** "Cross-sectional imaging modalities (CT and magnetic resonance imaging) with enterography protocols have largely replaced small bowel follow-through for the routine evaluation of known and suspected CD. These modalities are first-line imaging techniques that can rapidly and accurately identify areas of mucosal inflammation, fibrostenotic and penetrating complications, and many EIMs of CD."

**Evidence presented:** CTE advantages: rapid image acquisition, lower cost, high accuracy for small bowel inflammation. MRE advantages: no ionizing radiation, superior soft tissue contrast, better for fistula and perianal disease characterization, validated scoring instrument (MRE global score) for serial quantitative assessment. Both require adequate small bowel distension with oral contrast and use IV contrast to identify mucosal hyperenhancement. Classic findings: "comb sign" (engorged vasa recta, highly specific for active inflammation), "target sign" (trilaminar enhancement: bright mucosa, dark submucosal edema, bright serosa). Small bowel ultrasound (SBUS) with IV contrast is an emerging alternative -- advantages of low cost, no radiation, speed -- but is operator-dependent and may be less sensitive than MRE for detecting extent and activity of small bowel CD. Capsule endoscopy is highly sensitive for mucosal lesions missed on cross-sectional imaging but limited by retention risk (~2-5% in CD), requiring patency assessment first. Balloon enteroscopy allows biopsies and therapeutic interventions deep in the small bowel but is reserved for targeted evaluation after capsule endoscopy.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The superiority of cross-sectional enterography over barium studies is well-established. The CTE vs MRE trade-off is the standard clinical teaching.

**What's at stake:** Choice of imaging modality has significant consequences: CTE exposes young patients (CD typically diagnosed in 20s-30s) to cumulative radiation with repeated studies over a lifetime. MRE avoids radiation but is more expensive and less accessible. For perianal CD, MRE is unequivocally superior -- Figure 64.14 demonstrates the dramatic difference in fistula visualization. The chapter's recommendation of MRE for perianal disease and CTE as an acceptable alternative for small bowel inflammation alone reflects the standard of care.

**Who disagrees:** Some centers advocate small bowel ultrasound as a first-line imaging modality (particularly in Europe), reserving MRE/CTE for cases where ultrasound is inconclusive or where therapeutic decisions require precise anatomic mapping. The chapter notes this debate: SBUS "may be less sensitive than MRE for detecting the presence, extent, and activity of small bowel CD."

**My assessment:** The imaging section is one of the best in this chapter. The descriptions of comb sign and target sign are clear. The MRE preference for perianal disease is unequivocally correct -- CT simply cannot resolve fistula anatomy relative to sphincter muscles the way MRE can. For clinical practice: MRE for perianal CD and serial monitoring in young patients; CTE acceptable for acute presentations where speed matters or MRE is unavailable.

## Claim 3: Ileocolonoscopy with biopsies is the primary diagnostic modality for CD; noncaseating granulomas are pathognomonic but present in only ~15% of patients

**Author's claim:** "Colonoscopy with ileal intubation is generally required for the assessment of CD and should include biopsies for histopathology assessment to exclude infection and lymphoma and corroborate the severity of inflammation... Noncaseating granulomata are considered pathognomonic for CD in this setting, although they are seen in only about 15% of patients with CD."

**Evidence presented:** Endoscopic findings: erythema, erosions, ulcerations (classified as aphthous, linear, stellate, or serpiginous), strictures. Endoscopic scoring: CDEIS (Crohn's Disease Endoscopic Index of Severity) and SES-CD (Simple Endoscopic Score for CD) are validated for clinical trials. Postoperative recurrence: Rutgeerts score (i0-i4) grades the ileocolonic anastomosis; i2 or higher predicts clinical recurrence and need for therapy. Histology: chronic inflammation markers include lymphoid aggregates, mixed acute/chronic infiltrates, crypt abscesses, neuronal hypertrophy, Paneth cell metaplasia. Granulomas can be found in both inflamed and uninflamed mucosa and should be distinguished from ruptured crypts. Transmural, focal/patchy inflammation is characteristic. "Creeping fat" (mesenteric fat wrapping around inflamed ileum) seen in surgical specimens. Upper endoscopy: advocated because up to 15% of adults with established CD have asymptomatic upper tract involvement (focal non-H. pylori gastritis, esophagitis, duodenitis).

**Confidence:** HIGH for endoscopic and histological features. MEDIUM for the 15% granuloma prevalence -- this varies by biopsy protocol (number of biopsies, whether serial sections are examined).

**What's at stake:** The low sensitivity of granulomas (15%) means their absence does not exclude CD. Conversely, their presence in a patient with colonic inflammation strongly favors CD over UC -- this can be the deciding factor in IBD-U cases. The distinction between a true granuloma and a ruptured crypt is a known pitfall in histopathology. The recommendation for upper endoscopy in suspected CD is not universally followed in practice but may identify subclinical upper tract involvement that confirms the diagnosis.

**Who disagrees:** Some pathologists argue that microgranulomas (not just well-formed granulomas) should be considered diagnostic. The 15% figure may underestimate true prevalence if serial sectioning and meticulous search protocols are used.

**My assessment:** The granuloma sensitivity problem illustrates a broader point about CD diagnosis: no single finding is pathognomonic in most patients. The diagnosis rests on a constellation of clinical, endoscopic, histological, and radiological findings, not on any one feature. The Rutgeerts score for postoperative recurrence is one of the most clinically useful endoscopic classification systems in IBD -- it directly guides therapeutic decisions.

## Claim 4: The Montreal Classification is the standard CD classification system, categorizing by age at diagnosis, disease location, and disease behavior

**Author's claim:** "The Montreal classification includes age at diagnosis, disease location, and the presence of perianal disease and is the most recognized CD classification in clinical research."

**Evidence presented:** Montreal Classification components: Age (A1 <16, A2 17-40, A3 >40), Location (L1 ileal, L2 colonic, L3 ileocolonic, L4 isolated upper disease [modifier added to L1-L3]), Behavior (B1 non-stricturing non-penetrating, B2 stricturing, B3 penetrating), with "p" modifier for perianal disease. Example: A2L1B2 = diagnosed age 17-40, ileal disease, stricturing behavior. The chapter acknowledges that "the Montreal classification does not capture the nuances of phenotypic differences between patients that may reflect differences in pathophysiology or inflammatory pathways." Disease behavior changes over time: at diagnosis, 56-81% have inflammatory phenotype (B1), ~5% stricturing (B2), ~25% penetrating (B3); over 20 years, many progress to B2/B3, especially with ileal involvement.

**Confidence:** HIGH. Montreal is the universally accepted classification in CD research.

**What's at stake:** Classification drives treatment decisions. B1 disease may be managed with conventional therapy; B2/B3 disease or perianal disease (p modifier) generally warrants earlier biologic therapy. The behavior modifier is dynamic -- a patient classified as B1 at diagnosis may develop a stricture years later and become B2, which is why ongoing reassessment is needed.

**Who disagrees:** There is growing recognition that Montreal is insufficient for precision medicine. Genetic, serological, and microbiome subtyping may eventually supplement or replace anatomic classification. The chapter notes: "it is widely accepted that the Montreal classification does not capture the nuances of phenotypic differences."

**My assessment:** Montreal is practical and widely used but is essentially descriptive, not mechanistic. The chapter's acknowledgment of its limitations is appropriate. The important clinical point is that behavior (B1 vs B2/B3) matters more for prognosis and treatment than location (L1 vs L2 vs L3).

## Claim 5: Serological markers have prognostic but NOT diagnostic utility in CD; they should not be used for screening or diagnosis

**Author's claim:** "Because of low sensitivity, screening for IBD in the general population is not appropriate... serological testing is insufficiently sensitive and specific to accurately diagnose IBD independent of clinical, endoscopic, and histological criteria."

**Evidence presented:** IBD-associated antibodies (ASCA IgA/IgG, anti-OmpC, anti-CBir1, anti-I2) have >90% specificity but low sensitivity for distinguishing IBD from non-IBD. Diagnostic accuracy improves when markers are combined. Small bowel CD is associated with higher ASCA, CBir1, OmpC, and I2 levels; colonic CD and UC are associated with higher ANCA levels. Prognostic value: complicated CD (stricturing/penetrating) is associated with both increased number and higher titers of antibodies. These markers can predict future complicated disease when drawn at diagnosis. Postoperative recurrence in pediatric CD is associated with anti-CBir1. High ANCA levels are associated with non-response to anti-TNF therapy. However: "There has been little work in determining the role of serologies in ethnically diverse populations, but the limited data suggest that differences exist."

**Confidence:** HIGH for prognostic associations. HIGH for the recommendation against diagnostic use.

**What's at stake:** Commercial serological panels (Prometheus IBD sgi Diagnostic, etc.) are marketed as diagnostic tools, but the evidence does not support this use. The chapter is unambiguous: serology is adjunctive, not diagnostic. The prognostic utility is more promising -- a patient with high-titer multiple antimicrobial antibodies at diagnosis may warrant more aggressive initial therapy.

**Who disagrees:** Some clinicians use serological panels in IBD-U to guide classification (ASCA+/pANCA- favors CD; pANCA+/ASCA- favors UC). The chapter acknowledges this limited role but does not endorse it as standard practice.

**My assessment:** This is a consistent message across chapters 62, 63, and 64: IBD serology is for prognosis, not diagnosis. The prognostic value in CD is better established than in UC. The lack of data in non-European populations is a significant gap -- most serological studies were conducted in Caucasian cohorts, and the limited data suggest different performance characteristics in African American and Asian populations.

## Claim 6: Fecal calprotectin is useful for noninvasive monitoring but shows equal efficacy for colonic and small bowel CD

**Author's claim:** "Fecal calprotectin has been shown to be equally effective for the detection of inflammation in both colonic and small bowel CD... Changes in stool calprotectin have been positively associated with variability in the disease course, and calprotectin has been shown to increase to more than normal values several months before clinical signs of a disease flare are apparent both in ulcerative colitis and in CD."

**Evidence presented:** Calprotectin correlates with serum CRP, endoscopic activity, and histological inflammation. It predicts postoperative recurrence after ileocecal resection. It rises months before clinical flare, suggesting a role in proactive monitoring. However, it is "relatively nonspecific and can be elevated in a variety of conditions in addition to IBD, including infection and malignancy." The chapter notes that some patients may not mount a CRP response despite active inflammation, making calprotectin particularly useful in this subgroup.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The calprotectin data for CD is robust, though the sensitivity is somewhat lower than for UC (the Ch 63 meta-analysis showed calprotectin is more sensitive in UC than CD).

**What's at stake:** The finding that calprotectin rises months before clinical symptoms is clinically significant -- it supports proactive monitoring with preemptive treatment adjustment rather than waiting for symptoms, which would align with the "treat to target" paradigm of targeting mucosal healing. However, the nonspecificity means an elevated calprotectin should trigger endoscopic confirmation, not blind treatment escalation.

**Who disagrees:** The optimal calprotectin threshold for CD is debated. The chapter does not specify thresholds as explicitly as Ch 63 did for UC (50/250), reflecting the greater heterogeneity in CD.

**My assessment:** Calprotectin monitoring is especially valuable in CD because clinical symptoms correlate poorly with endoscopic activity -- a patient can have active small bowel inflammation with normal stool frequency and no rectal bleeding. The "months before symptoms" lead time is the strongest argument for serial calprotectin monitoring in high-risk patients.

## Claim 7: IBD-U represents 5-15% of colonic IBD cases where CD vs UC cannot be distinguished; misdiagnosis risk is highest when inflammatory changes are partial or when disease-modifying therapy has been initiated

**Author's claim:** "In 5-15% of patients with isolated colonic involvement, it may not be possible initially to distinguish between ulcerative colitis or CD; these patients are given a diagnosis of IBD-unclassified (IBDU). Before making an IBDU determination, evaluation of these patients should include careful clinical, radiographic, colonoscopic, and histological assessment for features more typical for CD."

**Evidence presented:** Features favoring CD in indeterminate colitis: family history of CD, nonbloody diarrhea and weight loss at presentation, patchy inflammation or rectal sparing on endoscopy/histology, serological markers (ASCA+/pANCA-). Features favoring UC: continuous inflammation from rectum, bloody diarrhea, pANCA+/ASCA-. The chapter emphasizes that "it is vital to wash off these exudates and examine the underlying mucosa when characterizing endoscopic disease activity" (Ch 63) because exudates can be mistaken for ulceration leading to overestimation of severity or misclassification.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The 5-15% figure is derived from cohort studies but varies by how rigorously CD features are sought.

**What's at stake:** IBD-U has enormous therapeutic implications. If the patient actually has CD and undergoes IPAA (ileal pouch-anal anastomosis, the standard surgery for UC), the pouch is at high risk for CD recurrence, fistula formation, and pouch failure. Conversely, if the patient has UC and is denied curative surgery because of IBD-U classification, they may endure years of unnecessary immunosuppression.

**Who disagrees:** Some argue IBD-U is overdiagnosed and that rigorous evaluation (including small bowel imaging, upper endoscopy, serology, and repeat colonoscopy after treatment) can correctly classify most cases. Others argue it's underdiagnosed because clinicians prematurely commit to UC or CD based on insufficient evidence.

**My assessment:** IBD-U is a diagnosis of humility -- it acknowledges diagnostic uncertainty rather than forcing a premature classification. The chapter's approach of using all available data (clinical, endoscopic, histological, radiological, serological) before accepting IBD-U is appropriate. In practice, many IBD-U cases eventually declare themselves as CD (if small bowel disease develops or fistulas appear) or UC (if colectomy specimens show only mucosal disease).

**Strongest section:** The imaging section (Claim 2). The description of CTE vs MRE trade-offs, the explanation of comb sign and target sign, and the superiority of MRE for perianal disease are clear, practical, and evidence-based. The Figure 64.14 comparison of CT vs MR for perianal fistula is particularly instructive.

**Weakest section:** The differential diagnosis (surrounding text for Claim 7). While the list of entities is comprehensive, the chapter lacks the systematic differential diagnosis framework that Ch 63 provided for UC. The infectious mimics (TB, MAI, amebiasis) are mentioned but the specific distinguishing features are not presented in a structured format.

**Cross-reference opportunities:** The CD-specific endoscopic features should be read alongside Ch 63 Table 63.1 (UC vs CD endoscopic specificity index). The serological discussion connects to Ch 62 (pathogenesis) which covers ASCA, pANCA, anti-OmpC, and anti-CBir1 in more mechanistic detail. The fecal calprotectin data should be cross-referenced with Ch 63 which provides the meta-analytic sensitivity/specificity data. The Montreal Classification should be cross-referenced with the clinical guidelines (ACG, ECCO) which use Montreal criteria for trial eligibility and treatment algorithms.
