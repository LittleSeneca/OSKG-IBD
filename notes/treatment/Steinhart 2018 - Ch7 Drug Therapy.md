# Steinhart -- Ch7: Drug Therapy for Managing IBD

Steinhart, Medical Director of the Mount Sinai Hospital (Toronto) IBD Centre, presents a comprehensive patient-facing overview of every drug class used in IBD. This is the bridge between the clinical canon and the lived experience: pharmacology is explained accessibly, side effects are given honest treatment, and the treatment philosophy (step-up vs top-down, treat-to-target, mucosal healing) is laid out clearly. The chapter covers 5-ASA preparations, glucocorticoids, antibiotics, immunosuppressants (thiopurines, methotrexate, cyclosporine), JAK inhibitors, anti-TNF biologics, anti-integrin (vedolizumab), anti-IL-12/23 (ustekinumab), biosimilars, and emerging microbiome-directed therapies.

## Claim 1: Mucosal healing -- not just symptom control -- should be the goal of IBD therapy

**Author's claim:** "There has been a move toward making elimination of active inflammation and healing of ulcers -- called mucosal healing -- a goal of therapy in IBD." Steinhart gives three reasons: (1) residual inflammation in asymptomatic patients increases flare risk, (2) in Crohn's, persistent inflammation leads to irreversible bowel damage (strictures, abscesses, fistulas) even without symptoms, and (3) in UC, long-term inflammation increases colon cancer risk.

**Evidence presented:** Clinical observation and the natural history of Crohn's complications. Steinhart acknowledges that mucosal healing "is a higher hurdle to achieve than remission or elimination of symptoms" and that complete healing "may be an unrealistic goal" in long-standing Crohn's. He notes that colonoscopy or imaging -- not just blood/stool tests -- is required to confirm mucosal healing, adding patient burden.

**Confidence:** HIGH. Mucosal healing as a treatment target is now the standard in IBD care, validated by the CALM trial (tight control vs symptom-based management) and endorsed by STRIDE-II guidelines. Steinhart's framing in 2018 captures the transition from symptom-based to objective-target-based care.

**What's at stake:** If mucosal healing is the real goal, then medications must be assessed not just by symptom relief but by endoscopic remission. This disqualifies some therapies (e.g., steroids do not produce sustained mucosal healing) and elevates others (biologics, thiopurines). It also means regular objective monitoring -- calprotectin, colonoscopy, imaging -- is medically necessary, not optional.

**Who disagrees:** No major disagreement on the concept. The debate is practical: how much healing is enough, whether complete healing is achievable in all patients, and whether the cost/inconvenience of repeated scopes is justified for all patients.

**Alternative reading:** Some clinicians argue that symptom-based management with escalation on flare is sufficient for mild disease and that mucosal healing as a universal goal overtreats patients who would do well with less aggressive approaches.

**My assessment:** Steinhart presents this as an emerging consensus, which it was in 2018. The CALM trial (2017) had just demonstrated that tight control with objective targets produces better outcomes than symptom-driven care. This claim has aged well -- it has only been reinforced since 2018.

## Claim 2: Top-down therapy with biologics early in Crohn's disease can prevent irreversible bowel damage

**Author's claim:** "There has been a move toward using the more potent and more consistently effective therapies that are more likely to result in ulcer healing earlier on in the course of the disease, before complications have occurred." Steinhart contrasts this with traditional step-up therapy (start with safest/weakest drugs, escalate only on failure) and describes an intermediate approach -- "accelerated step care" -- with defined timelines and goals for assessing response.

**Evidence presented:** The observation that Crohn's complications (strictures, fistulas, abscesses) are often irreversible once they occur, and that surgery becomes necessary. The argument is preventive: treat aggressively before damage accumulates. Steinhart notes that predicting which patients will have aggressive disease is difficult -- "certain features of the disease and certain blood tests that might help with the determination, they are not sufficiently accurate to be reliable."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the principle in moderate-to-severe Crohn's. The SONIC trial demonstrated superiority of combination therapy (infliximab + azathioprine) over either alone in recent-onset Crohn's. However, top-down for ALL Crohn's patients would overtreat the subset with indolent disease. The risk-stratification problem Steinhart identifies (who needs top-down?) remains unsolved.

**What's at stake:** If top-down is correct, delaying biologics in moderate-to-severe Crohn's allows preventable bowel damage. If step-up is correct, starting with biologics exposes patients unnecessarily to infection risk and cost. This is the central tension in Crohn's management.

**Who disagrees:** Cost-conscious health systems and insurers favor step-up. Some gastroenterologists argue that the long-term safety data for biologics is still accumulating and that rushing to them in all patients is premature.

**My assessment:** Steinhart's framing is appropriately nuanced. He does not advocate universal top-down but explains why the pendulum is swinging that direction for patients with aggressive or extensive disease. The "accelerated step care" compromise -- essentially treat-to-target with time-bound escalation -- has become the dominant approach in guidelines since 2018.

## Claim 3: 5-ASA preparations are effective for UC but disappointing in Crohn's disease

**Author's claim:** "The controlled-release 5-ASA medications are useful in milder flares of inflammatory bowel disease, particularly ulcerative colitis... When the 5-ASA drugs were first developed, it was hoped that they would provide a safe and effective alternative to steroid medications in patients with Crohn's disease. Unfortunately, they have not been found to be as effective in Crohn's disease as had been hoped for."

**Evidence presented:** Clinical experience and trial data. Steinhart notes that 5-ASA drugs may benefit Crohn's patients with colonic involvement or mild disease, but "only a small minority of patients will respond." He also notes their potential role in post-surgical recurrence prevention in Crohn's, where "they are particularly appealing... because the 5-ASA drugs have a well-proven, long-term safety record."

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is well-established in the literature. 5-ASA (mesalamine) is first-line for mild-moderate UC induction and maintenance. For Crohn's, the evidence is weak -- most guidelines no longer recommend 5-ASA for Crohn's, and the post-surgical recurrence benefit is modest at best.

**What's at stake:** Using 5-ASA in Crohn's when ineffective delays effective therapy. However, the excellent safety profile means the risk of trying it is low.

**My assessment:** Steinhart is measured. He acknowledges the controversy ("there is some controversy about their effectiveness in Crohn's disease") and the practice pattern ("many physicians still elect to use 5-ASA drugs early in the course of the disease because of their excellent safety record"). His clinical honesty about the drug's limitations is valuable for patients making treatment decisions.

## Claim 4: Corticosteroids are rapidly effective but carry unacceptable long-term risk; budesonide offers a safer alternative for mild-moderate disease

**Author's claim:** "Steroids have been used for many decades in the treatment of inflammatory bowel disease. For most of this time, they have been the most consistently effective class of medications... they generally work very quickly." But they have "two main drawbacks -- their lack of benefit when used to prevent recurrence of disease flares... and their potential for producing short-term and long-term side effects."

**Evidence presented:** Steinhart provides an unusually detailed side effect catalogue, distinguishing common/reversible effects (moon face, acne, weight gain, insomnia, mood swings) from uncommon/possibly irreversible effects (osteoporosis, cataracts, diabetes, hypertension, avascular necrosis). He estimates that "approximately half of patients who take steroids experience at least one side effect." He presents budesonide (Entocort for ileal/right colon Crohn's; Uceris/Cortiment for UC) as a safer alternative with first-pass hepatic metabolism that minimizes systemic exposure.

**Confidence:** HIGH for prednisone's efficacy and side effect profile. MEDIUM-HIGH for budesonide's safety advantage. The budesonide claims are well-supported by clinical trials showing similar efficacy to prednisone for mild-moderate ileocecal Crohn's with fewer side effects, though budesonide is less effective than prednisone for severe disease.

**What's at stake:** Steroid dependence and steroid side effects remain major clinical problems in IBD. Approximately 20-30% of patients become steroid-dependent or steroid-refractory. The availability of budesonide and the push toward early biologic use are both responses to steroid toxicity.

**Who disagrees:** No one disputes steroid efficacy or toxicity. The debate is about how quickly to transition patients off steroids and onto steroid-sparing agents (thiopurines, biologics).

**My assessment:** Steinhart's treatment of steroids is thorough and honest. The side effect list does not sugarcoat. The distinction between common/reversible and uncommon/irreversible effects is clinically useful. The budesonide section correctly positions it as a bridge therapy for mild-moderate disease, not a replacement for prednisone in severe flares.

## Claim 5: Thiopurines (azathioprine/6-MP) are effective steroid-sparing agents for maintenance but carry rare but serious risks

**Author's claim:** "Azathioprine and 6-mercaptopurine... are useful for keeping patients in remission and reducing the need, over time, for other medications, such as steroids." But they are slow-acting (3 months to full effect), useless for acute flares, and require ongoing blood monitoring. Steinhart notes the combination of azathioprine with infliximab is "more effective than either drug given alone" in recent-onset Crohn's (SONIC trial).

**Evidence presented:** Decades of clinical use since the 1960s-70s. The SONIC trial (combination therapy superiority). Side effect data: pancreatitis in ~3%, allergic reactions, bone marrow suppression, liver enzyme abnormalities. Cancer risk: "the risk of lymphoma... is extremely small. It has been calculated that azathioprine would need to be taken for more than 4,300 years for someone between the ages of 20 and 29 before observing one extra case of lymphoma."

**Confidence:** HIGH for efficacy in maintenance. HIGH for side effect profile. The lymphoma risk quantification (4,300 patient-years for one extra case) puts the absolute risk in perspective but should not minimize the rare hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma risk in young males on combination therapy, which Steinhart addresses separately.

**What's at stake:** Thiopurines remain the most commonly used immunomodulator worldwide due to cost and oral administration. The risk/benefit calculus changes with the availability of biosimilars (cheaper biologics may displace thiopurines) and with the recognition of lymphoma risk in young males on combination therapy.

**Who disagrees:** Some pediatric gastroenterologists avoid thiopurines entirely in young males due to HSTCL risk, preferring methotrexate or early biologic monotherapy.

**My assessment:** Steinhart presents an even-handed assessment. He does not minimize the risks but places them in the context of absolute magnitude. His recommendation for TPMT testing before starting, regular blood monitoring, and minimum 4-year treatment duration reflects standard practice.

## Claim 6: Anti-TNF biologics (infliximab, adalimumab) are the most effective drug class for moderate-severe IBD, capable of achieving mucosal healing

**Author's claim:** Infliximab produces "60% to 80% of patients responded positively" in active Crohn's. "Repeated dosing of infliximab can produce complete healing of the inner lining of the intestine, something that is not consistently observed with any of the previous medical therapies." It is effective for fistulizing Crohn's ("the first treatment shown to cause fistulas to stop draining") and for treatment-resistant UC. Adalimumab, a fully human anti-TNF, offers the convenience of subcutaneous self-injection.

**Evidence presented:** Clinical trial data. Steinhart references the ACT trials for UC, the fistula trials for Crohn's, and general clinical experience. He notes the infusion reaction rate (5-10%), the tuberculosis reactivation risk (now mitigated by pre-treatment screening), and the antibody formation problem (which reduces drug levels and can cause loss of response).

**Confidence:** HIGH. Anti-TNF agents are the best-studied biologic class in IBD with 25+ years of data. The efficacy claims are supported by multiple RCTs. The safety concerns (infection, lymphoma) are real but the absolute risk is low.

**What's at stake:** Anti-TNF agents remain first-line biologics in most guidelines. The development of biosimilar infliximab has dramatically reduced cost, making these drugs accessible to more patients. Antibody formation and loss of response remain the major clinical challenge -- therapeutic drug monitoring (drug levels + antibody testing) has become standard to manage this.

**Who disagrees:** The debate is not about efficacy but about positioning: should anti-TNF be first biologic (current practice) or should vedolizumab (better safety) or ustekinumab be used first in appropriate patients?

**My assessment:** Steinhart presents infliximab as transformative, which it was. The case study of Naveen (19-year-old with extensive Crohn's, recommended for early biologic) illustrates the top-down logic effectively. The antibody/loss-of-response problem is well-explained, and the practical distinction between infliximab (IV infusion, every 6-8 weeks) and adalimumab (subcutaneous, every 1-2 weeks) matters for patient preference.

## Claim 7: Vedolizumab is gut-selective with an excellent safety profile; ustekinumab targets IL-12/23

**Author's claim:** Vedolizumab "is 'gut specific'... it does not appear to suppress the entire body's immune system, only the immune system of the gut... serious infections occurring only very rarely." For ustekinumab: "It is an antibody that attaches to and blocks the action of two proteins, called interleukin-12 and interleukin-23... inflammation caused by Crohn's disease is reduced."

**Evidence presented:** Vedolizumab's mechanism (alpha4beta7 integrin blockade preventing lymphocyte gut homing) is described accessibly. Steinhart notes the unknowns: whether vedolizumab treats extra-intestinal manifestations (arthritis, skin, liver) is not established. Ustekinumab had only been tested in Crohn's at the time of writing (2018); its UC approval came later (2019).

**Confidence:** HIGH for vedolizumab's safety advantage and moderate efficacy. HIGH for ustekinumab's efficacy in Crohn's. Both drugs now have extensive post-marketing data confirming their profiles.

**What's at stake:** These drugs expanded the biologic options beyond anti-TNF. For patients who fail or cannot tolerate anti-TNF, vedolizumab and ustekinumab provide mechanistically distinct alternatives. The safety profile of vedolizumab makes it attractive for elderly patients or those with infection risk.

**My assessment:** Steinhart correctly identifies the gut-selectivity of vedolizumab as its defining advantage. His caution about the unknowns (extra-intestinal manifestations, fistula efficacy) is appropriate for a 2018 book. The ustekinumab section is briefer, reflecting the more recent approval at the time of writing. Since 2018, both drugs have established themselves firmly in treatment algorithms.

## Claim 8: JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib) represent the first effective oral small-molecule option for biologic-refractory UC

**Author's claim:** Tofacitinib "has been shown to be effective in treating ulcerative colitis. It has not been properly tested in Crohn's disease." As a small molecule, it is orally administered, does not provoke antibody formation, and has a different side effect profile from biologics -- notably an increased risk of shingles.

**Evidence presented:** Clinical trial data for UC. Steinhart notes that other JAK inhibitors (filgotinib, upadacitinib) were in development for Crohn's at the time of writing. The shingles risk is highlighted: "One infection that has been seen more often than expected in people taking tofacitinib is shingles."

**Confidence:** HIGH for UC efficacy. The shingles signal is real and was confirmed in post-marketing surveillance. Since 2018, upadacitinib has received approval for both UC and Crohn's, validating the JAK inhibitor class in IBD.

**What's at stake:** JAK inhibitors are the first oral advanced therapy for IBD. For patients who prefer pills over injections/infusions, they represent a significant quality-of-life advance. The safety concerns (shingles, potential cardiovascular/thrombotic risk at higher doses seen in rheumatoid arthritis) require ongoing monitoring.

**My assessment:** Steinhart's coverage of JAK inhibitors is appropriately cautious given the 2018 publication date -- tofacitinib had only recently been approved for UC. The class has expanded significantly since then, and the oral administration advantage Steinhart identifies has proven to be a major factor in patient preference.

## Claim 9: Biosimilars are clinically equivalent to originator biologics and essential for cost control

**Author's claim:** Biosimilar infliximab (Inflectra/Remsima) has proven "not different from Remicade in any important way when used in Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis." Biosimilars "have the potential to help limit the rapidly increasing cost of drug treatment for IBD, as they are generally substantially less costly than the originator biologic drugs."

**Evidence presented:** The regulatory pathway: biosimilar must prove chemical similarity and clinical equivalence in at least one indication, after which it receives approval for all indications of the originator. Steinhart acknowledges patient anxiety about switching: "They may ask, 'Why should I switch my medication when I am doing so well? Just so my insurance plan can save some money?'"

**Confidence:** HIGH. The clinical equivalence of biosimilar infliximab to originator Remicade has been demonstrated in multiple studies and confirmed by extensive real-world experience since 2018.

**What's at stake:** Biosimilars have reduced the cost of biologic therapy by 30-50% or more in many markets. This has expanded access dramatically and made early biologic use economically feasible in public health systems. The non-medical switching debate (switching stable patients from originator to biosimilar) was active in 2018 and has largely been resolved in favor of biosimilar use.

**My assessment:** Steinhart's balanced treatment acknowledges both the cost imperative and the patient's legitimate preference for stability. The biosimilar section has aged well -- the predicted cost reductions and safety profile have materialized.

## Claim 10: Probiotics and FMT are unproven for IBD despite mechanistic plausibility and patient interest

**Author's claim:** "Aside from the E. coli Nissle probiotic, there is no evidence that any probiotic, including VSL#3, is effective in improving outcomes in ulcerative colitis or Crohn's disease." For FMT: "it is far from proven as an effective and safe therapy for IBD."

**Evidence presented:** E. coli Nissle for UC maintenance (roughly equivalent to 5-ASA). VSL#3 for pouchitis prevention (some positive studies). FMT studies have "huge variation in how FMT is carried out" making results impossible to interpret. Steinhart notes donor selection, processing, delivery route, and timing all vary dramatically between studies.

**Confidence:** HIGH for the claim that probiotics/FMT are unproven for luminal IBD. The evidence has not substantially changed since 2018 -- FMT remains investigational for UC (some positive signals in selected trials) and unproven for Crohn's. E. coli Nissle remains the best-supported probiotic.

**What's at stake:** Patient interest in microbiome-directed therapies is high and will likely increase. The gap between patient expectation and evidence is wide. Unregulated probiotic products make claims that exceed the data.

**My assessment:** Steinhart's skepticism is appropriate and evidence-based. His willingness to say "this appeals to many IBD sufferers and merits further study" while clearly stating the evidence limitations is the right tone for a patient-facing book.

## Ch7 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Mucosal healing as treatment goal | HIGH | Already validated; risk is overtreatment of mild disease |
| 2: Top-down therapy for Crohn's | MEDIUM-HIGH | Long-term biologic safety data still maturing; risk stratification imprecise |
| 3: 5-ASA ineffective in Crohn's | HIGH | Some patients with colonic Crohn's may benefit; post-surgical recurrence role is weak |
| 4: Steroids effective but toxic | HIGH | Budesonide positioning may shift as more biologics become first-line |
| 5: Thiopurines for maintenance | HIGH | May be displaced by biosimilars; HSTCL risk in young males |
| 6: Anti-TNF as most effective class | HIGH | Newer biologics (ustekinumab, risankizumab) challenging first-line positioning |
| 7: Vedolizumab gut-selective safety | HIGH | Efficacy in Crohn's is modest compared to anti-TNF |
| 8: JAK inhibitors oral advantage | HIGH | Safety concerns (shingles, cardiovascular) may limit use |
| 9: Biosimilars clinically equivalent | HIGH | No-claim switching anxiety is psychological, not pharmacological |
| 10: Probiotics/FMT unproven | HIGH | FMT for UC remains investigational; donor selection may be key |

**Strongest section:** The steroid section is the most detailed and clinically useful for patients, distinguishing common/reversible from uncommon/irreversible side effects with unusual honesty. The biologic sections are practical, addressing administration route, antibody formation, and safety in accessible terms.

**Weakest section:** The CAM section is relatively superficial for a patient-facing book. Given that "up to half of IBD sufferers have tried CAM," a more detailed evidence table for specific CAM therapies (aloe, curcumin, Boswellia, cannabis) would have been valuable. The cannabis section correctly notes that marijuana helps symptoms but not inflammation, but doesn't address CBD or the cannabinoid-derivative trials in sufficient detail.

**Overall quality:** This is an excellent patient-facing drug therapy overview. Steinhart writes with clinical authority and genuine respect for the patient's decision-making role. The case studies (Jonathan, Naveen, Rebecca) ground the pharmacology in real clinical decisions. The treatment philosophy sections (mucosal healing, step-up vs top-down, personalized medicine) give patients the conceptual framework to understand why specific drugs are recommended, not just what they do.
