# Yamada 7E -- Ch65: Surgical Treatment of Inflammatory Bowel Disease

The surgical textbook chapter from Yamada 7E, authored by colorectal surgeons Lightner and Holubar at the Cleveland Clinic. This chapter provides the surgeon's perspective on operative indications, techniques, and outcomes for both Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. It complements the patient-facing Steinhart Ch 8 with technical detail, morbidity data, and procedure-specific outcome rates.

## Claim 1: Up to 80% of CD patients still require surgery despite biologic therapy; early laparoscopic resection may be a valid alternative to biologic escalation

**Author's claim:** "Although biologics have largely replaced corticosteroids as the cornerstone of medical therapy, the natural history of the disease has not been significantly altered, with up to 80% of patients still requiring surgical intervention at some point in their disease course because of primary and secondary loss of response to biologic medication." Furthermore, "another more recent valid option is early laparoscopic resection as a reasonable option instead of initiating biologic therapy in the setting of refractory, relatively limited disease (e.g., short-length <40 cm, inflammatory, nonstricturing, ileocolic disease); a randomized controlled trial demonstrated the benefit of this approach with regard to patient-reported outcomes."

**Evidence presented:** Cited references [1,2] for the 80% surgical rate. Reference [19] for the RCT on early laparoscopic resection vs biologic escalation. The authors note that 90% of CD patients have endoscopic recurrence within 1 year of surgery and up to 35% require repeat resection by 10 years.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the 80% surgical rate (these are pre-biologic and early biologic era estimates; modern rates are lower). HIGH for the early laparoscopic resection RCT evidence. HIGH for the recurrence rates.

**What's at stake:** The claim that biologics haven't "significantly altered" the natural history of CD with respect to surgery is provocative but supported by population-level data showing surgical rates have declined but not collapsed. The early laparoscopic resection option challenges the default of escalating biologics in limited ileocecal disease.

**My assessment:** This is a surgical perspective that usefully complicates the medical narrative. The RCT supporting early laparoscopic resection for limited ileocecal CD is an important counterpoint to the "biologics first" approach. The surgical recurrence data (90% endoscopic at 1 year) underscores that surgery is not curative and postoperative prophylaxis is essential.

## Claim 2: Strictureplasty preserves bowel length in multifocal small bowel CD with acceptable morbidity

**Author's claim:** "Given CD is not cured by surgery, and short bowel syndrome can occur in up to 13% of cases, strictureplasty has been used" to avoid resection in patients with multiple short strictures. Postoperative morbidity is reported in "up to 18% of patients" (surgical-site infection, obstruction, hemorrhage, sepsis, early reoperation). Five-year recurrence after strictureplasty is 25-30%, with the majority of recurrences occurring "in intestinal segments remote from original strictureplasty sites."

**Evidence presented:** Multiple surgical series. Risk factors for complications: low preoperative albumin, weight loss, advanced age, preoperative steroid use, abscess/fistula at time of strictureplasty. Reoperation rates: 29% at 5 years, 34% at 7.5 years, 73% at 10 years.

**Confidence:** HIGH. Strictureplasty is a well-established bowel-sparing technique with extensive surgical literature. The morbidity and recurrence data come from multiple series with consistent findings.

**What's at stake:** Strictureplasty is the primary surgical strategy for preventing short bowel syndrome in patients with multifocal small bowel CD. The high long-term reoperation rates (73% at 10 years) place realistic expectations on the procedure's durability.

**My assessment:** This section provides the surgical outcome data that Steinhart's patient-facing chapter lacks. The complication risk factors (albumin, weight, steroids, abscess) give concrete guidance for preoperative optimization. The finding that recurrences occur "remote from original strictureplasty sites" supports the concept that CD recurrence represents new disease, not failure of the surgical technique.

## Claim 3: The IPAA procedure transforms UC from a disease cured by proctocolectomy to one managed with a functional pouch, with specific complications

**Author's claim:** The UC section (lines 84908+) covers total proctocolectomy with end ileostomy as the historical standard and IPAA as the current restorative procedure of choice. The chapter covers technical aspects: pouch construction, temporary diverting ileostomy, and staged procedures for sick/malnourished patients.

**Evidence presented:** Surgical technique descriptions and outcome data. The chapter acknowledges indeterminate colitis as a diagnostic challenge: patients with indeterminate colitis undergoing IPAA have "only a slightly higher risk of pouch failure" compared to UC patients, but known CD patients are "usually not candidates for pelvic pouch reconstruction surgery, except in special circumstances." CD of the pouch after IPAA has "a poor prognosis, with up to 50% of affected patients requiring ileal diversion, with or without pouch resection."

**Confidence:** HIGH for the technical descriptions and complication rates. HIGH for the CD-in-pouch prognosis.

**What's at stake:** The distinction between UC and CD is surgically critical. Misclassifying Crohn's colitis as UC and performing IPAA can lead to pouch failure in up to 50% of cases. The indeterminate colitis category represents a gray zone where shared decision-making with informed consent about higher failure risk is essential.

**My assessment:** This chapter provides the surgeon's technical perspective that complements the patient-facing Steinhart Ch 8. The IPAA section is notably thorough on the staged approach (3-stage for sick patients: colectomy, then pouch creation with ileostomy, then ileostomy closure). The CD-in-pouch prognosis data (50% require diversion) quantifies the risk that Steinhart describes qualitatively.

## Ch65 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: 80% CD surgical rate; early lap resection option | MEDIUM-HIGH | Modern biologic-era surgical rates may be lower |
| 2: Strictureplasty with 10-year reoperation 73% | HIGH | Patient selection strongly influences outcomes |
| 3: IPAA for UC; CD-in-pouch 50% diversion | HIGH | Indeterminate colitis classification continues to evolve |

**Strongest section:** The operative morbidity data for specific procedures (strictureplasty, ileocecectomy, IPAA) provides quantitative risk estimates useful for surgical consent. The risk factor identification (albumin, weight loss, steroids, abscess) for strictureplasty complications has direct preoperative optimization implications.

**Weakest section:** The chapter has limited discussion of the combined medical-surgical approach to perianal CD, which is the standard of care. The medical management of postoperative recurrence is referenced only briefly and largely deferred to Chapter 64.

**Overall quality:** A technically thorough surgical reference that complements the medical chapters. The authors' willingness to present early laparoscopic resection as an alternative to biologic escalation for limited ileocecal CD is a useful surgical perspective that challenges medical default pathways.
