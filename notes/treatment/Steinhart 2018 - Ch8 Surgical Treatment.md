# Steinhart -- Ch8: Surgical Treatment of IBD

Steinhart presents surgery not as a failure of medical management but as a legitimate treatment option with distinct indications in Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. The chapter covers the full surgical armamentarium: colectomy with ileostomy or pelvic pouch for UC, segmental resections and strictureplasty for Crohn's, perianal procedures, laparoscopic approaches, and post-operative recurrence prevention. The patient perspective is central -- living with a stoma, bowel function after pouch surgery, fertility concerns, and the psychological adjustment to surgical alteration are addressed frankly. The case studies (Kelly's UC colectomy decision, Lauren's fibrostenotic Crohn's resection) ground surgical principles in real clinical reasoning.

## Claim 1: Surgery for UC is curative (colectomy removes the disease); surgery for Crohn's is damage control (disease can recur)

**Author's claim:** "Patients with ulcerative colitis have a major advantage over patients with Crohn's disease because their disease involves only the large intestine (colon): when the large intestine is surgically removed, the disease is effectively 'cured.'" For Crohn's: "Crohn's disease can recur in previously unaffected segments of intestine following surgical resection of a diseased area. This may lead to the need for multiple operations."

**Evidence presented:** Decades of surgical experience. Recurrence data: "Approximately 1 out of every 3 patients undergoing surgery for Crohn's disease will have a recurrence of symptoms within 3 to 5 years if they are on no treatment, and a portion of those will end up requiring another operation." The clinical consequence: "the real challenge in the field of surgery for Crohn's disease is not so much how to perform an operation, but how to prevent the disease from coming back."

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is the fundamental surgical distinction between the two diseases and remains unchanged. The UC-surgery-as-cure paradigm is well-established (though pouchitis complicates the picture for IPAA patients). Crohn's post-surgical recurrence is a central clinical challenge with extensive literature.

**What's at stake:** This distinction determines surgical strategy entirely. In UC, the goal is complete removal of the colon and rectum. In Crohn's, the goal is bowel conservation -- removing only what is necessary, using strictureplasty to preserve bowel length, and accepting that re-operation is likely.

**My assessment:** Steinhart's framing is correct and clinically useful. He does not shy away from the recurrence problem in Crohn's but also does not use it to dissuade patients from surgery when it is indicated. The honesty about recurrence rates empowers patients to make realistic decisions.

## Claim 2: Approximately 70-80% of Crohn's patients and 20-40% of UC patients will require surgery

**Author's claim:** "Approximately 70% to 80% of people suffering from Crohn's disease and approximately 20% to 40% of people suffering from ulcerative colitis will require surgery at some point following their diagnosis. These estimates are based on information from previous decades and do not necessarily reflect the effect of recent advances in drug therapy. There is some indication that surgical rates are coming down."

**Evidence presented:** Historical surgical epidemiology. Steinhart acknowledges the limitation: these are pre-biologic era estimates, and rates are declining with earlier and more effective medical therapy.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM for current applicability. Modern cohort studies show surgical rates for Crohn's have decreased to approximately 40-50% at 10 years with biologic use. For UC, colectomy rates have dropped to approximately 10-15% at 10 years. The direction Steinhart suggests (declining rates) has been confirmed.

**What's at stake:** If surgical rates are declining, the calculus for delaying surgery shifts -- biologics may truly prevent or postpone the need. But for the substantial minority who still need surgery, delay can lead to worse outcomes (poorer nutrition, more emergencies, more complications).

**My assessment:** Steinhart correctly flags that these are historical figures and likely overestimates for the modern era. This is important context for patients weighing surgery against newer medical options.

## Claim 3: The pelvic pouch (IPAA) is the standard restorative procedure for UC, with >90% patient satisfaction, but carries specific complications

**Author's claim:** "The pelvic pouch procedure is offered to most patients who need surgery for ulcerative colitis. More than 90% end up being very satisfied with the result." The procedure creates a reservoir from the terminal ileum, attached to the anal canal, preserving continence without a permanent stoma. However, patients typically have 4-12 bowel movements per day (average 6-8), and complications include bowel obstruction, anastomotic leak, pouchitis (10-15%), and reduced fertility in women (potentially "as high as 1 out of every 3").

**Evidence presented:** Surgical case series and clinical experience. The pouchitis rate of 10-15% is cited, with the observation that "some centers that have a large experience with the pelvic pouch procedure report higher rates." Pouchitis is treated with antibiotics (ciprofloxacin) and responds differently from UC. Fertility impact is attributed to scarring around the fallopian tubes.

**Confidence:** HIGH for the general satisfaction rate and complication profile. The fertility impact is real and should be discussed with women of childbearing age considering IPAA -- this is a critical counseling point. The pouchitis rate varies by center and follow-up duration; long-term rates may be higher than 10-15%.

**What's at stake:** IPAA revolutionized UC surgery by offering continence preservation. The trade-offs (bowel frequency, pouchitis risk, fertility impact) must be weighed against the alternative (permanent ileostomy) and against continued medical management. For young women planning pregnancy, the fertility concern may tip the decision toward delayed surgery or ileostomy.

**My assessment:** Steinhart presents IPAA honestly, including its limitations. The fertility discussion is particularly important and often underemphasized in surgical consultations. His inclusion of this data point reflects the patient-centered approach of the book.

## Claim 4: Strictureplasty conserves bowel length in Crohn's patients at risk of short bowel syndrome

**Author's claim:** "Strictureplasty is a method of avoiding removal of additional segments of intestine... by opening up the affected and narrowed segment of the intestine and creating a larger internal passageway for food to pass through without causing symptoms of obstruction." It is indicated for patients with multiple previous resections or multiple short strictures who are at risk of short bowel syndrome.

**Evidence presented:** Surgical technique description. Steinhart notes the limitations: "The affected segment is often too long," "sometimes too diseased and thickened," and when "there may be only a single short segment of narrowing... the risk of the strictureplasty procedure (leak, infection, recurrent obstruction) is not worth the potential benefit of avoiding a resection."

**Confidence:** HIGH. Strictureplasty is a well-established bowel-sparing technique in Crohn's surgery. Multiple series have demonstrated its safety and efficacy in preserving bowel length. The patient selection criteria Steinhart describes are standard.

**What's at stake:** Short bowel syndrome is "a serious consequence of Crohn's disease surgery and can lead to the need for long-term or permanent intravenous feeding at home." Strictureplasty is the primary surgical strategy to prevent this outcome. Every centimeter of bowel preserved matters.

**My assessment:** This is appropriately presented as a conservation strategy rather than a first-line approach. Steinhart's honesty about when strictureplasty is and is not appropriate (too long, too diseased, single short stricture) gives patients realistic expectations.

## Claim 5: Perianal Crohn's disease requires combined medical-surgical management; setons prevent recurrent abscesses

**Author's claim:** "An approach that is often used to manage perianal Crohn's disease is a combined surgical and medical approach, whereby the surgeon drains any abscesses and puts in setons as necessary and the gastroenterologist treats the Crohn's disease with medication, most often infliximab or adalimumab." Setons work by keeping fistulas open for controlled drainage, preventing the fistula opening from closing and forming an abscess: "the string or tube... keeps the fistula open and allows it to drain in a controlled way."

**Evidence presented:** Clinical practice patterns. Steinhart acknowledges "there haven't been large trials to study this combined approach, but it appears that it may provide long-term healing and symptom control better than what can be provided by either surgery or drug therapy alone." Surgical flap procedures have "a fairly high failure rate of approximately 40% to 50%."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for combined approach (well-established in practice, supported by observational data, no large RCTs). HIGH for seton function. HIGH for flap failure rate.

**What's at stake:** Perianal disease is one of the most disabling manifestations of Crohn's. The combined med-surg approach has transformed outcomes from a cycle of recurrent abscesses and sphincter damage to controlled drainage and biologic-mediated healing. Access to coordinated gastroenterology-colorectal surgery care is inequitably distributed, as Steinhart notes.

**My assessment:** The combined approach is the standard of care for complex perianal Crohn's. Steinhart's description of seton mechanics (counterintuitive -- keeping a fistula open to prevent abscesses) is well-explained for a patient audience. His acknowledgment that coordinated care is "not readily available everywhere" is an honest structural critique.

## Claim 6: Smoking cessation is the most effective intervention for preventing post-surgical Crohn's recurrence

**Author's claim:** "Perhaps the best way to reduce the risk of recurrence of Crohn's disease after surgery is not to smoke cigarettes. Although it has not been proven that for smokers who quit after surgery the risk of recurrent disease is lower, it has been shown that smokers tend to have earlier and more severe recurrences after surgery."

**Evidence presented:** Observational data. Steinhart lists the medication options for post-operative prevention: 5-ASA (reduces recurrence by "about 10% to 12%"), antibiotics ("not clear whether the beneficial effect... extends past 1 year after surgery"), thiopurines ("evidence supporting their use is not entirely clear"), and infliximab ("some evidence that it is an effective preventive treatment after surgery").

**Confidence:** HIGH for smoking as a risk factor for recurrence. MEDIUM for the comparative efficacy of pharmacologic prevention -- the evidence base has evolved since 2018, with anti-TNF agents (particularly infliximab) now having stronger evidence for post-operative prevention, especially in high-risk patients.

**What's at stake:** Post-operative recurrence prevention is the unsolved problem in Crohn's surgery. The 5-ASA benefit (10-12% risk reduction) is modest. The thiopurine evidence is mixed. Anti-TNF agents have the strongest evidence. The decision to use prophylaxis versus surveillance colonoscopy at 6-12 months with treatment only for endoscopic recurrence (the "treat when you see it" strategy) remains debated.

**My assessment:** Steinhart correctly identifies smoking as the single most impactful modifiable risk factor. His review of pharmacologic options accurately reflects the 2018 evidence base. Since then, the PREVENT trial and other studies have strengthened the case for post-operative anti-TNF prophylaxis in high-risk patients. The surveillance colonoscopy approach he describes (scope at 6-12 months, treat endoscopic recurrence before symptoms) has become standard practice.

## Claim 7: Laparoscopic surgery reduces scarring and may speed recovery, but benefits over open surgery in complex IBD are modest

**Author's claim:** "When the conditions are right, laparoscopic surgery can be done safely and without an increase in complications over the open approach. It leaves a much smaller scar on the abdomen than open surgery. It may also reduce pain and speed up recovery." However, "these benefits over the open approach in the average case of IBD are not as obvious. There is still postoperative pain requiring medication, a hospital stay that averages about 5 days if no complications occur, and 3 to 6 weeks before someone is ready to return to work."

**Evidence presented:** Surgical experience since approximately 1990. Steinhart notes limitations: adhesions from prior surgery, complex fistulizing disease, and abscesses may preclude laparoscopic completion and require conversion to open.

**Confidence:** HIGH. Laparoscopic colectomy and ileocolic resection are well-established. The cosmetic benefit is real; the recovery time advantage in IBD specifically is real but smaller than in other laparoscopic procedures (e.g., cholecystectomy).

**What's at stake:** For a young patient facing a permanent abdominal scar, the cosmetic difference of laparoscopy may be psychologically significant. The recovery time advantage, while modest, matters for return to work and school.

**My assessment:** Steinhart's even-handed assessment is appropriate. He doesn't oversell laparoscopy but acknowledges its advantages. The conversion-to-open discussion sets realistic expectations -- patients should not feel the surgery "failed" if laparoscopic completion isn't possible.

## Ch8 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: UC surgery curative, Crohn's surgery not | HIGH | Pouchitis complicates "cure" for UC IPAA patients |
| 2: Surgical rates 70-80% CD, 20-40% UC | MEDIUM | Modern rates are lower with biologic era |
| 3: IPAA with >90% satisfaction, specific complications | HIGH | Pouchitis rates may be higher long-term; fertility data is rough estimate |
| 4: Strictureplasty conserves bowel length | HIGH | Limited to short, non-inflamed strictures |
| 5: Combined med-surg for perianal CD | MEDIUM-HIGH | No large RCTs; access to coordinated care is uneven |
| 6: Smoking cessation for recurrence prevention | HIGH | Pharmacologic prevention evidence is stronger now for anti-TNF |
| 7: Laparoscopic benefits modest in IBD | HIGH | Benefit is real but smaller than for gallbladder surgery |

**Strongest section:** The pelvic pouch (IPAA) section is comprehensive -- it covers the procedure, the temporary ileostomy, bowel function expectations (4-12 BMs/day), complications (leak, obstruction, pouchitis), and the fertility impact. This is exactly the information a UC patient facing colectomy needs.

**Weakest section:** The post-operative pharmacologic prevention section is thin relative to its clinical importance. The evidence for specific agents (5-ASA: 10-12% reduction; antibiotics: unclear durability; thiopurines: "evidence not entirely clear"; anti-TNF: "some evidence") is presented without the risk-stratification framework (high-risk vs low-risk patients) that would help patients understand who needs prophylaxis.

**Overall quality:** An excellent patient-facing surgical overview. The case studies (Kelly's progression from steroid-responsive UC to colectomy consideration; Lauren's fibrostenotic Crohn's with mechanical obstruction requiring resection despite biologic therapy) illustrate the clinical reasoning that leads to surgical referral. The stoma education (appliances, odor, gas, intimacy, ET nurse role) is notably thorough and destigmatizing. The key weakness is a lack of comparative data to help patients weigh surgery against continued medical therapy -- but this reflects the state of the evidence rather than an omission by the author.
