# Yamada 7E -- Ch64: Crohn's Disease: Clinical Manifestations and Management

The authoritative textbook chapter on Crohn's disease management from Yamada's Textbook of Gastroenterology, 7th Edition (2022). Authored by Ha, McGovern, and Melmed at Cedars-Sinai's F. Widjaja Foundation Inflammatory Bowel and Immunobiology Research Institute. This chapter represents the clinical canon: comprehensive, heavily referenced, and organized around evidence-based treatment algorithms. The treatment section covers 5-ASA, antibiotics, corticosteroids, thiopurines, methotrexate, anti-TNF biologics, vedolizumab, ustekinumab, therapeutic drug monitoring, postoperative prevention, and special populations.

## Claim 1: Treat-to-target with objective markers (not just symptoms) improves long-term outcomes in CD

**Author's claim:** "To truly reduce long-term outcomes of CD, clinicians should aim to achieve not only symptomatic improvement but rather deep remission as a goal of therapy." Deep remission encompasses symptomatic improvement, normalization of CRP, and endoscopically confirmed mucosal healing. The STRIDE recommendations propose evaluation every 6-9 months with objective markers (colonoscopy, imaging, CRP, fecal calprotectin), escalating therapy when targets are not met.

**Evidence presented:** The REACT and CALM trials are cited as the evidence basis. REACT demonstrated improved long-term outcomes (hospitalization, surgery) with algorithmic 12-week reassessment and dose adjustment based on symptom-based scores. CALM showed that targeting objective markers (fecal calprotectin, CRP) produced superior outcomes to symptom-based management alone. Mucosal healing is associated with reduced hospitalization, surgery, and greater likelihood of maintaining remission off biologic therapy.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The treat-to-target paradigm is now standard of care, endorsed by STRIDE-II and major guidelines. The CALM trial (2017) is the landmark study. The authors' framing of "deep remission" as encompassing clinical, biochemical, and endoscopic remission is the current consensus.

**What's at stake:** If treat-to-target is correct, then monitoring with biomarkers and endoscopy is not optional -- it drives therapeutic decision-making. This represents a shift from the historical pattern of reacting to symptoms. It also means patients who are "feeling well" may need therapy escalation based on objective inflammation, which requires patient buy-in.

**My assessment:** This is the most important conceptual claim in the chapter. The authors present it as settled, which reflects the 2022 publication date -- the evidence from CALM (2017) had matured. The discussion of the "therapeutic pyramid" being "challenged on several fronts" and replaced by treat-to-target is historically illuminating: it captures the paradigm shift that occurred between 2010-2020.

## Claim 2: 5-ASA preparations are not effective for Crohn's disease despite widespread clinical use

**Author's claim:** "Neither sulfasalazine nor mesalamine was effective at maintaining remission in an intention-to-treat analysis." Sulfasalazine showed modest benefit over placebo for colonic CD (but inferior to corticosteroids). Mesalamine, "regardless of dose, was not effective for induction of remission or response." The authors explicitly state that "the use of 5-ASAs for the treatment of active CD or maintenance of medically induced remission is probably not much better than placebo" and that "drug expenditures for the treatment of CD using this therapeutic class are enormous, despite the fact that no 5-ASA products are US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved for CD."

**Evidence presented:** Cochrane systematic review and meta-analysis. Sulfasalazine: RR of 0.85 for induction in colonic disease. Mesalamine: per-protocol analysis modestly reduced relapse risk (RR 0.79, 95% CI 0.66-0.95) but intention-to-treat was negative. ACG and ECCO guidelines hold 5-ASA is "probably not much better than placebo."

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is one of the clearest evidence-to-practice gaps in gastroenterology. The data is consistent across multiple systematic reviews. The authors' explicit criticism of ongoing widespread use ("despite the fact that no 5-ASA products are FDA approved for CD") is unusually pointed for a textbook.

**What's at stake:** If 5-ASA is ineffective for CD, billions of dollars in drug expenditure are wasted annually, and patients are exposed to ineffective therapy while disease progresses. The authors note the clinical inertia: "their relative safety and physician familiarity with these medications" perpetuates use despite evidence.

**My assessment:** This is a well-argued section. The authors are methodical: they present the trial data, the meta-analyses, the guideline positions, and then explicitly call out the practice gap. This is exactly the kind of evidence-to-practice friction that the OSKG is designed to capture.

## Claim 3: Corticosteroids effectively induce remission but do not produce mucosal healing and should not be used for maintenance

**Author's claim:** "Corticosteroids have been shown to be more effective than placebo, sulfasalazine, and elemental diets for the induction of clinical remission." However, "corticosteroids are not associated with mucosal healing despite the fact that they are effective at reducing symptoms" and "steroids are not considered to be appropriate long-term therapy for maintenance of remission because of lack of efficacy and the significant toxicities associated with long-term corticosteroid exposure."

**Evidence presented:** Multiple studies comparing corticosteroids to placebo, sulfasalazine, and elemental diets. Population-based data showing "about a third of patients with CD treated with corticosteroids for a disease flare will be steroid dependent over the following year." Budesonide (ileal-release, 9 mg daily) is more effective than placebo for mild-moderate ileal CD but less effective than prednisone, and is not effective for maintenance beyond 6 months.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The steroid paradox (effective for symptoms, ineffective for mucosal healing, toxic long-term) is well-established. The budesonide data is consistent across trials.

**What's at stake:** Steroid dependence and steroid toxicity remain major clinical problems. The need for corticosteroids is itself "a marker of poor prognosis." Patients who require repeated steroid courses should be escalated to steroid-sparing therapy (immunomodulator or biologic).

**My assessment:** The authors present the steroid data clearly and with appropriate clinical guidance. The budesonide section correctly positions it as a first-line agent for mild-moderate ileal/right-sided CD while noting its limitations. The statement that "patients who require a prolonged course or repeated courses of corticosteroids should be considered for maintenance therapy with an immunomodulator or biologic" is the key clinical takeaway.

## Claim 4: Combination therapy (anti-TNF + thiopurine) is superior to either agent alone for induction and maintenance in CD

**Author's claim:** In the SONIC trial, "patients who received the combined regimen had superior rates of corticosteroid-free clinical remission at 6 months relative to patients treated with AZA or infliximab alone (57% vs 44% vs 30%)." Furthermore, "patients treated with combination therapy were more likely to achieve mucosal healing." The concomitant use of AZA is "the only clinically modifiable factor definitively associated with an increased likelihood of response to induction and maintenance infliximab therapy."

**Evidence presented:** SONIC trial (n > 500, infliximab + AZA vs infliximab vs AZA). Combotherapy patients had lower anti-infliximab antibodies and higher infliximab drug levels. The COMMIT trial (methotrexate + infliximab vs infliximab monotherapy) did not show superiority for the primary endpoint (treatment failure) but did show lower antibody formation with combotherapy (4% vs 20%, p = 0.01).

**Confidence:** HIGH for infliximab + AZA combination (SONIC). MEDIUM for methotrexate + anti-TNF (COMMIT was negative for primary endpoint; benefit limited to antibody reduction). The authors appropriately distinguish between these two combinations.

**What's at stake:** Combination therapy improves efficacy but increases immunosuppressive burden (infection risk, lymphoma risk -- particularly HSTCL in young males). The risk-benefit calculus differs by patient age, sex, and disease severity. The boxed warning for HSTCL on anti-TNF labels is a direct consequence of combination therapy in young males.

**My assessment:** The authors present the SONIC data as transformative, which it was (2010). Their handling of the risk-benefit tension is appropriately nuanced: "the potential gain of using combination therapy needs to be carefully weighed against the theoretical risk of using two immunosuppressive agents." The COMMIT discussion is honest about the negative primary endpoint while acknowledging the antibody benefit.

## Claim 5: Vedolizumab and ustekinumab provide mechanistically distinct alternatives to anti-TNF with favorable safety profiles

**Author's claim:** Vedolizumab "is an effective induction and maintenance agent approved for use in CD," with "up to 89% of patients in remission after 3 years" among initial responders, though "the efficacy of vedolizumab is lower among those with prior anti-TNF failure." Ustekinumab "is an effective induction and maintenance agent for CD among patients who are experienced or naive to anti-TNF therapy." Neither carries the boxed warnings for serious infections or lymphoma that anti-TNF agents carry.

**Evidence presented:** Vedolizumab: pivotal trials (GEMINI), post-hoc analyses showing trough level-response relationships, immunogenicity of ~5%. Ustekinumab: UNITI trials, immunogenicity <5% through week 156, higher trough levels correlated with clinical remission. The authors note that drug level monitoring for these agents is less established than for anti-TNF.

**Confidence:** HIGH for efficacy in both anti-TNF-naive and experienced populations (ustekinumab stronger in experienced). HIGH for safety advantage. MEDIUM for the clinical utility of TDM with vedolizumab/ustekinumab (relationship less clear than for anti-TNF).

**What's at stake:** These drugs have expanded second-line options dramatically. For elderly patients, those with infection risk, or those with cancer history, vedolizumab or ustekinumab may be preferred over anti-TNF as first-line biologic (see Table 64.4 in the chapter). The absence of boxed warnings is clinically meaningful.

**My assessment:** The authors present a balanced comparison. The vedolizumab section appropriately notes its lower efficacy in anti-TNF-experienced patients. The ustekinumab section highlights its lower immunogenicity. The Table 64.4 (special considerations) is clinically useful: abscess → drainage + antibiotics/biologics; TB+ → vedolizumab/thiopurine/methotrexate; melanoma history → vedolizumab; older age → vedolizumab or ustekinumab.

## Claim 6: Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) is a cost-effective strategy for optimizing biologic therapy

**Author's claim:** "Measurement of drug concentration and anti-drug antibody levels has been shown to be a cost-effective strategy given the ability to optimize treatment and potentially prevent CD-associated complications." Higher trough levels are associated with improved efficacy including mucosal healing. Anti-drug antibodies are "present in as many as 65% of patients treated with episodic infliximab" and associated with infusion reactions and loss of response.

**Evidence presented:** Trough level thresholds: infliximab >=5 ug/mL, adalimumab >=7.5 ug/mL, certolizumab >=20 ug/mL for clinical remission. Loss of response: 13% risk per year for infliximab, 24% per year for adalimumab. Addition of immunomodulator reduces antibody formation. HLA-DQA1*05 allele associated with immunogenicity. Dose escalation strategies: infliximab 5 mg/kg q8wk -> 10 mg/kg q8/6/4wk; adalimumab 40 mg q2wk -> 40 mg weekly.

**Confidence:** HIGH for the association between trough levels and outcomes. HIGH for the cost-effectiveness of TDM. MEDIUM for specific trough thresholds (assay-dependent). The immunogenicity data (65% with episodic dosing) underscores the importance of scheduled maintenance therapy over episodic treatment.

**What's at stake:** TDM transforms biologic therapy from empiric ("it stopped working") to mechanistic ("your drug level is subtherapeutic due to antibodies -- let's add an immunomodulator or switch mechanism of action"). This prevents futile dose escalation and identifies patients who need a different drug class.

**My assessment:** This section is notably thorough and practical. The distinction between primary non-response (no benefit from optimized exposure) and secondary non-response (initial response then loss) is clinically foundational. The dose escalation strategies (infliximab 10 mg/kg q4wk, adalimumab weekly) give concrete guidance. The approach to anti-drug antibodies (low levels may be transient; high levels require drug switch) is evidence-based.

## Claim 7: Postoperative endoscopic recurrence is rapid and predictable; risk-stratified prophylaxis and 6-12 month surveillance colonoscopy are standard

**Author's claim:** "Endoscopic recurrence rates of 70-90% have been reported as early as 1 year after surgery." The Rutgeerts score (i0-i4) at 6-12 months postoperatively "correlated strongly with risk for future clinical recurrence." Those with i0-i1 had 20% clinical recurrence at 3 years; those with i3-i4 had >90% clinical recurrence. "Patients at low risk... can be monitored without immediate postoperative treatment, and patients at high risk... should be started on anti-TNF therapy shortly after surgery."

**Evidence presented:** Rutgeerts' prospective study (n=89). Anti-TNF for postoperative prevention: infliximab reduced endoscopic recurrence from 85% to 9% (p<0.001) in one RCT; the PREVENT trial showed infliximab associated with significantly less endoscopic recurrence at 76 weeks; the POCER study found adalimumab superior to AZA in high-risk patients. Mesalamine: NNT of 10 for clinical recurrence prevention (modest). Antibiotics: metronidazole reduced endoscopic recurrence from 75% to 52% (p=0.09) but intolerance limits use. Thiopurines: NNT of 13 and 7 for clinical and endoscopic recurrence respectively.

**Confidence:** HIGH for the Rutgeerts classification and its prognostic value. HIGH for anti-TNF superiority in high-risk patients. MEDIUM for the precise risk-stratification schema (risk factors are identified but the optimal prophylaxis strategy for moderate-risk patients remains debated).

**What's at stake:** Without postoperative prophylaxis, the majority of Crohn's patients will have endoscopic recurrence within a year and 50% will require re-operation within 10 years. Risk-stratified prophylaxis with colonoscopic surveillance represents a systematic approach to breaking this cycle.

**My assessment:** This is an excellent synthesis of postoperative management. The Rutgeerts score is explained with clinical context. The evidence hierarchy for each drug class is presented (anti-TNF > thiopurines > antibiotics > 5-ASA > budesonide/placebo). The risk-stratified algorithm (low risk: monitor; moderate: thiopurine +/- antibiotics; high: anti-TNF) is evidence-based and clinically actionable.

## Claim 8: The therapeutic pyramid (step-up) model is obsolete; early effective therapy prevents bowel damage

**Author's claim:** "The concept of a broad-based pyramid implies that the majority of patients have mild, uncomplicated, and nonprogressive disease. Although many patients will have mild disease 5 years after diagnosis, the long-term clinical course suggests gradual progression toward fibrostenotic and penetrating complications for the majority of patients over time." Furthermore, "several lines of evidence suggest that earlier use of biologic agents improves overall long-term complications requiring hospitalization and surgery, and that these agents are more effective when used earlier in the disease course."

**Evidence presented:** Natural history studies showing progressive complications. The TREAT safety registry: concomitant corticosteroid or narcotic use were each associated with increased mortality -- challenging the assumption that "lower" therapies on the pyramid are safer. The treatment algorithm (Figure 64.15) stratifies by disease severity AND prognosis, with anti-TNF + immunomodulator positioned as first-line for moderate-severe disease.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The pyramid model has been replaced by risk-stratified early intervention. The evidence that biologics are more effective earlier in the disease course (before irreversible bowel damage) is consistent across multiple lines of evidence.

**What's at stake:** If the pyramid is wrong, then "step-up" therapy (starting with safest/weakest drugs and escalating on failure) allows preventable bowel damage. The paradigm shift toward early effective therapy is the most significant change in CD management philosophy in the biologic era.

**My assessment:** The authors dismantle the pyramid model systematically: (1) it assumes most patients have mild disease (false -- natural history shows progression), (2) it assumes lower-tier therapies are safer (false -- steroid/narcotic mortality in TREAT), (3) it ignores the window of opportunity for biologics to prevent damage. This is the strongest section of the chapter from a conceptual standpoint.

## Ch64 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Treat-to-target with objective markers | HIGH | Implementation barriers (cost, patient adherence, access to monitoring) |
| 2: 5-ASA ineffective for CD | HIGH | Per-protocol analyses show modest benefit; some colonic CD may respond |
| 3: Steroids induce remission not healing | HIGH | Budesonide positioning may shift with newer therapies |
| 4: Combination anti-TNF + thiopurine superior | HIGH (SONIC) | HSTCL risk in young males limits universal application |
| 5: Vedolizumab/ustekinumab safety advantages | HIGH | Efficacy in anti-TNF-experienced is lower for vedolizumab |
| 6: TDM cost-effective | HIGH | Thresholds are assay-dependent; vedolizumab/ustekinumab TDM less established |
| 7: Post-op risk-stratified prophylaxis | HIGH | Optimal strategy for moderate-risk patients remains debated |
| 8: Pyramid model obsolete | HIGH | Early biologic use may overtreat indolent disease; risk stratification is imprecise |

**Strongest section:** The treatment algorithm (Figure 64.15) and Table 64.4 (special considerations) together provide a clinically actionable framework. The evidence synthesis for each drug class is methodical, with systematic review data, number-needed-to-treat/harm where available, and explicit acknowledgment of evidence gaps.

**Weakest section:** The nutrition/diet section is notably thin for a chapter of this scope. EEN is mentioned for pediatrics but adult dietary strategies receive minimal attention. Given the patient interest in diet as therapy and the emerging evidence base (CDED, Mediterranean diet), this gap is significant.

**Overall quality:** This is the definitive reference chapter for CD management. The authors at Cedars-Sinai bring both research depth and clinical pragmatism. The treatment algorithm is evidence-based, the drug class discussions include both trial data and practical monitoring guidance, and the special populations section addresses the most common clinical scenarios (abscess, TB, pregnancy, elderly). The chapter's conceptual framework -- treat-to-target, early effective therapy, risk-stratified management -- reflects the 2022 state of the art.
