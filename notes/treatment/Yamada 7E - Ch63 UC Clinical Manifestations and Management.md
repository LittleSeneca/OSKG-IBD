# Yamada 7E -- Ch63: Ulcerative Colitis: Clinical Manifestations and Management

The authoritative textbook chapter on ulcerative colitis management from Yamada's Textbook of Gastroenterology, 7th Edition (2022). This chapter covers the full UC treatment armamentarium: 5-ASA formulations, corticosteroids, thiopurines, methotrexate, anti-TNF biologics, vedolizumab, JAK inhibitors, and the treatment monitoring framework. Several key findings differentiate UC treatment from Crohn's disease: 5-ASA is first-line and effective for UC (unlike CD), methotrexate is ineffective for UC (unlike CD where it has a role), and JAK inhibitors have robust phase III evidence for UC (OCTAVE program).

## Claim 1: Combined oral and rectal 5-ASA is the most effective induction strategy for mild-moderate UC

**Author's claim:** "Based on a network metaanalysis of 48 induction trials including 8020 participants... combined oral and rectal 5-ASA and high-dose mesalamine (>3 g/day) were ranked most efficacious in inducing remission." Approximately 63%, 33%, and 26% of patients treated with combined oral and rectal 5-ASA, high-dose mesalamine, and standard-dose mesalamine, respectively, would achieve induction of clinical remission.

**Evidence presented:** Network meta-analysis of 48 trials. The comparative efficacy data (Figure 63.16) shows combined oral+rectal 5-ASA with OR 0.21 (95% CI 0.11-0.40) vs placebo -- the strongest effect of any 5-ASA strategy. Standard-dose mesalamine (2-3 g/day) is the recommended starting point for most patients per AGA guidelines. Once-daily dosing is recommended over divided dosing to improve adherence. For maintenance, standard-dose mesalamine is sufficient; high-dose offers no additional maintenance benefit.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The 5-ASA evidence base in UC is among the most robust in IBD. The network meta-analysis methodology is appropriate. The key clinical insight that combined oral+rectal therapy is superior to either alone is important and often underutilized in practice.

**What's at stake:** 5-ASA is the cornerstone of UC therapy. Optimizing 5-ASA delivery (combined routes, adequate dosing, once-daily administration, dose escalation guided by fecal calprotectin) could reduce the number of patients who escalate to immunomodulators or biologics unnecessarily.

**My assessment:** This is the strongest section of the chapter. The authors present a data-driven algorithm (Figure 63.15) with specific evidence rankings. The fecal calprotectin-guided dose optimization study (escalating mesalamine for elevated FC >50 in symptomatic remission reduced clinical relapse) is a practical application of treat-to-target principles to 5-ASA therapy. The reminder that "patients who fail one optimized mesalamine do not benefit from switching to equivalent doses of another mesalamine" counters a common clinical impulse to cycle through 5-ASA formulations.

## Claim 2: Methotrexate is ineffective for ulcerative colitis

**Author's claim:** "Methotrexate is not effective in patients with ulcerative colitis as monotherapy." In the METEOR trial, subcutaneous methotrexate was not more effective than placebo for corticosteroid-free remission (32% vs 20%, p=0.15). In the MERIT-UC withdrawal trial, continuing methotrexate was not more effective than placebo for maintaining remission (27% vs 30%, p=0.86).

**Evidence presented:** Two well-designed RCTs (METEOR and MERIT-UC). The authors explicitly state that "clinical guidelines recommend against the use of methotrexate monotherapy for induction of maintenance in patients with ulcerative colitis."

**Confidence:** HIGH. This is an important evidence-based negative finding. Methotrexate works for Crohn's (parenteral 25 mg weekly) but not for UC. The reason for this disease-specific difference is not well understood.

**What's at stake:** Methotrexate should not be used as a thiopurine alternative in UC. This is a common clinical error -- extrapolating from Crohn's data. Patients who cannot tolerate thiopurines in UC need to move to biologics or JAK inhibitors, not methotrexate.

**My assessment:** The authors present this negative finding clearly and with appropriate supporting evidence. This is a concise, clinically useful section that prevents therapeutic misadventure.

## Claim 3: Infliximab has direct evidence for reducing colectomy and hospitalization in UC (ACT trials)

**Author's claim:** "Infliximab was shown to be effective in inducing and maintaining clinical remission of ulcerative colitis in the ACT-1 and ACT-2 trials." Furthermore, "post hoc analyses of these datasets have demonstrated that early achievement of endoscopic improvement with infliximab is associated with improved long-term clinical outcomes in ulcerative colitis, including reductions in hospitalization and colectomy. Therefore, infliximab has direct evidence in support of its ability to achieve both surrogate endpoints of future complications and of its ability to directly impact the risk of future complications."

**Evidence presented:** ACT-1 and ACT-2 (each n=364, placebo vs infliximab 5 or 10 mg/kg at weeks 0, 2, 6, then every 8 weeks). Clinical response at week 8: 69.4% (ACT-1) and 64.5% (ACT-2) with 5 mg/kg vs 37.2% and 29.3% with placebo. The UC-SUCCESS trial demonstrated combination therapy (infliximab + azathioprine) superiority over monotherapy for clinical remission and endoscopic improvement.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The ACT trials are landmark studies in UC. The colectomy reduction data is a hard endpoint that few UC therapies have demonstrated directly. The UC-SUCCESS trial established combination therapy superiority in UC, paralleling the SONIC findings in Crohn's.

**What's at stake:** Infliximab is the best-studied biologic in UC with the strongest evidence for preventing colectomy. This makes it the preferred anti-TNF for severe hospitalized UC and for patients at high risk of colectomy.

**My assessment:** The authors appropriately emphasize infliximab's unique position in the UC evidence hierarchy. The detailed presentation of ACT trial data (response, remission, mucosal healing, sustained remission) provides the evidence basis for clinical decision-making. The treatment optimization discussion (TDM, combination therapy algorithm Figure 63.20) is practical and nuanced.

## Claim 4: Vedolizumab offers gut-selective immunosuppression with a favorable safety profile

**Author's claim:** "Vedolizumab suppresses inflammation in the gastrointestinal tract without inducing the systemic immunosuppression that characterizes the anti-alpha4 integrin monoclonal antibody natalizumab. This gut-selective targeting of inflammation potentially offers a distinct advantage of treating intestinal mucosal inflammation while avoiding off-target side-effects seen with TNF-alpha antagonists."

**Evidence presented:** GEMINI trial: clinical response at week 6 in 47.1% vs 25.5% placebo (p<0.001). Maintenance at week 52: remission in 42-45% (vedolizumab q8wk/q4wk) vs 16% placebo. Safety: serious infection rates similar to placebo (4.3 vs 3.8 per 100 PY). PML reported only once in a patient with concurrent AIDS. Immunogenicity is low (~5%). Efficacy is higher in anti-TNF-naive patients.

**Confidence:** HIGH for safety advantage and moderate efficacy. MEDIUM for positioning relative to anti-TNF -- vedolizumab has a slower onset of action and generally lower efficacy in anti-TNF-experienced patients but superior safety.

**What's at stake:** Vedolizumab's gut selectivity makes it particularly attractive for elderly patients, those with infection risk, cancer history, or concerns about systemic immunosuppression. The slower onset means it is not appropriate for hospitalized severe UC (where infliximab is preferred).

**My assessment:** The vedolizumab section correctly emphasizes the gut-selectivity advantage. The absence of a boxed warning is clinically meaningful. The authors note that "there is less evidence in support of concomitant immunomodulator therapy improving treatment outcomes with vedolizumab in ulcerative colitis" -- this is an important distinction from anti-TNF, where combination therapy is proven to enhance efficacy.

## Claim 5: Tofacitinib is the first oral JAK inhibitor approved for UC, with efficacy independent of prior anti-TNF exposure

**Author's claim:** "Rates of clinical remission and endoscopic improvement were significantly higher with 10 mg twice-daily dose of tofacitinib for induction, and significantly higher with both the 5 mg and 10 mg twice-daily dose of tofacitinib during maintenance." Importantly, "the rates of response to therapy were comparable between TNF-alpha antagonist-naive and TNF-alpha antagonist-exposed individuals, suggesting that prior exposure to TNF-alpha antagonists therapy may not impact treatment efficacy."

**Evidence presented:** OCTAVE Induction 1 and 2, OCTAVE Sustain (phase III program). Remission at induction: ~18.5% tofacitinib vs 8.2% placebo. Maintenance: 34.3% (5 mg) and 40.6% (10 mg) vs 11.1% placebo. Safety: herpes zoster rate is dose-dependent (6.6 per 100 PY at 10 mg bid vs 2.1 at 5 mg bid). No immunogenicity concerns (small molecule). A black box warning exists for thrombosis and mortality at 10 mg bid in rheumatoid arthritis patients with cardiovascular risk factors.

**Confidence:** HIGH for UC efficacy. HIGH for shingles risk. MEDIUM for long-term safety (cardiovascular/thrombotic signals from RA population require ongoing monitoring).

**What's at stake:** JAK inhibitors are the first oral advanced therapy for UC. For patients averse to injections/infusions, they represent a paradigm shift. The comparable efficacy in anti-TNF-naive and experienced patients is a significant advantage over vedolizumab (which works better in naive patients). The shingles risk mandates vaccination (non-live recombinant zoster vaccine) before starting therapy.

**My assessment:** The tofacitinib section appropriately emphasizes both the convenience advantage (oral) and the safety concerns (shingles, potential cardiovascular risk). The authors note that tofacitinib "should not be used alongside other immunomodulators" -- a pragmatic safety recommendation. The OCTAVE data is presented clearly with the dose-response relationship for both efficacy and herpes zoster risk.

## Claim 6: Reactive therapeutic drug monitoring is recommended for anti-TNF agents in UC; proactive TDM lacks strong evidence

**Author's claim:** "Current guidelines therefore recommend that drug concentration monitoring only be done reactively when a patient is observed to have active persistent disease despite therapy to assess for whether they have adequate drug exposure and are a true nonresponder, and/or if they have developed antidrug antibodies as a source of nonresponse."

**Evidence presented:** The TAXIT trial (n=263 IBD patients, 85 UC): drug concentration-based management achieved similar remission rates to clinically-based management (69% vs 66%) but lower relapse rates (7% vs 17%, p=0.018). However, a subsequent Crohn's trial comparing symptom-based, biomarker-based, and drug concentration-based dosing found no significant difference. The authors conclude "there appears to be a lack of strong evidence in support of proactive therapeutic drug monitoring."

**Confidence:** HIGH for reactive TDM (assessing drug levels/antibodies when disease is active). MEDIUM for proactive TDM (routine dose adjustment to target levels). The evidence is mixed and guideline recommendations appropriately favor reactive over proactive TDM.

**What's at stake:** Reactive TDM prevents inappropriate dose escalation in patients with adequate drug levels and identifies antibody-mediated loss of response that requires a switch in drug class rather than dose increase. Proactive TDM (routine monitoring in well patients) is more controversial -- it could prevent future loss of response but may also lead to unnecessary dose escalation.

**My assessment:** This is a nuanced and evidence-based discussion of TDM. The distinction between reactive and proactive TDM is clinically important but often conflated. The authors' conclusion that proactive TDM lacks strong evidence and should not be routine is consistent with current guidelines.

## Ch63 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1: Combined oral+rectal 5-ASA superiority | HIGH | Patient adherence to rectal therapy limits real-world effectiveness |
| 2: Methotrexate ineffective for UC | HIGH | Negative finding -- unlikely to be overturned |
| 3: Infliximab with colectomy reduction evidence | HIGH | Positioning challenged by vedolizumab (safety) and tofacitinib (oral) |
| 4: Vedolizumab gut-selective safety | HIGH | Lower efficacy in anti-TNF-experienced patients |
| 5: Tofacitinib oral JAK inhibitor for UC | HIGH | Long-term cardiovascular safety data still maturing |
| 6: Reactive TDM recommended; proactive unproven | HIGH | Future trials may shift toward proactive TDM |

**Strongest section:** The 5-ASA treatment algorithm (Figure 63.15) and the evidence synthesis (Figure 63.16 network meta-analysis) represent the most comprehensive evidence-based approach to 5-ASA optimization in any textbook. The treatment monitoring framework (Figure 63.12) integrates symptoms, biomarkers, and endoscopy into an actionable clinical algorithm.

**Weakest section:** Newer therapies (upadacitinib, ozanimod) receive brief mention -- the chapter was written before some 2022 approvals. The surgical section is thin relative to the medical management content, deferring to Chapter 65.

**Overall quality:** This is the definitive reference for UC management. The 2022 edition captures the expanding therapeutic landscape (vedolizumab, tofacitinib, ustekinumab for UC) with evidence-based treatment algorithms. The treatment monitoring framework is particularly strong -- it operationalizes treat-to-target with specific thresholds (RBS, fecal calprotectin, MES) and a clear algorithm for when to escalate.
