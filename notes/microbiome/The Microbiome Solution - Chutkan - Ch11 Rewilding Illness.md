# Chutkan — Ch 11: A Rewilding Approach to Illness

Practical clinical guidance for minimizing microbiome damage during illness and medical treatment. The chapter provides specific scripts for patient-clinician communication, protocols for antibiotic damage mitigation, a catalog of medications to avoid, alternative therapies for common conditions, and a microbiome-preserving birth plan. It is the "how to" companion to Ch 5's "what is dysbiosis."

## Claim 1: Patients should actively question antibiotic prescriptions using a structured set of questions — physician prescribing behavior is influenced by perceived patient expectations

**Author's claim:** Doctors prescribe more antibiotics when they perceive the patient wants one. A structured set of 10 questions (Table 11-1) can signal that the patient is "not keen on taking an antibiotic unless absolutely necessary" and shift the clinical conversation from automatic prescribing to shared decision-making.

**Evidence presented:** Cites studies showing that "doctors are far more likely to prescribe an antibiotic when they perceive that the patient is interested in taking one." The ten questions range from the foundational ("Is the antibiotic absolutely necessary?") to the practical ("What's the shortest amount of time I can take it for?") to the risk-assessment ("What's the worst thing that could happen if I don't take an antibiotic?").

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The evidence that perceived patient expectation drives antibiotic prescribing is well-documented. The specific question set has not been validated in a trial but follows shared decision-making principles.

**What's at stake:** If patients can be trained to question antibiotic prescriptions effectively, they function as a check on overprescribing at the point of care. This is a bottom-up supplement to antibiotic stewardship programs.

## Claim 2: When antibiotics are unavoidable, a protocol of probiotics, prebiotic foods, sugar elimination, and liver support can mitigate microbiome damage

**Author's claim:** The "Ten Things to Do When Taking Antibiotics" (Table 11-2) includes: (1) take a probiotic during and for at least one month after antibiotics, staggered from antibiotic doses; (2) request narrow-spectrum rather than broad-spectrum antibiotics when culture results permit; (3) eat prebiotic foods high in fiber and resistant starch; (4) eliminate sugary/starchy foods that feed yeast overgrowth; (5) eat yeast-fighting foods (onion, garlic, coconut oil); (6) drink ginger tea for GI comfort; (7) use bentonite clay for antibiotic-associated diarrhea; (8) drink mushroom tea (shiitake, maitake) for immune support; (9) support liver function with leafy greens and cruciferous vegetables; (10) stop acid-suppressing drugs 72 hours before antibiotics to restore stomach acid barrier function.

**Evidence presented:** For probiotics: cited studies showing reduction in antibiotic-associated diarrhea and C. difficile risk. For narrow-spectrum antibiotics: culture-directed therapy is standard infectious disease practice. For dietary recommendations: mechanistic reasoning based on prebiotic/anti-yeast principles.

**Confidence:** HIGH for probiotics during antibiotics (multiple RCTs support reduction in AAD and C. diff), HIGH for narrow-spectrum preference (standard of care), MEDIUM for specific dietary protocols (reasonable but not tested as a package), LOW for bentonite and mushroom tea (traditional remedies without antibiotic-specific trial evidence).

## Claim 3: Multiple common medications beyond antibiotics should be avoided in dysbiosis patients, including PPIs, steroids, hormonal contraceptives, and NSAIDs

**Author's claim:** Table 11-3 catalogs the "Medications to Avoid" for microbiome health: broad-spectrum antibiotics (including topical forms), acid-suppressing drugs (particularly beyond 8 weeks), oral/IV corticosteroids, birth control pills, hormone replacement therapy, and NSAIDs including aspirin.

**Evidence presented:** Reiterates and organizes the evidence from Ch 4-5. Specific tapering guidance for PPIs (rebound acid hypersecretion) and steroids (adrenal suppression).

**Confidence:** HIGH for PPIs (C. diff risk, microbiome alteration well-documented), HIGH for steroids (immunosuppression, fungal overgrowth), MEDIUM for hormonal contraceptives (association with yeast infections but microbiome-specific evidence is thinner).

**Clinical relevance for IBD:** This list creates a tension in IBD management — steroids are a mainstay of flare treatment, and many IBD patients use PPIs, NSAIDs, or hormonal contraceptives. Chutkan's framework forces acknowledgment that these medications, while sometimes necessary, have microbiome costs that should be factored into risk-benefit calculations.

## Claim 4: Antibiotic use for acne is a major risk factor for later IBD, and dermatology's routine use of long-term oral antibiotics represents a significant public health problem

**Author's claim:** "Almost all of the patients I see in my practice with Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis have a history of taking antibiotics for acne, and while that doesn't necessarily prove causation in those particular patients, the medical literature is full of studies that now confirm that antibiotic use, particularly in childhood, is a major risk factor for the development of these diseases."

**Evidence presented:** Cites the epidemiological literature. Describes acne antibiotics as especially damaging because: they are broad-spectrum, taken for months or years, and highly active against gut bacteria. Notes that dermatologists often tell teenagers "what they eat doesn't have any effect on their skin" while prescribing years of microbiome-damaging antibiotics — a practice Chutkan finds indefensible.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the association. The acne-antibiotic-IBD pathway is epidemiologically supported. The claim about "almost all" IBD patients in her practice is a clinical observation, not a controlled finding.

**What's at stake:** This claim, if robust, has major implications for dermatology practice standards and informed consent for acne treatment. The risk of IBD should be disclosed to patients (and parents) before initiating long-term antibiotic therapy for a non-life-threatening condition.

## Claim 5: A microbiome-preserving birth plan should prioritize vaginal delivery, avoidance of unnecessary interventions, immediate skin-to-skin contact, and breastfeeding

**Author's claim:** Table 11-5 provides a detailed "Sample Pregnancy and Birth Plan/Goals" covering pregnancy (avoid antibiotics and medications, avoid early induction), labor (no IV/catheter unless necessary, dim lights, freedom of movement, avoid Pitocin, pain management choice), delivery (immediate skin-to-skin, breastfeeding within first hour, no baby antibiotics without consent, no antibacterial products on baby), C-section (vaginal swabbing of baby if C-section unavoidable, immediate breastfeeding post-surgery), and breastfeeding (no formula without medical necessity, rooming-in).

**Evidence presented:** Based on the microbiome seeding literature (Ch 2, 7): vaginal delivery inoculates the infant with maternal vaginal and fecal bacteria; C-section babies are colonized by skin and environmental bacteria instead; breastfeeding provides human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) that selectively feed Bifidobacterium species.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The microbiome benefits of vaginal delivery and breastfeeding are well-established. The birth plan format is a patient-empowerment tool, not a clinical trial — its value is in giving patients language to advocate for evidence-based microbiome-preserving practices.

**My assessment:** This is one of the book's strongest practical contributions. The birth plan synthesizes microbiome evidence into actionable patient communication. The inclusion of vaginal swabbing for C-section babies (a practice that was gaining attention in 2015) is appropriately cautious in its language ("please consider allowing").

## Chapter Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Structured antibiotic questioning | MEDIUM-HIGH | No trial validation of the question set |
| Antibiotic damage mitigation protocol | HIGH (probiotics), MEDIUM (diet/supplements) | Package not tested as protocol |
| Avoid PPI/steroid/NSAID when possible | HIGH | Practicality in IBD management |
| Acne antibiotics → IBD risk | MEDIUM-HIGH | Clinical observation, not controlled evidence |
| Microbiome-preserving birth plan | HIGH | Supported by microbiome seeding literature |

**Strongest sections:** The 10 questions for antibiotic prescribing and the birth plan. Both are practical, evidence-grounded, and give patients language for clinical conversations they might otherwise not know how to have.

**Weakest section:** The dietary and supplement recommendations during antibiotics (beyond probiotics) rely more on traditional remedies and mechanistic reasoning than on antibiotic-specific clinical trials. They are unlikely to harm and may help, but the evidence base is thin.

**Clinical significance for IBD/SIBO:** The acne-antibiotic-IBD claim is the most important takeaway for the knowledge graph. Ch 11 also provides the practical bridge between recognizing dysbiosis (Ch 5) and doing something about it — the patient-empowerment scripts are useful for translating microbiome knowledge into clinical action.
