# Pimentel & Rezaie -- Ch10-11: Probiotics, FMT, and the 10 Myths of IBS

The book's closing chapters serve a corrective function: dismantling misconceptions about interventions that don't work (probiotics, prebiotics, fecal transplant) and myths about IBS that persist despite contrary evidence. Together they form Pimentel's final argument that IBS is an organic disease requiring mechanism-directed treatment, not a functional disorder responsive to generic "gut health" interventions.

## Ch10: Probiotics, Prebiotics, and Fecal Transplantation

### Claim 1: There is no definitive evidence that probiotics are beneficial for IBS or SIBO; the concept of "good bacteria" is a marketing construct

**Author's claim:** "There's really is no such thing as 'good' and 'bad' native bacteria in the gastrointestinal tract. Actually, when it comes to the gastrointestinal tract, the concept of 'good' bacteria is a misnomer... The concept of 'good' bacteria has been largely promoted by the companies that manufacture probiotics."

Evidence quality: "Most studies show no statistically significant improvement in IBS symptoms with probiotics over placebo." Meta-analyses are compromised by pooling different microorganisms (Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Saccharomyces) as if equivalent, publication bias (negative studies unpublished), and variable quality.

Specific finding: "One recent double-blind study found that there were no measurable improvements among IBS patients who were given Lactobacillus acidophilus. There was, however, some improvement in IBS symptoms among patients who were given Bifidobacterium bifidum."

**Evidence presented:** Multiple RCTs and meta-analyses. The Lancet 2013 study: 3000 patients taking probiotics post-antibiotics showed "exactly the same" rates of C. difficile infection and diarrhea as placebo; probiotics caused more bloating and flatulence. Two NEJM studies: "probiotics don't protect against viral gastroenteritis." Quality control: "up to half of probiotic supplements don't contain the right amount, the right strain, or the live probiotics listed on the label."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The evidence for probiotics in IBS is genuinely weak, and Pimentel's critique of meta-analysis methodology is valid. However, some specific strains (Bifidobacterium infantis 35624, now Align) have shown benefit in well-designed trials. Pimentel acknowledges this: "Bifidobacterium bifidum" showed some improvement. The blanket statement that probiotics don't work overstates the evidence against specific strains.

**What's at stake:** Probiotics are a multi-billion-dollar industry. If they don't work for IBS/SIBO, patients are wasting money and delaying effective treatment. The "good bacteria" marketing construct (perpetuated by companies like Activia, Align, Culturelle) undermines the more nuanced understanding of the microbiome that Pimentel advocates.

**Who disagrees:** Probiotic manufacturers. Some academic microbiome researchers would argue that specific strains have demonstrated efficacy (e.g., B. infantis 35624 for IBS, VSL#3 for ulcerative colitis). The debate is whether probiotics as a category are ineffective (Pimentel's position) or whether the right strain at the right dose for the right patient can be effective.

**My assessment:** Pimentel's probiotic skepticism is evidence-based but overbroad. The "probiotics don't work" claim is stronger for IBS/SIBO than for other conditions (e.g., VSL#3 has good evidence in pouchitis and UC). His core argument -- adding more bacteria to a condition defined by bacterial overgrowth is illogical -- is mechanistically sound but doesn't preclude specific strains having beneficial metabolic or immunomodulatory effects independent of bacterial numbers. The quality control problem (half of products don't contain what's on the label) is a genuine concern that applies across the supplement industry.

### Claim 2: Fecal transplantation for IBS has failed in randomized trials -- the two largest trials were negative, and one showed placebo superiority

**Author's claim:** "In six randomized controlled trials of fecal transplant in IBS, one trial was positive, one was barely positive, one was neutral, one was negative, and two were strongly negative (the placebo worked better than the transplant). The two largest, most robust trials were conducted in Denmark and the US and they were both negative."

Key finding: "The transplant made the patients' stool look like a normal person's stool, but the IBS patients still had symptoms. This tells us that the source of IBS symptoms is in the small intestine, not the colon."

**Evidence presented:** Danish trial (placebo capsules superior to FMT capsules for IBS symptoms). US trial (stopped early due to placebo superiority). Harvard study (antibiotics + FMT showed no difference from antibiotics alone). Safety concern: FDA reported two deaths from multidrug-resistant bacteria during FMT in immunocompromised patients (2019). Donor SIBO risk: "If the donor had SIBO, the fecal transplant recipient had a 50 percent chance of developing IBS after the transplant."

**Confidence:** HIGH for the trial results (published data). The mechanistic interpretation (small intestine vs. colon) is Pimentel's inference but consistent with REIMAGINE data.

**What's at stake:** FMT has been hyped as a cure-all for microbiome diseases. The IBS trial data strongly suggests it is not effective. The finding that FMT normalizes stool microbiome composition without improving symptoms is the strongest evidence for Pimentel's thesis that IBS originates in the small intestine, not the colon.

**My assessment:** The FMT-for-IBS failure is one of the most important negative results in the microbiome field. It demonstrates that stool microbiome composition is not the driver of IBS symptoms, supporting Pimentel's small-intestine-focused approach. The donor SIBO transmission risk is a cautionary tale that fecal transplantation is not benign -- it can transmit functional GI disorders.

### Claim 3: Prebiotics and synbiotics are unproven for IBS and may worsen SIBO by feeding overgrown bacteria

**Author's claim:** "If you have SIBO, taking a prebiotic may actually provide food for the bacteria you don't want to overgrow. We've also found that prebiotics cause more bloating in our patients." Synbiotics: "There's not a lot of data to show that synbiotics are effective in treating IBS." The FDA "doesn't supervise probiotics or prebiotics, as they're considered food, not medication."

**Evidence presented:** Clinical observation at Cedars-Sinai (prebiotics cause bloating). Mechanistic argument: prebiotics are fermentable fiber that feeds all bacteria, including the overgrown disrupter species.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The mechanistic argument is sound (feeding bacteria when you have bacterial overgrowth is counterproductive). Clinical data on prebiotics in SIBO specifically is limited. The general prebiotic literature (outside SIBO) shows benefits for metabolic health.

**My assessment:** Pimentel's prebiotic caution is logical within the SIBO framework. For a patient with confirmed SIBO, adding fermentable fiber is genuinely likely to worsen symptoms. For patients without SIBO, prebiotics may have metabolic benefits. The problem is that many IBS patients are prescribed prebiotics without first being tested for SIBO.

## Ch11: The 10 Myths of IBS

| # | Myth | Verdict | Key Point |
|---|------|---------|-----------|
| 1 | SIBO is not a real medical condition | FALSE | Most gastroenterologists now recognize SIBO; findings replicated by other researchers |
| 2 | Stomach acid can be replaced with oral HCl tablets | FALSE | Stomach produces 2L acid/day; a few tablets are physiologically irrelevant; may worsen methane SIBO |
| 3 | IBS is more common in women due to lower pain threshold | FALSE | Hormonal (progesterone affects motility); post-infectious IBS more common in women (autoimmune link); IBS-C predominantly female, IBS-D predominantly male |
| 4 | Restricted diets are safe long-term | FALSE | Overly restrictive diets cause macro/micronutrient deficiencies; standard reintroduction within 2-6 weeks; no evidence restrictive diets prevent IBS in family members |
| 5 | Symptom relief from antidepressants means IBS is psychological | FALSE | Antidepressants work through side effects (constipation), not antidepressant mechanism; rifaximin NNH 846 vs. tricyclic NNH 2.3 |
| 6 | IBD/celiac patients can't also have IBS | FALSE | 57% of IBD patients with controlled inflammation but persistent symptoms have SIBO; celiac patients with partial gluten-free diet response commonly have SIBO |
| 7 | Federal research funding correlates with disease burden | FALSE | IBD gets ~$250M/year (1.2M patients); IBS gets $10-15M/year (40M patients) -- 20x less funding despite 33x more patients |
| 8 | IBS is a disease of developed countries | FALSE | Highest rates in Africa, Mexico, Pakistan (up to 40%); global prevalence 11.2% |
| 9 | Exercise makes IBS better | TRUE | Increases blood flow to gut, improves motility, regulates bowel movements, releases stress |
| 10 | IBS is contagious and hereditary | FALSE | Not contagious (requires both SIBO + impaired motility); genetic predisposition suggested (11% of Campylobacter-infected develop IBS) but microbiome sharing doesn't transmit IBS |

### Claim 4 (Myth 2 resolution): HCl supplementation is physiologically implausible and potentially harmful for SIBO

**Author's claim:** "The stomach produces two liters (about half a gallon) of acid each day. Taking a few tablets of hydrochloric acid doesn't make physiologic sense as SIBO treatment... adding more acid to the gut may fuel more methane production (recall that methane-producing archaea use the hydrogen in acid to make methane), thus making constipation symptoms of SIBO even worse."

Additionally: "In a large study, we have shown that antacids do not increase the chance of SIBO, likely because antacids don't change the acidity of the small bowel, which is controlled by the pancreas."

**Evidence presented:** Pimentel's large published study on PPIs and SIBO (finding no association). Physiological reasoning about acid production volume vs. supplement dose.

**Confidence:** HIGH for the PPI-SIBO finding (published data). MEDIUM for the HCl-harm claim (mechanistic inference; no direct study of HCl supplementation in SIBO).

**What's at stake:** HCl supplementation is commonly recommended by naturopaths and functional medicine practitioners for SIBO, based on the hypothesis that low stomach acid permits bacterial survival. Pimentel argues this is physiologically implausible (stomach acid volume dwarfs supplement dose) and potentially harmful (acid may fuel methanogens).

**My assessment:** Pimentel's debunking of HCl supplementation is physiologically sound. The more important finding is the PPI-SIBO non-association: despite widespread belief that PPIs cause SIBO by reducing stomach acid, Pimentel's data suggests they don't. This challenges a common functional medicine teaching and is consistent with the primacy of small intestinal (not gastric) protective factors.

### Claim 5 (Myth 5 resolution): The risk-benefit ratio of antidepressants for IBS is inferior to SIBO-directed therapy

**Author's claim:** "2.3 people would benefit from tricyclic antidepressants for IBS before one person had to stop the drug due to a side effect or harm. Contrast that to rifaximin, where 846 people would get better before another person might experience a side effect."

**Evidence presented:** Pimentel's published "number needed to harm" analysis. Systematic review of antidepressant trials in IBS showing "small sample sizes with variable design. More importantly, a significant proportion of these trials didn't properly report the adverse events and side effects."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. The NNH numbers come from Pimentel's analysis and reflect his methodology. Independent systematic reviews have also noted the limitations of antidepressant trials in IBS but have been more favorable about their clinical utility.

**What's at stake:** If antidepressants are genuinely more harmful and less effective than SIBO-directed therapy (rifaximin + prokinetics), then antidepressant prescribing for IBS should be reserved for patients who have failed or been ruled out for SIBO treatment. The NNH comparison (2.3 vs. 846) implies a 368-fold difference in harm profile.

**My assessment:** The NNH comparison, even if directionally correct, almost certainly overstates the difference. Tricyclic antidepressants at low doses (10-25 mg) have been used safely for decades. The 2.3 NNH likely reflects high-dose studies or includes mild side effects (dry mouth) rather than serious harms. Rifaximin's 846 NNH is for serious harm requiring drug discontinuation. This is comparing different harm thresholds. Pimentel's point (rifaximin is safer than tricyclics for IBS) is defensible; the magnitude of the difference is overstated.

## Combined Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| No definitive evidence for probiotics in IBS/SIBO | MEDIUM-HIGH | Specific strains (B. infantis) show benefit in some trials |
| FMT trials negative for IBS | HIGH | Published RCT data; mechanistic inference is Pimentel's |
| Prebiotics may worsen SIBO | MEDIUM | Mechanistically sound; limited SIBO-specific clinical data |
| HCl supplementation is implausible | HIGH (physiology) | HCl supplement advocates would dispute |
| Antidepressants inferior to SIBO therapy | MEDIUM-HIGH | NNH comparison likely overstates difference |

**Strongest section:** The FMT for IBS failure analysis. The finding that FMT normalizes stool microbiome without improving IBS symptoms is the strongest evidence that IBS is a small intestinal disease, and validates Pimentel's two-decade focus on the small intestine.

**Weakest section:** The probiotic discussion, which treats probiotics as a homogeneous category when strain-specific effects are well-documented for some conditions. Pimentel is right about probiotics in IBS/SIBO specifically but overstates the case against probiotics in general.

**Closing note:** The book's final chapters return to the themes of the Introduction: IBS is a real, organic disease; SIBO is its most common cause; mechanism-directed therapy works; generic "gut health" interventions (probiotics, prebiotics, restrictive diets, FMT) don't. The 10 myths serve as a summary of the book's argument in reverse -- each myth represents a misconception the book aims to correct. The Conclusion's closing message: "IBS is an invisible illness with no visible manifestations, but it's time for IBS to be recognized as a legitimate gastrointestinal disorder." This is the same message as the Introduction, now supported by 11 chapters of evidence.
