# Pimentel & Rezaie -- Ch5: Small Intestinal Bacterial Overgrowth -- Definition, Diagnosis, and the Three-Gas Model

This chapter operationalizes SIBO: the quantitative definition (>10^3 CFU/mL), the diagnostic tools (lactulose breath test vs. jejunal aspirate culture), and the three-gas model that categorizes SIBO subtypes by dominant microbial gas production. It introduces the critical 2020 terminology change from "methane-dominant SIBO" to "Intestinal Methanogen Overgrowth (IMO)," reflecting the finding that methane producers are archaea (Methanobrevibacter smithii), not bacteria. The three-gas model (hydrogen, methane, hydrogen sulfide) provides a mechanistic framework linking specific microbial populations to specific symptom profiles.

### Claim 1: SIBO is defined as >10^3 CFU/mL in the small intestine and is caused by impaired clearance, not infection

**Author's claim:** "SIBO occurs when there are greater than 1000 bacteria per milliliter (>10^3/mL) in the small intestine. In SIBO, this excessive quantity of bacteria is not an infection, but rather an over-colonization of specific bacteria in an area where they don't belong. The bacteria aren't invading or attacking you; they are your own resident bacteria. SIBO is, in effect, a phenomenon of bacteria dislocated from your large intestine to your small intestine."

The normal gradient: "your mouth contains millions of bacteria, but there are less than 100 bacteria per mL in your stomach... In the small intestine, the quantity of resident bacteria increases, but there are usually less than 1000 per mL."

**Evidence presented:** Standard SIBO definition from gastroenterology. The REIMAGINE study's confirmation of the small intestine-colon bacterial gradient. The mechanism of bacterial reflux: "the ileocecal valve... is a simple connector rather than a functioning sphincter. This means that the valve never fully closes and therefore bacteria can pass through it. The small intestine dumps these bacteria back into the colon when the housekeeper waves kick in."

**Confidence:** HIGH for the >10^3 definition (established consensus). MEDIUM for the colonic reflux hypothesis -- REIMAGINE data suggests small intestinal SIBO bacteria may not be exclusively colonic in origin but rather an overgrowth of native small intestinal proteobacteria.

**What's at stake:** The distinction between SIBO as bacterial overgrowth vs. infection is clinically important: antibiotics for SIBO aim to reduce bacterial load, not eradicate a pathogen. The SIBO-is-not-infection framing explains why SIBO recurs if the underlying clearance defect is not addressed.

**My assessment:** The >10^3 definition is the field standard, though some researchers use >10^5 for jejunal aspirate. Pimentel's SIBO-as-dislocation model is his working framework, but the REIMAGINE finding that the small intestine has its own distinct microbiome (proteobacteria + firmicutes) complicates the simple colon-to-small-intestine reflux model. The "lawn and weeds" analogy (proteobacteria as weeds outcompeting firmicutes grass when the MMC "lawnmower" fails) is more consistent with REIMAGINE data.

### Claim 2: The small intestinal "disrupter" bacteria in SIBO are predominantly E. coli and Klebsiella (both hydrogen producers)

**Author's claim:** "In a recent publication, we showed that the two main 'weeds' in SIBO are E. coli and Klebsiella. These bacteria are referred to as 'disrupters.' When they increase in quantity, they bully the other bacteria to die off and they dominate the small intestine with their presence and their effects. And, by the way, they are hydrogen producers."

**Evidence presented:** REIMAGINE study data. The ecological model: "Think of the small intestine as a lawn. If you don't mow the lawn (if there are no cleaning waves), the weeds (proteobacteria) that are always there outperform the grass (firmicutes, a type of gram-positive bacteria)."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH. This is one of the most important findings from REIMAGINE and represents the first characterization of the specific bacterial species overgrown in SIBO, as opposed to the historical assumption that SIBO involved colonic-type bacteria. However, this is from a single research group's data and needs independent confirmation.

**What's at stake:** If E. coli and Klebsiella are the primary SIBO pathogens, then: (a) rifaximin should be particularly effective against these gram-negative bacteria, (b) probiotic strains that compete with E. coli/Klebsiella might be preventive, and (c) breath test patterns (hydrogen production) should correlate with E. coli/Klebsiella abundance.

**My assessment:** The identification of specific disrupter species is a major advance beyond the generic "SIBO is colonic bacteria in the wrong place" model. It opens the door to targeted antimicrobial therapy and biomarker development. The E. coli/Klebsiella finding also explains why rifaximin (broadly active against gram-negatives) is effective for SIBO.

### Claim 3: The three-gas model explains SIBO symptomatology: hydrogen (bloating), methane/IMO (constipation), hydrogen sulfide (diarrhea)

**Author's claim:** "There are several subpopulations of microorganisms in SIBO, depending on which gas they produce after metabolizing food. One group of bacteria produces hydrogen, which can lead to bloating. Another group produces hydrogen sulfide, which usually leads to diarrhea, pain, and an urgent need to run to the bathroom. A third group of microorganisms is not bacteria, but rather archaea: single-celled organisms that produce methane. Methane affects normal movement in the gastrointestinal tract, and leads to spastic contractions that slow down propagation of contents through the bowel. That's why constipation is very common in this group of patients."

The ecological competition model: "Think of the hydrogen sulfide-reducing bacteria as foxes and the methanogen-reducing microorganisms as wolves. They're both looking for prey -- hydrogen molecules... If the wolves (methane-producing microorganisms) win, you become constipated. If the foxes (hydrogen sulfide-reducing) win, then you develop diarrhea."

**Evidence presented:** Breath test data showing distinct hydrogen, methane, and hydrogen sulfide patterns correlated with symptom profiles. The mechanism: hydrogen is produced first (by E. coli, Klebsiella), then scavenged by either methanogens (producing methane → constipation) or sulfate-reducing bacteria (producing hydrogen sulfide → diarrhea). The "winner" of the hydrogen competition determines symptoms.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for hydrogen (bloating) and methane (constipation). MEDIUM for hydrogen sulfide (diarrhea) -- the hydrogen sulfide breath test is newer and less validated. The ecological competition model is elegant but simplified; many patients likely have mixed gas profiles.

**What's at stake:** The three-gas model transforms SIBO from a single disease to a family of related conditions distinguished by dominant gas type. Treatment should be gas-type-specific: rifaximin for hydrogen SIBO, different approaches for IMO (methane), and still-developing approaches for hydrogen sulfide SIBO. The model also explains why some SIBO patients have diarrhea while others have constipation -- it's not random variation but reflects different microbial populations.

**My assessment:** The hydrogen/methane distinction is well-established and clinically actionable. The hydrogen sulfide component is newer and needs more validation -- the hydrogen sulfide breath test is not yet commercially available. The "foxes and wolves" competition model is a useful heuristic but likely oversimplifies a more complex microbial ecosystem. Nonetheless, this is the most clinically useful conceptual framework for SIBO subtyping.

### Claim 4: Lactulose breath testing is superior to glucose breath testing for SIBO diagnosis because it assesses the full length of the small intestine

**Author's claim:** "Glucose is very easy for humans to digest... the body is designed to absorb glucose in the first few feet of the small intestine, but it's absorbed so fast that any bacteria below four feet in the small intestine won't receive any glucose. If you have bacterial overgrowth below those four feet of small intestine (which, remember, is 20 feet long), we won't be able to assess three-quarters of the small intestine using glucose in the breath test. Hence, we believe lactulose is a better substrate for breath testing."

**Evidence presented:** Physiological argument: glucose is absorbed in the proximal small intestine; lactulose is non-absorbable and therefore available to bacteria throughout the small intestine. The 90-minute cutoff for hydrogen rise: "If your hydrogen levels rise by 20 parts per million (ppm) or more before or at 90 minutes, then we consider this gas derives from excess bacteria in the small intestine... We have shown that the 90-minute cutoff correlates directly with the small bowel microbiome."

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The glucose vs. lactulose debate is one of the most contentious in SIBO diagnosis. Glucose breath testing has higher specificity (less false positives from rapid transit) but lower sensitivity (misses distal SIBO). Lactulose has higher sensitivity but lower specificity (rapid transit can produce false positives). The ACG 2020 guideline notes that "there is insufficient evidence to recommend one substrate over another."

**What's at stake:** If lactulose is truly superior (higher sensitivity, acceptable specificity), then glucose breath testing should be abandoned and the higher SIBO prevalence figures (60-70%) are correct. If glucose is superior (higher specificity, acceptable sensitivity), then lactulose overdiagnoses SIBO and the true prevalence is lower.

**Who disagrees:** Many academic gastroenterologists prefer glucose breath testing for its higher specificity. The North American Consensus on breath testing (2017) acknowledged both substrates as acceptable. The debate reflects a deeper tension: is it worse to miss SIBO (false negative with glucose) or to overdiagnose it (false positive with lactulose)?

**My assessment:** Pimentel's argument for lactulose is physiologically sound -- distal SIBO is common, and glucose simply can't reach it. The specificity concern (false positives from rapid transit) is real but may be overstated in clinical practice. The 90-minute cutoff is designed to minimize colonic fermentation, and dual-peak patterns (early rise, fall, late colonic rise) can help distinguish small intestinal from colonic gas production. For clinical purposes, lactulose's higher sensitivity is preferable: missing SIBO is worse than a false positive that leads to a treatment trial.

### Claim 5: The 2020 terminology change from "methane-dominant SIBO" to "Intestinal Methanogen Overgrowth (IMO)" is scientifically necessary because methane producers are archaea, not bacteria

**Author's claim:** Methane production involves "Methanobrevibacter smithii (M. smithii for short), which is not bacteria but rather archaea." The diagnostic criterion: "If at any point over two hours your methane level exceeds 10 ppm, this is considered positive for methane and you are diagnosed with what is now called intestinal methanogen overgrowth (IMO)."

**Evidence presented:** The three-step pathway: sugar → hydrogen-producing bacteria (E. coli, Klebsiella) → hydrogen → methanogenic archaea (M. smithii) → methane. Methanogens are archaea, a distinct domain of life separate from bacteria. Despite the name change, the clinical presentation (constipation, bloating) and treatment implications remain similar.

**Confidence:** HIGH. M. smithii is definitively an archaeon, not a bacterium. The terminology correction is scientifically accurate. The clinical implications of the name change are minimal (treatment approaches remain similar), but the conceptual precision matters.

**What's at stake:** The IMO terminology resolves a semantic inaccuracy that had persisted for years. It also has practical implications: antibiotics that target bacterial cell walls (e.g., penicillins) will not affect archaea, which have different cell wall structures. Rifaximin, which has broad activity, may have different efficacy against IMO than against hydrogen SIBO.

**My assessment:** The IMO renaming is a scientifically necessary correction that Pimentel's group championed. It reflects the growing recognition that the gut microbiome includes archaea and fungi, not just bacteria. Clinically, the key distinction is that IMO may require different antimicrobial approaches (e.g., neomycin or metronidazole added to rifaximin) than hydrogen-predominant SIBO.

### Claim 6: Jejunal aspirate culture -- the traditional "gold standard" -- is unreliable due to contamination and sampling limitations

**Author's claim:** "This culturing technique... has been considered the gold standard of SIBO diagnosis. That said, it can't really be the ultimate gold standard, as laboratories have differing culture techniques; furthermore, not all bacteria are culturable. Aspirations are taken from only a few inches of the small intestine, which may not represent the whole small intestine." Contamination is a major problem: "One study from Dr. Brian Lacy at Mayo Clinic showed that sampling the small bowel with conventional techniques has about 20 percent rate of contamination."

Pimentel's lab achieves better results: "We never see those numbers in our aspirations because we use a special catheter with a sterile barrier tip... we see about half the quantity of excessive bacteria in the aspirate than do other medical centers, meaning that about half of the aspirate labeled as positive for bacteria from other centers is actually contaminated."

**Evidence presented:** Lacy/Mayo Clinic contamination data (20%). Pimentel's own experience with sterile catheter technique. The broader limitations of culture-based methods: many gut bacteria are unculturable; sampling only the proximal small intestine misses distal overgrowth; standard lab processing (treating aspirate like urine culture with >100,000 threshold) is inappropriate.

**Confidence:** HIGH for the limitations of aspirate culture. The 20% contamination rate is published data. The claim that breath testing correlates with treatment response and is therefore clinically valid is well-supported.

**What's at stake:** If the "gold standard" is unreliable, then breath testing -- which is non-invasive, cheaper, and assesses the full small intestine -- may be the superior diagnostic tool despite its own limitations. This inverts the traditional diagnostic hierarchy.

**My assessment:** Pimentel's critique of aspirate culture is well-founded. The field has long treated jejunal aspirate as an imperfect gold standard while acknowledging its limitations. The question is whether breath testing is good enough to replace it. Pimentel's argument that breath testing correlates with treatment response is the most clinically relevant standard: a test that predicts who will respond to treatment is more useful than a test that meets an arbitrary microbiological threshold.

## Chapter 5 Overall Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| SIBO defined as >10^3 CFU/mL; caused by impaired clearance | HIGH (>10^3 definition) / MEDIUM (colonic reflux model) | REIMAGINE suggests native small intestine overgrowth, not just colonic reflux |
| E. coli and Klebsiella are primary SIBO disrupters | MEDIUM-HIGH | Single-group data; needs independent replication |
| Three-gas model (H2, CH4, H2S) explains symptoms | MEDIUM-HIGH (H2/CH4) / MEDIUM (H2S) | H2S test not yet commercially available |
| Lactulose superior to glucose for breath testing | MEDIUM | Field lacks consensus; ACG says evidence insufficient |
| IMO terminology replaces methane-dominant SIBO | HIGH | Archaeal nature of methanogens is definitive |
| Jejunal aspirate is an unreliable gold standard | HIGH | 20% contamination rate; culture limitations well-documented |

**Strongest section:** The three-gas model and IMO terminology. These represent genuine conceptual advances that have practical implications for diagnosis and treatment. The "foxes and wolves" ecological competition model is a useful clinical heuristic.

**Weakest section:** The lactulose vs. glucose debate. Pimentel's argument for lactulose is physiologically reasonable but the field has not reached consensus, and the ACG guideline does not endorse one substrate over the other.

**Historical note:** The 2022 book's SIBO chapter represents a substantial evolution from the 2006 book. In 2006, SIBO was presented as a single entity diagnosed by lactulose breath test and treated with antibiotics. In 2022, SIBO is a family of conditions (hydrogen SIBO, IMO, hydrogen sulfide SIBO) with distinct microbial populations and gas profiles, diagnosed by multi-gas breath testing, and treated with gas-type-specific antibiotic combinations. The three-gas model did not exist in 2006. This is the most important conceptual evolution in Pimentel's thinking between the two books.
