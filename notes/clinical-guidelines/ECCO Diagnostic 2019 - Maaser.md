# ECCO-ESGAR Guideline for Diagnostic Assessment in IBD Part 1 (Maaser et al., 2019)

The first joint diagnostic guideline from ECCO and ESGAR, merging the former separate imaging and endoscopy guidelines. Drafted by 30 members from 17 European countries. Part 1 (of two) covers initial diagnosis, monitoring of known IBD, and detection of complications. Part 2 addresses scoring systems and technical aspects. Uses the Oxford CEBM grading system (EL1=highest to EL5=expert opinion), with statements finalized at ≥80% consensus (Barcelona, October 2017). This is the authoritative European diagnostic reference, distinct from US guidelines in its emphasis on intestinal ultrasound (IUS), transmural healing as an endpoint, and detailed histopathology criteria.

## Scope and Methodology

Five working parties: initial diagnosis, monitoring treatment, detecting complications, scoring systems, and technical aspects. Two voting rounds with national representation from ECCO's 36 and ESGAR's 28 member countries. Unlike the ACG GRADE system (strong/conditional), ECCO uses the Oxford EL system — statements describe consensus positions with evidence levels rather than actionable recommendation strengths. The guideline contains 44 numbered statements (1.1-3.8.4) organized across three chapters.

## Initial Diagnosis

### Claim 1: No single reference standard exists for IBD diagnosis — composite assessment of clinical, biochemical, endoscopic, imaging, and histological findings is required

**Statement 1.1:** "A single reference standard for the diagnosis of CD or UC does not exist. The diagnosis is based on a combination of clinical, biochemical, stool, endoscopic, cross-sectional imaging, and histological investigations." **[EL5]**

**Evidence summary:** At minimum: biochemical assessment (FBC, CRP, electrolytes, LFTs), stool microbiology (including *C. difficile*), and ileocolonoscopy with biopsies. CRP broadly correlates with clinical severity in CD but less so in UC; laboratory markers may be normal in both. Loose stools >6 weeks discriminate IBD from most infectious diarrhoea. Anaemia workup uses inflammation-adjusted ferritin cut-offs: <30 µg/L without active disease [EL2]; up to 100 µg/L may still indicate iron deficiency with inflammation [EL4]; ferritin 30-100 µg/L + transferrin saturation <20% suggests mixed iron deficiency + ACD [EL2]. Immunisation status (HBV, HAV, measles, varicella, HCV, EBV, HIV) should be assessed at diagnosis; consider latent TB screening [EL5].

**Statement 1.2:** "Genetic or serological testing is currently not recommended for routine diagnosis of CD or UC." **[EL3]** — pANCA/ASCA accuracy is limited; antiglycan/antimicrobial antibodies add little value; >250 IBD SNPs exist but no genetic panel allows diagnosis.

**Comparison against G1 (ACG Crohn's 2018):** G1 similarly recommends FC for IBD/IBS differentiation (R1) and against serologic/genetic testing (Summary Statements 21-23). ECCO provides more detailed anaemia workup with inflammation-adjusted ferritin cut-offs (from the separate ECCO Anaemia Guideline integrated here). G1's diagnostic section is notably shorter (summary statements, not GRADE-rated). ECCO's Statement 1.10 ("all newly diagnosed CD patients should undergo small bowel assessment" [EL2]) is stronger than G1's more permissive recommendation.

**My assessment:** The composite diagnostic approach is methodologically honest — IBD lacks a pathognomonic test. The inflammation-adjusted ferritin cut-offs are a crucial practical distinction absent from G1. The vaccination screening recommendations reflect ECCO's emphasis on preventive care before immunosuppression.

### Claim 2: Ileocolonoscopy with biopsies is required for diagnosis; endoscopic and histological features differ between CD and UC; the earliest UC histological feature is basal plasmacytosis

**Statement 1.6:** Ileocolonoscopy with biopsies from inflamed and uninflamed segments required [EL1]; sigmoidoscopy may suffice in acute severe colitis [EL3]. **Statement 1.7:** No single endoscopic feature is specific [EL2]; UC features = continuous, confluent colonic involvement with clear demarcation and rectal involvement; CD features = discontinuous lesions, strictures, fistulae, perianal involvement.

**Histopathology:** **CD** — granulomas (when present with one other feature on surgical samples), focal/patchy chronic inflammation, mucin preservation at active sites. Patchy inflammation is only diagnostic in untreated adults. No consensus on minimum number of features. **UC** — focal/diffuse basal plasmacytosis is the earliest diagnostic feature (38% within 2 weeks of symptoms); crypt distortion in only ~20% at 2 weeks; widespread architectural distortion appears ≥4 weeks. Correct UC diagnosis in ~75% with 2-3 features; exact minimum unknown. Infectious colitis is distinguished by preserved crypt architecture, though very early disease can also show preserved architecture.

**Statement 1.9:** Upper GI endoscopy only in CD patients with upper GI symptoms, not routine in asymptomatic adults [EL5]. May support diagnosis when histology is difficult (focal gastritis can be a CD feature). Mandatory if coeliac disease is suspected.

**Comparison against G1:** G1 mentions granulomas (up to 33%) and biopsies of uninvolved mucosa but provides minimal histopathology detail. ECCO's temporal description of UC histopathology evolution — basal plasmacytosis within 2 weeks, crypt distortion later, architectural distortion at 4+ weeks — is a uniquely detailed contribution that explains why early IBD can be histologically ambiguous.

**My assessment:** The EL1 rating for ileocolonoscopy with biopsies is appropriate — this is the diagnostic bedrock. The temporal histopathology description (early basal plasmacytosis → later crypt distortion) is the guideline's most clinically useful histopathology contribution, directly explaining why repeat endoscopy may be needed when initial biopsies are non-diagnostic.

### Claim 3: Fecal calprotectin is the most sensitive non-invasive inflammatory marker; all newly diagnosed CD patients require small bowel assessment via MRE, IUS, and/or capsule endoscopy

**Statement 1.10:** "All newly diagnosed CD patients should undergo small bowel assessment (intestinal ultrasound, MR enterography and/or capsule endoscopy)." **[EL2]**

**Evidence summary:** **FC:** Most sensitive marker; good diagnostic accuracy at ~150 µg/g cut-off (meta-analysis, Lozoya Angulo 2017) but no exact IBD/IBS cut-off exists; lacks specificity to discriminate IBD from other intestinal inflammation. FC correlates well with endoscopic indices and is useful in initial diagnosis, relapse detection, and treatment monitoring. Likelihood of positive SBCE is very low with FC <50 µg/g.

**SBCE:** Two validated indexes (CECDAI, Lewis Score). Diagnostic yield comparable to MRE/IUS except proximal small bowel where SBCE is superior (OR 2.62; p=0.03). Capsule retention: ~1.5% in suspected CD, 2-13% in established CD. Use patency capsule if stenosis suspected. MRE overestimates obstruction risk (specificity 59% for predicting retention — ~40% of patients unnecessarily denied SBCE based on imaging alone). ≥3 small intestine ulcers on SBCE highly suggest CD (provided no NSAIDs for ≥1 month) [EL4]. In UC/unclassified IBD, SBCE reclassifies ~25% to CD; 37% remain unclassified. **Statement 1.12:** Balloon-assisted enteroscopy may confirm diagnosis histologically if endoscopy negative and MRE/SBCE positive [EL3].

**Ileocolonoscopy limitations:** Samuel et al. — 53.7% of CD patients with normal ileoscopy had active small bowel CD; disease can skip the distal ileum or be confined to the intramural portion and mesentery.

**Comparison against G1:** G1 recommends small bowel imaging as part of workup; ECCO's "all patients" mandate [EL2] is stronger. G1's SBCE retention risk estimate (0-5.4%) is broader; ECCO provides more precise 1.5%. G1 lacks the "≥3 ulcers" diagnostic threshold. Both agree FC is the preferred non-invasive marker.

**My assessment:** The 53.7% active small bowel CD with normal ileoscopy is the key number justifying mandatory small bowel assessment. The patency capsule specificity limitation (MRE predicts obstruction in only ~60% — meaning 40% unnecessarily denied SBCE) is an important clinical nuance.

### Claim 4: MRI is preferred over CT for small bowel imaging; IUS has comparable diagnostic accuracy and is radiation-free

**Evidence summary (Chapter 1):** CT and MRI have comparable high sensitivity/specificity for small intestinal lesions. Direct comparison: IUS sensitivity 94%, specificity 97% vs. MRE 96%, 94% (Castiglione 2013, 234 patients). IUS less accurate for disease extent (r=0.69) but high concordance for location (κ=0.81). **METRIC trial** (Taylor 2018, 284 patients): MRE significantly greater sensitivity for small bowel disease extent (80% vs. 70%, p=0.027) and presence (97% vs. 92%, p=0.025); MRE specificity 95% vs. IUS 81%. For colonic disease, IUS had superior sensitivity (67% vs. 47%). Due to absence of radiation, MRI should be preferred over CT, particularly in young patients.

**IUS:** Sensitivity 79.7%, specificity 96.7% for suspected CD; 89%/94.3% for known CD (Calabrese 2016 review). Lower accuracy for proximal small bowel. Oral contrast (SICUS) improves sensitivity/specificity. Advantages: non-invasive, widely available, no radiation, real-time visualization of wall thickness, vascularisation, and motility. Limitations: cannot assess rectum or proximal jejunum. **CT:** Largely reserved for emergency setting. Low-radiation CT enterography comparable to full-dose CT. Higher spatial resolution and inter-radiologist agreement than MRI. Barium studies (SBFT) discouraged — inferior to MRE/IUS/SBCE.

**Comparison against G1:** G1 also recommends MRE over CTE for young patients. The major transatlantic divergence: IUS is discussed as a primary modality here but essentially absent from G1. ECCO-ESGAR's radiology collaboration naturally elevates IUS, reflecting European expertise and adoption patterns.

**My assessment:** The METRIC trial provides the most rigorous comparison — MRE is better for small bowel extent (80% vs. 70%), but the difference may not justify MRE in all cases. IUS may suffice for confirming active disease; MRE is better for detailed mapping. The IUS disparity between European and North American guidelines reflects local expertise and reimbursement structures more than evidence.

### Claim 5: Perianal CD requires combined clinical, endoscopic, and imaging evaluation; MRI is first-line for fistula classification

**Statement 1.13:** Unexplained perianal abscesses or complex fistulae should be investigated for CD [EL4]. Perianal fistulas/abscesses occur in 21-54% of CD patients, more frequently with colonic involvement (41%) than isolated ileal disease (12%). A perianal abscess may be the first CD presentation.

**Classification:** "Simple" = low origin, single external opening, no abscesses/rectovaginal fistula/anorectal stricture. "Complex" = high origin, multiple openings, may have abscesses/rectovaginal fistula/anorectal stricture. Ileocolonoscopy should be routine in all perianal CD to assess luminal inflammation and exclude strictures/cancer. Proctitis is a risk factor for persistent non-healing fistulae and increased proctectomy rates. Capsule endoscopy provides 24% incremental diagnostic yield after negative conventional workup including ileocolonoscopy.

**Comparison against G1:** G1's fistulizing section (R35-R43) focuses on treatment; ECCO focuses on detection and classification. Complementary: ECCO tells you how to find/classify; G1 tells you how to treat.

**My assessment:** The "unexplained perianal abscess → investigate for CD" rule is practically important. Capsule endoscopy's 24% incremental diagnostic yield after negative conventional workup justifies its use in this setting.

## Monitoring Known IBD

### Claim 6: Mucosal healing is an important therapeutic goal in both UC and CD; FC is a validated surrogate marker; transmural healing (assessed by MRE/IUS) is an emerging endpoint

**Statement 2.1.1:** Response to treatment in active UC determined by combination of clinical parameters, endoscopy, and laboratory markers (CRP, FC) [EL1]. **Statement 2.1.2:** MH should be determined endoscopically or by FC ~3-6 months after treatment initiation [EL5]. **Statement 2.1.4:** Clinical/biochemical response in CD should be determined within 12 weeks [EL2]; endoscopic/transmural response within 6 months [EL5].

**UC monitoring:** Endoscopy is reference standard; sigmoidoscopy adequate in most. MH associated with reduced colectomy (OR 4.15), long-term clinical remission (OR 4.50), and corticosteroid-free remission (OR 9.70) vs. no MH (Shah 2016 meta-analysis). FC correlates with endoscopic Mayo subscore (r=0.96; p<0.0001). CRP has low sensitivity in UC — frequently normal even in active disease. IUS shows high concordance with endoscopic scores (weighted κ 0.76-0.90) but cannot assess proctitis.

**CD monitoring:** No reference standard for therapeutic success — CDAI is not reliable. SONIC sub-study: MH + steroid-free remission at Week 26 strongly predictive of steroid-free remission at Week 50 (82%). EXTEND: MH at 12 weeks correlated with MH at 52 weeks. **Transmural healing — MRI:** MaRIA score is valid, responsive, and reliable. Ordás et al. (2014): MRE determined ulcer healing with 90% accuracy and endoscopic remission with 83% accuracy. MaRIA scores changed significantly at Week 12 (18.86→8.73; p<0.001). **Transmural healing — IUS:** Anti-TNF reduced bowel wall thickness (p=0.005) and Doppler flow (p=0.02). TRUST trial (Kucharzik 2017, 243 patients, 50 German centres): almost all sonographic parameters significantly decreased (p<0.001); sonographic response at 12 weeks predicted 1-year response (85% vs. 28%; p<0.0001). Radiological response associated with reduced long-term risk of hospitalization, surgery, and corticosteroid use (Deepak 2016).

**Statement 2.1.5:** Endoscopic or cross-sectional reassessment in CD should be considered in relapse, persistent activity, new unexplained symptoms, and before therapy switch [EL5].

**Comparison against G1/G2:** G2 endorses MH as treatment target (R4: conditional, low quality) and FC as surrogate (R5: conditional, very low quality). G1 discusses MH but does not GRADE-rate it. Neither G1 nor G2 discusses transmural healing or IUS monitoring — these are distinctively European contributions.

**My assessment:** The transmural healing concept represents a European advance: CD is transmural, and mucosal-only assessment may miss residual disease. The TRUST trial provides the strongest IUS monitoring data. The 3-6 month reassessment window is practical guidance from trial protocols (EL5, reflecting absence of evidence for optimal timing).

### Claim 7: A structured FC-based monitoring strategy can detect preclinical relapse; two consecutively elevated FC levels are the best predictor of clinical flare

**Statement 2.2.1:** Monitoring aims at early recognition of flare [EL5]; interval 3-6 months depending on remission duration and current therapy [EL5]; relapse can be detected with FC before clinical symptoms [EL2].

**Evidence summary:** The four-phase FC monitoring model (Figure 2): Phase I (diagnosis), Phase II (induction), Phase III (remission — detect drift from target range), Phase IV (therapy adjustment). FC in target range → retest 3 months; action range → adjust treatment, retest 1 month; uncertain range → retest 1 month before decision. Two consecutively elevated FC best predicts clinical relapse. FC rises ~3 months before clinical relapse (Zhulina 2016). A single FC cannot predict course over 12 months. CALM study (Colombel 2018) demonstrated feasibility of tight-control management. CRP ≥5 mg/L has high specificity but poor sensitivity; almost two-thirds of asymptomatic patients with normal CRP have active endoscopic lesions. CRP is inferior to FC for postoperative recurrence or small bowel CD follow-up.

**Statement 2.2.2:** Asymptomatic patients with abnormal biochemical parameters — after excluding infection, perform endoscopic or cross-sectional imaging [EL5].

**Comparison against G1/G2:** G1 references FC for postoperative monitoring and anti-TNF withdrawal prediction but lacks a structured monitoring model. G2 endorses FC as surrogate (R5) without a detailed algorithm. ECCO's four-phase model is the most sophisticated non-invasive monitoring framework in any IBD guideline.

**My assessment:** The CALM study validates tight-control FC monitoring. The 3-month lead time before clinical relapse and the "two consecutively elevated" rule are clinically actionable. Implementation barriers: patient compliance with serial stool testing and lack of standardized action thresholds.

### Claim 8: Assessment of disease activity by endoscopy and/or imaging is necessary before withdrawal of maintenance therapy; TDM may guide management in loss of response

**Statement 2.2.3:** Before de-escalation or withdrawal of maintenance therapy, assess disease activity using clinical/biochemical markers and endoscopic and/or cross-sectional imaging, balancing risks and benefits [EL5].

**Evidence summary:** Meta-analysis (Torres 2015): ~75% relapse within 5 years after stopping immunomodulator monotherapy. ~50% maintained remission 24 months after anti-TNF discontinuation, decreasing over time. GETAID infliximab withdrawal trial (Louis 2012): absence of MH strongly associated with relapse (HR 2.6). Meta-analysis (Gisbert 2016): 1-year relapse 42% after anti-TNF discontinuation, decreasing to 26% when endoscopic remission required.

**Statement 2.1.9:** TDM might be beneficial in non-response to thiopurines [EL3] or anti-TNF [EL2]; mandatory during calcineurin inhibitor therapy [EL2]. TDM led to therapeutic changes in >70% of patients with secondary loss of response (Yanai 2015). High infliximab trough levels correlate with good clinical response and low CRP (meta-analysis of 22 trials, 3,483 patients). Higher adalimumab trough levels associated with MH (median 14.7 vs. 3.4 µg/mL; p<0.001). Primary non-response to anti-TNF in severe UC associated with faecal loss of infliximab (Brandse 2015).

**Comparison against G1:** G1 defers TDM to the separate AGA 2017 TDM guideline. ECCO integrates TDM within the diagnostic monitoring framework — a difference in architecture, not substance.

**My assessment:** The 26% vs. 42% 1-year relapse rate (with vs. without endoscopic remission confirmation) makes the case for objective assessment before withdrawal. TDM integration reflects the European approach of treating drug monitoring as a diagnostic tool.

### Claim 9: Ileocolonoscopy is the reference standard for postoperative recurrence within 6-12 months; FC, IUS, and MRE are non-invasive alternatives with excellent accuracy

**Statement 2.4.1:** Ileocolonoscopy is reference standard for postoperative recurrence after ileocolonic resection; recommended within first 6-12 months [EL3]. **Statement 2.4.2:** FC, IUS, MRE, and SBCE can be considered as non-invasive alternatives, particularly after small bowel resection [EL2].

**Evidence summary:** Without treatment, postoperative endoscopic recurrence: 65-90% within 12 months, 80-100% within 3 years. Rutgeerts score is used for description. After total proctocolectomy + ileostomy: 5-year/10-year median cumulative clinical recurrence 23.5%. **FC:** Meta-analysis (Qiu 2015, 10 studies): sensitivity 0.82, specificity 0.61. FC >100 µg/g: 89% sensitivity, 58% specificity for Rutgeerts ≥i2; NPV 91% — could avoid colonoscopy in 47%. **IUS:** Bowel wall >3.5 mm indicates recurrence; SICUS predicted recurrence correctly in 100% confirmed endoscopically. Bowel thickness >6 mm associated with 40% new surgical intervention vs. 13% for 3 mm. **MRE:** MRI-based index achieved high correlation with Rutgeerts score. Meta-analysis (Yung 2017): MRE, IUS, SBCE all had AUC >0.9 for detecting Rutgeerts ≥2. **SBCE:** Can detect proximal small bowel recurrence beyond anastomosis.

**Ileo-anal pouch (Statement 2.4.3):** Endoscopy with biopsies for pouch-related symptoms [EL2]. Up to 40% single pouchitis episode within 12 months; 19% intermittent, 5% chronic. Pouch failure: 7% at 3 years, 9% at 5 years. PDAI is the most common scoring system. FC ≥92.5 µg/g: 90% sensitivity, 76.5% specificity for pouch inflammation.

**Comparison against G1:** G1 focuses on postoperative prophylaxis (R54-R59) rather than detection. ECCO provides the more comprehensive detection framework.

**My assessment:** FC NPV of 91% for Rutgeerts ≥i2 means a negative FC spares nearly half of postoperative patients from unnecessary colonoscopy. IUS bowel wall cut-offs (>3.5 mm recurrence, >6 mm surgical risk) provide a graded assessment endoscopy alone cannot offer.

## Detection of Complications

### Claim 10: Cross-sectional imaging should detect small bowel strictures; no technique currently determines fibrosis vs. inflammation

**Statement 3.1.1:** Cross-sectional imaging should detect small bowel strictures [EL2]; MRI and/or IUS preferred over CT (radiation); no imaging technique currently determines degree of fibrosis [EL3].

**Evidence summary:** **Accuracy:** IUS sensitivity 79%, specificity 92% (SICUS improves to 89%). CTE sensitivity 92%, specificity 100%. MRI sensitivity 89%, specificity 94%. CT and MRI comparable: 85% vs. 92% sensitivity, 100% vs. 90% specificity. **The fibrosis problem:** Strictures contain variable inflammatory and fibrotic proportions. No technique is sufficiently accurate to distinguish them for clinical decisions. Novel techniques (dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI, magnetization-transfer MRI, ultrasound elastography, CEUS) have limited unstandardised data. Rimola et al. (2015) gadolinium enhancement technique (70s-7min on MRE) distinguished mild/moderate from severe fibrosis irrespective of inflammation — awaits external validation.

**Colonic strictures (Statement 3.1.2):** Any colonic stricture must be carefully surveyed for carcinoma [EL4]; 3.6% and 4.9% CRC probability at 5 and 10 years (Lovasz 2013). Dysplasia/cancer detected in 3.5% of IBD patients undergoing surgery for colonic strictures (GETAID). Yearly surveillance for strictures detected within 5 years. No method (including histology) can definitively rule out malignancy.

**Comparison against G1:** G1 lacks a dedicated stricture detection section. ECCO provides the most detailed stricture imaging guidance of any IBD guideline.

**My assessment:** The admission that no technique determines fibrosis degree is the most honest and clinically frustrating statement in the guideline — this is the holy grail of CD imaging. The 3.5% malignancy rate in colonic strictures justifies aggressive surveillance.

### Claim 11: MRI is the most accurate modality for perianal fistula classification; combining any two of MRI/TRUS/EUA achieves 100% accuracy

**Statement 3.2.2:** MRI is the most accurate and recommended first-line test for perianal CD [EL1]; TRUS is an alternative [EL2]; combining any two (MRI, TRUS, EUA) improves accuracy [EL2]. **Statement 3.2.3:** EUA with drainage recommended if abscess suspected — should not be postponed for imaging [EL2]. **Statement 3.2.4:** Endoscopic rectal evaluation essential for management strategy [EL2].

**Evidence summary:** MRI diagnostic accuracy: 80-100%. TRUS: 50-100% (variable — limited by discomfort, anal stenosis, smaller field of view). Schwartz et al. (2001): EUA 91%, MRI 87%, TRUS 91% accuracy individually; 100% when any two combined. Buchanan et al. (2004): digital rectal exam 33%, TRUS 75%, MRI 85% sensitivity. MRI changed management by detecting unsuspected abscesses; should precede EUA unless immediate sepsis drainage needed. Undiagnosed extensions/abscesses are major causes of recurrent disease after surgical cure.

**Intra-abdominal fistulae/abscesses (Statement 3.2.1):** Fistula: CT sensitivity 70%, specificity 97%; MRE 76%/96%; IUS 74%/95% [EL1]. MRI preferable for deep-seated or pelvic disease [EL4]. Abscess: IUS 84%/93%; CT 86%/88%; MRE 86%/93%. CEUS differentiates phlegmon from abscess.

**Perianal fistula monitoring (Statement 2.1.8):** Reassess by clinical evaluation + rectal endoscopy + MRI [EL1]; TRUS (without stenosis) [EL1] or TPUS [EL2] as MRI alternatives. MRI and endo-anal ultrasound may identify internal fistula tract persistence despite external closure — deep imaging healing is superior to clinical evaluation.

**Comparison against G1:** G1's fistulizing section addresses treatment; ECCO addresses detection and classification. Complementary, not conflicting. G1 lacks head-to-head imaging accuracy data.

**My assessment:** The 100% accuracy with any two combined modalities (Schwartz 2001) justifies the multimodality approach. The "deep healing" concept — imaging-detected internal tract persistence despite external closure — validates MRI as a more stringent endpoint than clinical examination.

### Claim 12: CRC surveillance begins at 8 years with screening colonoscopy; chromoendoscopy with targeted biopsies; risk-stratified surveillance intervals

**Statement 3.6.1:** Screening colonoscopy offered 8 years after symptom onset to reassess extent and exclude dysplasia [EL5]. **Statement 3.6.2:** Proctitis-only patients need not enter regular surveillance [EL2]. **Statement 3.6.3:** PSC patients — annual surveillance from PSC diagnosis regardless of activity/extent/duration [EL3]. **Statement 3.6.6:** Chromoendoscopy with targeted biopsies increases dysplasia detection [EL2]; if white-light endoscopy used, random quadrantic biopsies every 10 cm + targeted biopsies [EL3]; high-definition endoscopy preferred [EL2].

**Risk-stratified intervals:** Lower risk (extensive colitis with mild inflammation, <50% involvement) → 5 years. Intermediate (extensive colitis with mild-moderate inflammation, FDR CRC >50 years) → 2-3 years. Higher risk (moderate-severe inflammation, FDR CRC <50 years, PSC, stricture in past 5 years, past dysplasia declining surgery) → yearly.

Chromoendoscopy is superior to white-light endoscopy independent of operator familiarity or HD availability. NBI and endomicroscopy not recommended. Dysplasia of any grade without visible lesion → urgent repeat chromoendoscopy by experienced endoscopist [EL5]; confirmed LGD in flat mucosa → repeat chromoendoscopy within 3 months [EL5]; all dysplasia confirmed by independent GI pathologist [EL5].

**Comparison against G1:** G1's surveillance (R2-R5) concludes chromoendoscopy is beneficial over SD-WLE but the benefit over HD-WLE is less clear (RR 1.42, 95% CI 0.80-2.52). ECCO takes a stronger pro-chromoendoscopy position ("independent of high-resolution endoscopy availability"). G1 recommends against NBI (R4: conditional, very low) — ECCO concurs. ECCO's risk-stratified interval framework is more detailed.

**My assessment:** The strongest ECCO/G1 divergence is ECCO's unequivocal chromoendoscopy endorsement regardless of HD availability. The risk-stratified surveillance table is clinically practical. Both guidelines concur on NBI rejection and 8-year initial screening.

### Claim 13: In pregnancy, IUS and unenhanced MRI are safe imaging modalities; endoscopy only for strong indications; gadolinium is contraindicated

**Statement 3.7.1:** IUS and abdominal MRI without gadolinium are safest in pregnancy regardless of trimester [EL5]. **Statement 3.7.2:** Endoscopy generally safe but only with strong indication and clear clinical benefit [EL3].

**Evidence summary:** Ray et al. (2016, 1,424,105 deliveries, Ontario): first-trimester MRI (n=1,737) — no increased risk of congenital anomalies, neoplasms, or vision/hearing loss. **Gadolinium during pregnancy:** increased risk of rheumatological/inflammatory/infiltrative skin conditions (adjusted HR 1.36) and stillbirth/neonatal death (adjusted RR 3.70) — contraindicated. 751 neonates exposed to 1.5T MRI in utero: no difference in birthweight or hearing impairment vs. 10,042 controls. Stern et al.: 9 pregnant women (7 CD) — unenhanced MRI identified active CD and complications in 4, informing management. X-ray exposure: risk of congenital malformation and childhood cancer (~6% per Gy); ≤50 mGy considered safe. CT confers 1.52-1.68 cGy. Radio-isotopes should be avoided.

**Endoscopy:** Left pelvic tilt/left lateral position to avoid vena cava compression. Pregnancy-appropriate drugs at minimum effective dose. Avoid over-sedation.

**Comparison against G1:** G1 does not address pregnancy. ECCO fills a significant gap.

**My assessment:** The gadolinium warning (RR 3.70 for stillbirth/neonatal death) is the most clinically important finding — every clinician should know this before ordering contrast-enhanced MRI in pregnancy. IUS is the ideal first-line imaging modality in pregnancy (no radiation, no contrast, widely available).

## Guideline Assessment

### Summary of All Statements by Chapter

| Chapter | Statements | EL Range | Key Themes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ch 1: Initial diagnosis | 1.1-1.13 (13) | EL1-EL5 | Composite diagnosis, FC, ileocolonoscopy + biopsies, histopathology, small bowel assessment (MRE/IUS/SBCE), UGI endoscopy, perianal evaluation |
| Ch 2: Monitoring | 2.1.1-2.4.3 (15) | EL1-EL5 | MH in UC/CD, transmural healing (MaRIA/IUS), FC monitoring model, therapy de-escalation, TDM, postoperative recurrence (Rutgeerts), pouch |
| Ch 3: Complications | 3.1.1-3.8.4 (16) | EL1-EL5 | Stricture detection, fistula/abscess, pouch complications, emergency, CRC surveillance, pregnancy, biliary EIM |

### Overall Guideline Strengths

- **Multimodality integration:** The only IBD guideline where endoscopy and radiology are co-equal throughout — every section addresses both perspectives.
- **IUS emphasis:** The most comprehensive IUS guidance in any IBD guideline. TRUST trial (243 patients, 50 centres) and METRIC trial data provide evidence depth unmatched elsewhere.
- **Transmural healing concept:** Introduces and defends transmural healing as an endpoint distinct from MH, with specific MaRIA and IUS parameters — a European conceptual advance.
- **Structured FC monitoring:** The four-phase FC model (Figure 2) is the most sophisticated non-invasive monitoring framework in any IBD guideline.
- **Histopathology detail:** Temporal evolution of UC microscopic features (basal plasmacytosis → crypt distortion at 2-4 weeks) is uniquely detailed.
- **Pregnancy and pouch sections:** Not addressed in comparable US diagnostic guidelines.
- **Honesty about limitations:** Admitting no technique determines fibrosis degree (3.1.1) and no single feature is diagnostic (1.7).

### Overall Guideline Weaknesses

- **No formal recommendation grading:** Oxford EL system describes evidence quality but doesn't provide actionable "should/should not" recommendations with explicit strength — less clinically directive than GRADE.
- **Fragmented with Part 2:** Scoring systems (CDEIS, SES-CD, Mayo, UCEIS, MaRIA, Lewis Score, CECDAI) and technical aspects are deferred to Part 2. Reader must consult a separate document.
- **No treatment linkage:** Unlike ACG guidelines integrating diagnosis and treatment, this stops at detection/classification.
- **IUS availability bias:** Assumes European-level IUS expertise. North American and resource-limited settings may find recommendations aspirational.

### Key ECCO vs. US Guideline Divergences

| Topic | ECCO 2019 | ACG 2018/2019 |
|---|---|---|
| IUS role | First-line, equal to MRE | Not discussed as primary modality |
| Transmural healing | Explicit endpoint with MRE/IUS criteria | Not discussed |
| Chromoendoscopy | Strongly endorsed regardless of HD | Conditional; insufficient for universal use if HD-WLE available |
| FC monitoring | Four-phase model with action/target ranges | Recommended as test; no structured model |
| Histopathology | Detailed temporal criteria | Minimal |
| Small bowel assessment | Mandatory for all new CD (EL2) | Recommended, less prescriptive |
| Radiologic scoring | MaRIA score detailed | Not discussed |
| Pregnancy imaging | Dedicated section | Not addressed in G1; not diagnostic focus in G2 |

### Strongest Section
**Chapter 2 (Monitoring), particularly the FC monitoring model (2.2) and transmural healing assessment (2.1).** These are the guideline's most original contributions — concepts that US guidelines have not incorporated at comparable depth.

### Weakest Section
**Upper GI and biliary EIM sections.** Upper GI (1.9) is EL5 throughout with sparse data. Biliary EIM (3.8) simply references the separate ECCO EIM guideline without diagnostic detail. Both are placeholders.

### Most Controversial Claim
**The mandatory small bowel assessment for all new CD patients (Statement 1.10, EL2).** While evidence-supported, this represents a higher standard than most real-world practice, particularly in settings without ready access to MRE or expert IUS. The 53.7% active small bowel CD with normal ileoscopy (Samuel et al.) is the key number — if it's true that over half of CD patients with normal ileoscopy have active small bowel disease, a normal ileocolonoscopy alone is an incomplete diagnostic evaluation.

### Key Take-Home Messages
1. **No single test diagnoses IBD** — composite assessment required (EL5, foundational principle).
2. **FC is the cornerstone of non-invasive monitoring** — detects relapse ~3 months before symptoms, correlates with endoscopic healing (r=0.96 in UC).
3. **All newly diagnosed CD patients need small bowel assessment** (EL2) — ileocolonoscopy alone insufficient; 53.7% have active small bowel CD with normal ileoscopy.
4. **MRI preferred over CT** for small bowel — comparable accuracy, no radiation. IUS is valid alternative (METRIC: MRE 80% vs. IUS 70% for disease extent).
5. **No imaging distinguishes inflammatory from fibrotic strictures** — the critical unmet need.
6. **MRI first-line for perianal fistula classification** (EL1) — combining any two of MRI/TRUS/EUA = 100% accuracy.
7. **Transmural healing is an emerging endpoint** beyond MH — MaRIA score and IUS wall thickness/vascularisation assess treatment response.
8. **Chromoendoscopy with targeted biopsies** for CRC surveillance — yearly (high risk), 2-3 years (intermediate), 5 years (low risk). PSC: annual from diagnosis.
9. **In pregnancy, IUS and unenhanced MRI are safe** — gadolinium contraindicated (RR 3.70 for stillbirth).
10. **Before withdrawing therapy, confirm objective remission** — relapse drops from 42% to 26% with confirmed endoscopic remission.
