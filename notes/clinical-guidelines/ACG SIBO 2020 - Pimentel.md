# ACG Clinical Guideline: Small Intestinal Bacterial Overgrowth (Pimentel et al., 2020)

The first formal clinical practice guideline on SIBO from the American College of Gastroenterology. Authored by the leading SIBO research group at Cedars-Sinai (Pimentel) with a GRADE methodologist (Long). This is THE key SIBO document: it defines diagnostic criteria, establishes breath test thresholds, and summarizes treatment evidence. All 6 recommendations are conditional with low or very low quality of evidence -- reflecting the state of SIBO research more than the quality of the guideline. 15 key concepts fill the GRADE gaps with expert consensus.

## Definition and Pathophysiology

### Claim 1: SIBO is a clinical syndrome defined by excessive small bowel bacteria causing GI symptoms -- not merely a laboratory finding

**Recommendation:** SIBO is defined as "the presence of excessive numbers of bacteria in the small bowel causing gastrointestinal symptoms." The guideline emphasizes that the definition implies BOTH measurable bacterial overgrowth AND resultant symptoms. Pathologic fermentation of nutrients that would ordinarily be completely absorbed in the small intestine leads to excess gas and bloating.

**GRADE rating:** Not graded (definitional). However, the guideline cites the North American Consensus (Rezaie et al., 2017) establishing >=10^3 CFU/mL on duodenal/jejunal aspirate as the culture threshold, and a rise in hydrogen >=20 ppm above baseline within 90 minutes of 75g glucose or 10g lactulose as the breath test threshold.

**Evidence summary:** The 10^3 threshold is based on collated normal-subject data showing healthy controls have <10^3 CFU/mL. The older 10^5 threshold was based on postsurgical blind loop patients and is too stringent for general SIBO. The 20 ppm / 90 min breath test threshold replaced the older "double peak" criterion on lactulose -- the first peak within 90 min is now considered sufficient, eliminating the requirement for a second colonic peak.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** The AGA Practice Update (Quigley 2020) agrees on the 10^3 CFU/mL culture threshold but is more skeptical about breath testing. The Rome Foundation Report (2017) discusses breath testing limitations at length and emphasizes that no single test is definitive. The BSG guidelines (2019) do not address SIBO diagnostic criteria directly.

**My assessment:** The definitional shift from "laboratory finding" to "clinical syndrome" is important and overdue. It means a positive breath test alone is not sufficient for diagnosis -- symptoms must be present. But this creates a circular problem: the symptoms of SIBO (bloating, gas, distension, abdominal pain) are nonspecific and overlap completely with IBS, functional dyspepsia, and other functional GI disorders. The guideline acknowledges this: "no single symptom can be specifically attributed to SIBO."

### Claim 2: Bloating is the most common symptom of SIBO; vitamin deficiencies are uncommon except in structural/surgical cases

**Recommendation:** Key Concept 1. "The most common symptom of SIBO is bloating." Key Concept 2: "Vitamin deficiencies in SIBO are not common and are usually seen in patients with an iatrogenic or structural abnormality of the bowel such as blind loop syndrome." Note: folate may be ELEVATED in SIBO as bacteria produce folate.

**GRADE rating:** Key concept (expert consensus, not GRADEable).

**Evidence summary:** Abdominal pain, bloating, gas, distension, flatulence, and diarrhea are prevalent in >2/3 of patients (references 19-21). Severe cases with steatorrhea, weight loss, anemia, and fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies are associated with extraordinary causes (postsurgical blind loop, scleroderma). The cited study by Jacobs et al. found no difference in symptom intensity, frequency, or duration between culture-positive and culture-negative patients undergoing antroduodenal manometry -- underscoring that symptoms alone cannot distinguish SIBO from other conditions.

**My assessment:** The elevation of folate in SIBO is a clinically useful pearl -- if a patient with bloating has HIGH serum folate (without supplementation), SIBO should be on the differential. The fact that even culture-positive vs culture-negative patients have indistinguishable symptoms is sobering and supports the guideline's emphasis on objective testing before treatment.

## Diagnosis of SIBO

### Claim 3: Glucose or lactulose hydrogen breath testing is suggested for SIBO diagnosis in IBS, suspected motility disorders, and post-surgical patients

**Recommendation 1:** "We suggest the use of breath testing (glucose hydrogen or lactulose hydrogen) for the diagnosis of SIBO in patients with IBS." Conditional recommendation, very low level of evidence.

**Recommendation 2:** "We suggest using glucose hydrogen or lactulose hydrogen breath tests for the diagnosis of SIBO in symptomatic patients with suspected motility disorders." Conditional recommendation, very low level of evidence.

**Recommendation 3:** "We suggest testing for SIBO using glucose hydrogen or lactulose hydrogen breath tests in symptomatic patients (abdominal pain, gas, bloating, and/or diarrhea) with previous luminal abdominal surgery." Conditional recommendation, very low level of evidence.

**Evidence summary:** Breath testing is inexpensive, noninvasive, and widely available. Sensitivity and specificity vs small bowel aspirate culture: lactulose sensitivity 31-68%, specificity 44-100%; glucose sensitivity 20-93%, specificity 30-86% (Khoshini et al. systematic review). The guideline acknowledges these wide ranges and the low quality of evidence. The glucose breath test may miss distal SIBO (glucose absorbed in proximal duodenum). The lactulose breath test may produce false positives from accelerated transit and colonic fermentation. Methodological standardization from the North American Consensus: 75g glucose or 10g lactulose, measure hydrogen AND methane, avoid antibiotics 4 weeks prior, avoid promotility agents/laxatives 1 week prior, low-fermentable diet day before, 8-12 hour fast, avoid smoking and physical exertion during test.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** The AGA Practice Update (2020) is more cautious, noting that breath tests "lack sensitivity and specificity" and that "no test can be considered the gold standard." The Rome Foundation Report (2017) provides the most detailed critique of breath testing methodology and emphasizes the need for standardized protocols. The North American Consensus (Rezaie 2017) provided the standardization that this ACG guideline adopts.

**My assessment:** All three recommendations are "conditional, very low evidence" -- the weakest possible GRADE rating. This is honest. The guideline is saying: "breath testing is the best noninvasive tool we have, but it's not great, and we're recommending it because the alternatives (small bowel aspirate) are invasive and impractical for widespread use." The wide sensitivity/specificity ranges reflect the fundamental problem: we're comparing an indirect test (breath gas) against an imperfect gold standard (culture of a single duodenal aspirate that may miss patchy overgrowth).

### Claim 4: Small bowel aspirate and culture with >=10^3 CFU/mL is the reference standard but is invasive, expensive, and not widely practical

**Recommendation:** Not a graded recommendation. Discussed as the reference standard with significant limitations.

**Evidence summary:** Standardized techniques for aseptic collection are lacking. Requires upper endoscopy with deep duodenal intubation, sterile collection into 3rd/4th portion of duodenum, immediate transfer to microbiology for aerobic/anaerobic culture with precise colony counts (not just "positive/negative"). Diagnostic agreement with breath testing is only ~65% (reference 20). 16S rRNA gene sequencing is emerging and has validated the 10^3 CFU/mL threshold by correlating with symptoms, breath testing, and sequencing results (reference 35, Leite et al.).

**Comparison against other guidelines:** The AGA Practice Update concurs: "Small bowel aspiration and culture is the most direct method... however, it is invasive, costly, and not standardized." The Rome Foundation Report emphasizes the same limitations and notes that culture captures only a fraction of small bowel microbiota (many organisms are unculturable).

**My assessment:** The ~65% agreement between aspirate culture and breath testing is the key number. If the "gold standard" and the practical test agree only 2/3 of the time, we have a measurement problem, not just a clinical one. The 16S sequencing data from Leite et al. (2019) is promising -- it independently validates the 10^3 threshold by showing correlation with symptoms and breath testing results -- but large-scale validation is pending.

### Claim 5: Methane-positive breath testing (>=10 ppm) indicates intestinal methanogen overgrowth (IMO), not bacterial SIBO -- a nomenclature distinction with clinical implications

**Recommendation 5:** "We suggest testing for methane using glucose or lactulose breath tests to diagnose the overgrowth of methane-producing organisms (IMO) in symptomatic patients with constipation." Conditional recommendation, very low level of evidence.

**Key Concept 7:** "The presence of excessive methane on breath testing does not indicate SIBO, since methanogens are not bacteria (they are archaea). A better term would be IMO."

**Key Concept 9:** "Constipation is associated with elevated levels of breath methane and stool M. smithii."

**Evidence summary:** Methanogens (primarily Methanobrevibacter smithii) are archaea, not bacteria. They use hydrogen as substrate to produce methane. They can overgrow in the colon as well as the small intestine -- the location is not determined by breath testing. The North American Consensus established >=10 ppm methane at any point during the test as diagnostic of methanogen colonization. The guideline cites the neomycin trials (references 104, 105) and the TARGET trials. The combination of rifaximin + neomycin showed 87% methane eradication vs 33% for neomycin alone and 28% for rifaximin alone.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** The AGA Practice Update uses "intestinal methanogen overgrowth" sporadically but does not formally adopt the IMO nomenclature. The Rome Foundation Report discusses methane in the context of constipation-predominant IBS but does not propose a separate nomenclature.

**My assessment:** The IMO nomenclature proposal is Pimentel's signature contribution to this guideline. It is logically coherent (archaea are not bacteria) and clinically useful (methane-positive patients are predominantly constipated and may need different treatment -- neomycin added to rifaximin). The resistance to adoption by other societies reflects the entrenchment of "SIBO" as a catch-all term and the practical reality that most clinicians test for both hydrogen and methane simultaneously and treat based on which gas is elevated.

## Conditions Associated with SIBO

### Claim 6: SIBO is almost always an epiphenomenon -- the underlying cause (motility disorder, structural abnormality, medication) must be identified to prevent recurrence

**Key Concept 14:** "A focus on prevention of SIBO is important to avoid the need for repeated courses of antibiotics. Treatment of the underlying cause represents the primary mode of prevention."

**Key Concept 3:** "The cause(s) of SIBO in patients are varied, and this may need to be determined in order to best prevent a recurrence of SIBO."

**Evidence summary:** The guideline lists protective mechanisms: gastric acid barrier, bile acids, antegrade peristalsis, the migrating motor complex (MMC) -- particularly phase III, which sweeps bacteria from the small bowel toward the colon, and the ileocecal valve preventing colonic reflux. Breakdown in any of these can cause SIBO. Associated conditions (Table 4) include: motility disorders (scleroderma, diabetes, gastroparesis, chronic intestinal pseudo-obstruction), structural abnormalities (small intestinal diverticula, strictures, blind loop, fistulae, adhesions), postsurgical states (gastrectomy, ileocecal valve resection, bariatric surgery), medications (opioids, anticholinergics, PPI -- controversial), and systemic conditions (cirrhosis, chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, immunodeficiency).

**Recommendation 4:** "We suggest against the use of breath testing for the diagnosis of SIBO in asymptomatic patients on PPIs." Conditional recommendation, very low level of evidence.

**My assessment:** This is the most clinically important non-treatment recommendation. The emphasis on identifying and treating the underlying cause -- rather than cycling antibiotics indefinitely -- is sound. The recommendation against testing asymptomatic PPI users is pragmatic: the association between PPIs and SIBO exists but is weak, and testing asymptomatic patients leads to overtreatment. The recurrence data (12.6% at 3 months, 27.5% at 6 months, 43.7% at 9 months post-treatment) underscores why prevention matters: nearly half of patients will have SIBO again within a year if the underlying cause is not addressed.

## Treatment of SIBO

### Claim 7: Antibiotics are the cornerstone of SIBO treatment; rifaximin has the best evidence with 61-78% efficacy

**Recommendation 6:** "We suggest the use of antibiotics in symptomatic patients with SIBO to eradicate overgrowth and resolve symptoms." Conditional recommendation, low level of evidence.

**Evidence summary:** The meta-analysis of 32 clinical trials (reference 91, Gatta & Scarpignato 2017) on rifaximin: 7 RCTs, 24 cohort studies, 1 crossover trial, 1,331 patients total. Overall intention-to-treat success rate: 70.8% (CI 61.4-78.2). Adverse reactions: 4.6%. Doses ranged from 600-1600 mg/day, duration 5-28 days. Only one study compared rifaximin to placebo. The guideline's suggested dose: rifaximin 550 mg TID.

Other antibiotics with supporting data:
- Ciprofloxacin 500 mg BID: 43-100% response (3 small trials)
- Metronidazole 250 mg TID: 43-87%
- Neomycin 500 mg BID: 33-55% (primarily for methane/IMO)
- Amoxicillin-clavulanate 875 mg BID: ~50%
- Tetracycline 250 mg QID: ~87.5% (one study, 24 patients)
- Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole DS BID: ~95% (one pediatric study)
- Norfloxacin 400 mg daily: 30-100%
- Doxycycline 100 mg daily-BID: no breath test follow-up data

**Recurrence data:** 12.6% at 3 months, 27.5% at 6 months, 43.7% at 9 months (reference 102, Lauritano et al.)

**Combination therapy for IMO:** Rifaximin + neomycin achieved 87% methane eradication vs 33% neomycin alone and 28% rifaximin alone (reference 105, Low et al.)

**Comparison against other guidelines:** The AGA Practice Update also recommends rifaximin as first-line but is more circumspect about systemic antibiotics. The Rome Foundation Report discusses the limitations of antibiotic trials in detail (heterogeneity, small sample sizes, lack of placebo controls). No other guideline provides the antibiotic efficacy table that this ACG guideline does.

**My assessment:** This is the most clinically useful section of the guideline. The antibiotic table (Table 5) is the reference that clinicians actually use. The 70.8% pooled rifaximin efficacy is modest but consistent across multiple trial designs. The 43.7% recurrence at 9 months is the number that should shape clinical expectations: SIBO treatment is often cyclical, not curative. The 87% methane eradication with rifaximin + neomycin combination is a strong signal for dual therapy in IMO patients with constipation.

### Claim 8: Dietary therapy for SIBO is based on reducing fermentable substrates; evidence is extrapolated from IBS

**Recommendation:** Not graded. Discussed as adjunctive therapy without formal recommendation.

**Evidence summary:** The dominant theme is reduction of fermentable products: low fiber, avoid alcohol sugars and fermentable sweeteners (sucralose), avoid prebiotics (inulin). The guideline cites a meta-analysis (reference 106) that found "very low quality evidence" for low FODMAP diets in IBS. However, it also cites mechanistic studies: daily hydrogen output is higher on high FODMAP diets (reference 107), and low FODMAP diets produce a small decrease in hydrogen production (reference 108, McIntosh et al.). The guideline does NOT make a formal dietary recommendation, reflecting the absence of SIBO-specific dietary trials.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** The AGA Practice Update is similarly noncommittal on diet. The Rome Foundation Report discusses diet in the context of functional GI disorders broadly. The gap between this guideline's dietary position and the clinical reality (where diet is central to SIBO management) is notable.

**My assessment:** This is the guideline's weakest section. The statement that "data on using diet for SIBO are principally extensions of the data from IBS" is accurate but incomplete. Pimentel's own LFE protocol (published in book form in 2022) is SIBO-specific but lacks published trial data. The guideline was published in January 2020, just before the explosion of interest in SIBO-specific diets. A 2024 update would likely have a much stronger dietary section.

### Claim 9: Probiotics have insufficient evidence for SIBO treatment; FMT carries risks and should not be used outside trials

**Key Concept 12:** "There is a lack of consistent data to support recommending specific probiotics in the treatment of SIBO."

**Key Concept 13:** "There is currently no basis for the use of fecal microbiota transplant in the treatment of SIBO."

**Evidence summary:** A meta-analysis (reference 112) found probiotics reduced hydrogen production (OR 1.61, CI 1.19-2.17) but studies were small and of poor quality. A controlled study (reference 23) suggested probiotics may CAUSE SIBO and D-lactic acidosis in some patients. For FMT: one study found recipients of stool from SIBO-positive donors had more GI symptoms post-FMT. An FDA warning about multidrug-resistant organisms transmitted via FMT was cited. A case report documented transmission of constipation and methane-positive breath test from donor to recipient.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** The AGA Practice Update is similarly cautious on probiotics and FMT. The Rome Foundation Report discusses probiotics in the context of IBS rather than SIBO specifically.

**My assessment:** The probiotic caution is well-justified -- the concept of treating bacterial overgrowth by adding more bacteria is physiologically counterintuitive, and the one controlled study showing probiotics may cause SIBO is concerning. The FMT warning is prescient given subsequent FDA safety alerts. These two key concepts are among the most clinically actionable in the entire guideline because they steer clinicians away from commonly requested but unproven interventions.

## Guideline Assessment

| Recommendation | Statement | Strength | Quality of Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | Breath testing for SIBO in IBS | Conditional | Very low |
| R2 | Breath testing for SIBO in motility disorders | Conditional | Very low |
| R3 | Breath testing for SIBO in post-surgical patients | Conditional | Very low |
| R4 | Against breath testing in asymptomatic PPI users | Conditional | Very low |
| R5 | Methane testing for IMO in constipation | Conditional | Very low |
| R6 | Antibiotics for SIBO treatment | Conditional | Low |

**Overall GRADE assessment:** Every single recommendation is conditional, and all but one have very low quality of evidence. R6 (antibiotics) is the only "low" rating. This is not a weakness of the guideline -- it is an honest reflection of the SIBO evidence base. The 15 key concepts effectively function as de facto recommendations where GRADE methodology cannot reach.

**Strongest section:** Treatment (R6, Table 5, recurrence data). Provides the specific, actionable guidance clinicians need.

**Weakest section:** Diet. Acknowledges the mechanistic rationale but provides no formal recommendation, leaving the most commonly asked patient question ("what should I eat?") unanswered.

**Most controversial claim:** The IMO nomenclature proposal (methanogens are not bacteria, so "SIBO" is the wrong term). Logically sound but not adopted by other societies.

**Cross-reference priority:** Compare directly with G4 (AGA SIBO 2020) -- the two major US gastroenterology societies' SIBO guidance issued in the same year. Points of agreement (rifaximin first-line, breath test limitations) and divergence (AGA is more skeptical of breath testing, less prescriptive on antibiotic choice) should be mapped.
