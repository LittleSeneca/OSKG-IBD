# ACG Clinical Guideline: Management of Crohn's Disease in Adults (Lichtenstein et al., 2018)

The third iteration of the ACG practice guideline on Crohn's disease management, replacing the 2009 version. Authored by a panel of six leading US IBD experts with a GRADE methodologist (Gerson). The guideline contains **60 numbered GRADE recommendations** spanning diagnosis, disease modifiers, medical therapy by severity (mild-moderate, moderate-severe, severe/fulminant), fistulizing disease, maintenance therapy, postoperative prevention, and surgical referral. Additionally, **53 Summary Statements** (unnumbered, not GRADE-rated) cover clinical features, natural history, diagnosis, and disease monitoring where trial data are sparse. This is the foundational US guideline against which subsequent AGA (2021) and ECCO (2020) guidelines are measured.

## Scope and Methodology

The guideline used Ovid MEDLINE (1946-2018), EMBASE (1988-2018), and SCOPUS (1980-2018). Evidence was preferentially drawn from meta-analyses, systematic reviews, clinical trials, and prior guidelines. The GRADE system was applied: strength rated "strong" (desirable effects clearly outweigh undesirable) or "conditional" (uncertainty about trade-offs), and quality of evidence rated high, moderate, low, or very low. Of the 60 recommendations: 38 are strong, 22 are conditional. Highest-quality evidence (high) supports 5 recommendations (all biologic-related); moderate quality supports 26; low supports 21; very low supports 8.

**Key methodological note:** The guideline explicitly states that therapeutic drug monitoring was "beyond the scope" of this guideline, referencing the 2017 AGA TDM guideline instead. Similarly, health maintenance (vaccinations, bone health, cancer screening) is largely deferred to the separate ACG Preventive Care in IBD guideline (Farraye et al., 2017).

## Clinical Features and Natural History

### Claim 1: Crohn's disease is a chronic, progressive, destructive disease — only 20-30% have an indolent course

**Recommendation:** Summary Statements 4, 6-8. Not GRADE-rated.

**Evidence summary:** The majority of patients (70-80%) will develop intestinal complications (stricture, fistula, abscess) or require surgery. At diagnosis, 56-81% have inflammatory (B1) disease behavior; 5-25% present with stricturing (B2) or penetrating (B3) disease. Among those with initial B1 behavior, cumulative risk of developing an intestinal complication is 51% at 20 years (Olmsted County data). The Lémann index quantifies cumulative bowel damage. Median cumulative bowel resection length was 64 cm in patients with ≥1 resection.

**High-risk features for progression:** young age at diagnosis, initial extensive bowel involvement, ileal/ileocolonic involvement, perianal/severe rectal disease, penetrating or stenosis phenotype at diagnosis, visceral adiposity.

**Natural history key statistics:**
- 10-year cumulative risk of major abdominal surgery: 40-55% (possibly decreased to ~30% in biologic era)
- 10-year risk of second resection: ~35% (possibly ~30% in recent studies)
- 5-year symptomatic postoperative recurrence: ~50%
- Standardized mortality ratio: 1.4 (slightly increased, particularly GI disease and GI cancer)
- Up to 80% hospitalized at some point; annual hospitalization rate decreases over time
- Perianal fistulizing disease in ~25% of patients (cumulative risk 26% at 20 years)

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G6 (AGA 2021) and G8 (ECCO 2020) both acknowledge the progressive nature but G8 places greater emphasis on early aggressive therapy to alter natural history. The ACG 2018 guideline's natural history data is pre-biologic-era-heavy; newer population-based data in G6 may show lower surgery rates.

**My assessment:** The natural history data is the most important section for understanding *why* treatment matters. The 51% complication rate at 20 years for initially inflammatory disease is the number that justifies early effective therapy. However, the biologic-era surgery risk reduction data is noted only tentatively ("may have decreased to 30%") — later guidelines with more mature data should strengthen this claim.

### Claim 2: Symptoms do not correlate with active inflammation; objective assessment is mandatory

**Recommendation:** Summary Statement 9. "Symptoms of Crohn's disease do not correlate well with the presence of active inflammation, and therefore should not be the sole guide for therapy."

**Evidence summary:** The guideline explicitly cautions against treating symptoms alone. Objective evaluation by endoscopy or cross-sectional imaging should be undertaken periodically. This principle underlies the modern "treat-to-target" approach: mucosal healing (absence of ulceration on endoscopy) is a goal of therapy. Endoscopic scoring systems include CDEIS and SES-CD. Improvement in radiologic parameters on CTE/MRE correlates with better clinical outcomes (fewer hospitalizations, surgeries, steroid use). The guideline references the STRIDE consensus (Peyrin-Biroulet 2015) on selecting therapeutic targets.

**My assessment:** This is the most clinically transformative concept in the guideline. The disconnect between symptoms and mucosal inflammation explains why corticosteroid tapering fails in so many patients (symptoms resolve but mucosa doesn't heal) and why objective monitoring is essential. The guideline's consistent return to this theme throughout the treatment sections reinforces its importance.

## Diagnosis

### Claim 3: Fecal calprotectin is a helpful test to differentiate IBD from IBS (R1)

**Recommendation 1:** "Fecal calprotectin is a helpful test that should be considered to help differentiate the presence of IBD from irritable bowel syndrome." **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Fecal calprotectin (a calcium-binding protein derived from neutrophils) and fecal lactoferrin serve as noninvasive markers of intestinal inflammation. Multiple studies demonstrate utility in differentiating IBD from IBS (references 38-42). Additional uses: monitoring postoperative recurrence (calprotectin >100 μg/g indicates endoscopic recurrence with ~89% sensitivity), predicting relapse after anti-TNF withdrawal (calprotectin >160 μg/g: sensitivity 91.7%, specificity 82.9%).

**Initial laboratory evaluation (Summary Statements 19-20):** Should include evaluation for inflammation (CRP, ESR), anemia, dehydration, malnutrition. Stool testing should include fecal pathogens, *C. difficile* testing, and may include fecal calprotectin. CRP has a short half-life (19h) making it useful for monitoring; however, up to 40% of IBD patients with mild inflammation may have normal CRP/ESR.

**Serologic and genetic testing (Summary Statements 21-23):** Routine use of serologic markers (anti-glycan antibodies, etc.) for diagnosis is not indicated (low sensitivity). Genetic testing is not indicated for diagnosis — >200 IBD-associated loci and >71 CD susceptibility loci exist (NOD2, IL23R, ATG16L1), but no single variant has high enough frequency for diagnostic utility. NOD2 variants predict complicated disease behavior (ileal involvement, stenosis, penetrating disease, need for surgery) but testing remains a research tool.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G6 (AGA 2021) also endorses fecal calprotectin for diagnosis and monitoring. G8 (ECCO 2020) provides more detailed cut-off recommendations. The ACG guideline is notably restrained on genetic/serologic testing — even more so than G6.

**My assessment:** This is one of the few "strong, moderate" recommendations in the diagnostic section. The high sensitivity/specificity for IBD vs. IBS differentiation makes fecal calprotectin a high-value, low-cost triage tool. The guideline appropriately cautions against over-reliance on serologic and genetic panels that are commercially marketed but lack clinical validation for diagnosis.

### Claim 4: Ileocolonoscopy with biopsies is essential for diagnosis; small bowel imaging is required for complete evaluation

**Recommendation:** Summary Statements 24-35. Not GRADE-rated.

**Evidence summary:**
- **Ileocolonoscopy with biopsies** (Summary 24-25): Should be performed in all patients with suspected CD. Disease distribution and severity should be documented at diagnosis. Biopsies of uninvolved mucosa are recommended to identify extent of histologic disease. Granulomas are present in only a minority (up to 33%).
- **Upper endoscopy** (Summary 26): Only in patients with upper GI signs/symptoms. Asymptomatic CD patients may have mild endoscopic (64%) and histologic (70%) upper GI changes, but these have no clinical significance in adults.
- **Video capsule endoscopy** (Summary 27-28): Useful adjunct when high index of suspicion for small bowel CD. Superior to small bowel barium studies, CTE, and ileocolonoscopy (incremental diagnostic yield of 32%, 47%, and 22%, respectively). High negative predictive value (96%). Capsule retention risk: 0-5.4% in suspected CD; use patency capsule or imaging first in patients with obstructive symptoms.
- **Deep enteroscopy** (Summary 29): Not routine — reserved for when tissue sampling or therapeutic intervention (stricture dilation) is needed.
- **Cross-sectional imaging** (Summary 30-35): Small bowel imaging should be part of initial diagnostic workup. CTE and MRE have comparable sensitivity (~90% for detecting CD lesions). MRE preferred in patients <35 years and those needing serial exams, due to absence of radiation. MRI pelvis and/or endoscopic ultrasound for characterizing perianal disease (>90% accuracy).

**My assessment:** The diagnostic algorithm is well-reasoned: start with ileocolonoscopy + small bowel imaging, add capsule endoscopy if high suspicion persists. The guideline correctly cautions against capsule endoscopy without patency assessment in obstructive symptoms — capsule retention requiring surgery is a preventable catastrophe. The preference for MRE over CTE in young patients is an important radiation stewardship principle. Missing: the guideline doesn't provide a formal diagnostic algorithm diagram (ECCO 2020 does).

## Colorectal Cancer Surveillance

### Claim 5: Chromoendoscopy increases dysplasia detection over standard-definition white light, but may not be superior to high-definition white light (R2-R5)

**Recommendation 2:** In patients at high risk for colorectal neoplasia (personal history of dysplasia, PSC), chromoendoscopy should be used — it may increase diagnostic yield vs. standard-definition white light endoscopy. **Conditional recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 3:** For surveillance colonoscopy, there is insufficient evidence to recommend *universal* chromoendoscopy if the endoscopist has access to high-definition white light endoscopy. **Conditional recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 4:** Narrow-band imaging should NOT be used during colorectal neoplasia surveillance. **Conditional recommendation, very low level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 5:** Endoscopists sufficiently trained in chromoendoscopy may forgo random surveillance biopsies and rely on targeted biopsies alone. **Conditional recommendation, very low level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** A 2017 systematic review (10 RCTs, 1,500 patients) found chromoendoscopy significantly associated with higher dysplasia detection vs. other techniques (RR 1.37), but this was only significant vs. standard-definition white light (RR 2.12) — not vs. high-definition white light (RR 1.42, 95% CI 0.80-2.52). Chromoendoscopy adds ~9 minutes to procedural time. Random biopsies have extremely low yield (0.1-0.2% per biopsy) vs. targeted biopsies (80-90% of dysplastic lesions detected by targeted biopsies).

**Intestinal malignancy risk (Summary Statements 17-18):** Patients with colonic involvement have increased colorectal cancer risk (risk factors: disease duration, extent, PSC, family history, severity of ongoing inflammation). Small bowel CD carries markedly elevated relative risk of small bowel adenocarcinoma (>18-fold), though absolute risk remains low (~0.3/1,000 patient-years). Surveillance colonoscopy is recommended after 8 years of disease with ≥30% colonic involvement; PSC patients should start surveillance at diagnosis.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** The SCENIC international consensus (Laine 2015, referenced) provides more detailed surveillance recommendations. G8 (ECCO 2020) is more assertive on chromoendoscopy. G6 (AGA 2021) surveillance recommendations are more aligned with SCENIC.

**My assessment:** The nuanced recommendation on chromoendoscopy is honest — it helps over SD white light but the incremental benefit over HD white light is not clearly proven. The narrow-band imaging recommendation against use is definitive: two RCTs showed no benefit. The move toward targeted-only biopsies (without random biopsies) is the biggest practical change for endoscopists and is well-supported by the data.

## Disease Modifiers

### Claim 6: NSAIDs may exacerbate disease and should be avoided; smoking cessation is critical (R6-R7)

**Recommendation 6:** NSAIDs may exacerbate disease activity and should be avoided when possible. **Strong recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** NSAIDs damage small intestinal mucosa (ulcerations, erosions, webs) and increase mucosal permeability. Non-selective NSAIDs (naproxen, nabumetone) caused 17-28% relapse rate within 9 days in quiescent IBD (Takeuchi 2006). Recent NSAID use associated with increased emergency hospital admissions. Selective COX-2 inhibitors have not been shown to exacerbate UC in short-term studies, but CD-specific data are lacking.

**Recommendation 7:** Cigarette smoking exacerbates disease activity and accelerates disease recurrence; active smoking cessation programs should be encouraged. **Strong recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Smoking is associated with increased surgical intervention, hospitalizations, peripheral arthritis, penetrating phenotype, and relapse after anti-TNF discontinuation. Smoking cessation decreases flares, steroid need, and immunomodulator requirement. Smoking is the only modifiable risk factor for postoperative recurrence.

**My assessment:** Both are "strong, low evidence" — the evidence quality is low because RCTs of NSAID avoidance and smoking cessation in CD are neither feasible nor ethical, but the observational signal is consistent and the recommendations are unequivocal. The NSAID recommendation is clinically challenging: what do you tell a CD patient with severe arthritis? The guideline doesn't fully address this, though it hints that COX-2 inhibitors may be safer (UC data only).

### Claim 7: Antibiotics should not be restricted solely to prevent CD flares; stress/depression assessment is part of comprehensive care (R8-R9)

**Recommendation 8:** Usage of antibiotics should NOT be restricted in CD patients in order to prevent disease flares. **Conditional recommendation, very low level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Paradoxically, a case-crossover study from the General Practice Research Database found antibiotic exposure within 60 days was associated with *reduced* risk of flare. However, antibiotic-associated dysbiosis increases *C. difficile* risk, and IBD patients have higher carriage rates of toxigenic *C. difficile*. The guideline's practical point: don't withhold necessary antibiotics, but test for *C. difficile* if diarrhea worsens post-antibiotics.

**Recommendation 9:** Perceived stress, depression, and anxiety are common in IBD and lead to decreased health-related quality of life and lower adherence. Assessment and management of stress, depression, and anxiety should be included as part of comprehensive care. **Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Depression is associated with CD flares. Perceived stress has a strong relationship to IBD symptoms, though increased bowel symptoms are not always associated with increased inflammation. CD patients with major depression and anxiety have greater risk of surgery and higher healthcare utilization. Cognitive behavioral therapy reduced symptomatic disease activity in depressed children with CD vs. supportive listening.

**My assessment:** R9 is philosophically important — it elevates mental health to a GRADE recommendation level despite "very low" quality evidence. This reflects the panel's clinical experience that psychological comorbidity is underdiagnosed and undertreated in IBD. R8 (antibiotics) is practically useful: many clinicians unnecessarily restrict antibiotics in IBD patients out of fear of triggering a flare.

## Medical Therapy: Mild-to-Moderate Disease / Low-Risk Disease

### Claim 8: Oral mesalamine should NOT be used for active Crohn's disease; sulfasalazine is effective only for colonic disease (R10-R11)

**Recommendation 10:** Sulfasalazine (3-6 g/day) is effective for treating symptoms of colonic CD that is mild to moderately active. **Conditional recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 11:** Oral mesalamine has NOT consistently been demonstrated to be effective vs. placebo for induction of remission and achieving mucosal healing in active CD, and should NOT be used to treat patients with active Crohn's disease. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** The negative recommendation against mesalamine is one of the strongest in the guideline. Despite widespread use, multiple meta-analyses show no consistent benefit of mesalamine over placebo for induction or mucosal healing. Sulfasalazine is effective only in colonic/ileocolonic disease due to its colonic delivery mechanism. Topical 5-ASA (suppositories, enemas) has established efficacy in UC but limited benefit in CD.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G8 (ECCO 2020) similarly concludes mesalamine is ineffective for CD. G6 (AGA 2021) should agree. This recommendation directly contradicts common community practice patterns where mesalamine remains widely prescribed for CD.

**My assessment:** This is one of the most important "negative" recommendations in the guideline. The strong language ("should not be used") is notable — the panel is attempting to change entrenched practice. The "moderate" quality of evidence gives this real weight. The persistence of mesalamine prescribing for CD despite this recommendation reflects the gap between guideline evidence and real-world practice.

### Claim 9: Controlled ileal release budesonide 9 mg daily is first-line for mild-to-moderate ileocecal CD (R12)

**Recommendation 12:** Controlled ileal release budesonide at a dose of 9 mg once daily is effective and should be used for induction of symptomatic remission for patients with mild-to-moderate ileocecal Crohn's disease. **Strong recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** CIR budesonide is a pH-dependent ileal-release oral corticosteroid with high topical activity and low systemic bioavailability (~10-20%). Demonstrated effective in RCTs for active mild-to-moderate ileocecal CD (Greenberg 1994, Tremaine 2002). The lesser efficacy compared with conventional corticosteroids is balanced by the favorable safety profile (limited systemic exposure). The anatomic limitation is important: effective only for terminal ileum and right colon disease.

**My assessment:** Budesonide fills a genuine clinical niche — patients with mild ileocecal disease who need more than symptomatic therapy but don't yet warrant systemic steroids or biologics. The "low" evidence quality reflects the age and size of available trials, not the consistency of the effect. The delivery system limitation is a key practical point: budesonide won't work for jejunal, proximal ileal, or left-sided colonic disease.

### Claim 10: Metronidazole, ciprofloxacin, and antimycobacterial therapy should NOT be used as primary therapy for luminal CD (R13-R15)

**Recommendation 13:** Metronidazole is not more effective than placebo as therapy for luminal inflammatory Crohn's disease and should not be used as primary therapy. **Conditional recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 14:** Ciprofloxacin has shown similar efficacy to mesalamine in active luminal CD but has not been shown to be more effective than placebo to induce remission and should not be used as primary therapy. **Conditional recommendation, very low level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 15:** Antimycobacterial therapy has not been shown to be effective for induction or maintenance of remission or mucosal healing and should not be used as primary therapy. **Conditional recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Despite the rationale of targeting bacterial triggers, antibiotics have not demonstrated consistent benefit for luminal CD. Metronidazole was not superior to placebo (Sutherland 1991). Ciprofloxacin was equivalent to mesalamine (which itself is no better than placebo — see R11). Antimycobacterial therapy (multi-drug regimens targeting *M. avium paratuberculosis*) has been conclusively negative across multiple trials and meta-analyses. Antibiotics do have roles in pyogenic complications (abscesses) and postoperative prevention (see later sections).

**My assessment:** These three negative recommendations close the door on a therapeutic avenue that many patients and some clinicians continue to pursue. The antimycobacterial recommendation is particularly important — the MAP hypothesis drove decades of investigation and occasional off-label treatment. This guideline says: it's over, the evidence is negative.

### Claim 11: For low-risk patients, symptomatic treatment with careful observation is acceptable (R16)

**Recommendation 16:** For patients with low risk of progression, treatment of active symptoms with anti-diarrheals, other non-specific medications, and dietary manipulation, along with careful observation for inadequate symptom relief, worsening inflammation, or disease progression, is acceptable. **Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Some patients have genuinely mild, non-progressive disease. Dietary therapies (elemental, semi-elemental, defined diets) may reduce mucosal inflammation but benefits are not durable upon resumption of unrestricted diet. The key safeguard: providers must follow carefully for signs of disease worsening or progression, with the goal of normalizing objective indicators of mucosal inflammation.

**My assessment:** This is a pragmatic acknowledgment that not every CD patient needs biologics. The "very low" evidence quality is honest — there are no RCTs of "watchful waiting" vs. early biologic therapy. The critical caveat is "careful observation" with objective monitoring. This recommendation must be read in context: only for truly low-risk patients. High-risk features (young age, extensive disease, perianal involvement, penetrating phenotype) should push toward earlier effective therapy.

## Medical Therapy: Moderate-to-Severe Disease / Moderate-to-High-Risk Disease

### Claim 12: Oral corticosteroids are effective for short-term symptom relief but do not consistently achieve mucosal healing (R17-R18)

**Recommendation 17:** Oral corticosteroids are effective and can be used for short-term use in alleviating signs and symptoms of moderate to severely active Crohn's disease. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 18:** Conventional corticosteroids do not consistently achieve mucosal healing and should be used sparingly. **Weak (conditional) recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Prednisone equivalent doses of 40-60 mg/day (or 1 mg/kg/day) are effective for inducing symptomatic response. Typical taper: maintain initial dose 1-2 weeks, then taper by 5 mg weekly until 20 mg, then 2.5-5 mg weekly; total taper should not exceed 3 months. Despite symptomatic efficacy: ~20% of patients are steroid-refractory, ~33% become steroid-dependent. Corticosteroids are implicated in perforating complications and are relatively contraindicated in fistulizing disease. Nearly half of patients receiving steroids in the pre-biologic era were steroid-dependent, refractory, or required surgery within 1 year.

**Summary Statement 41:** Systemic corticosteroids are ineffective for maintenance therapy. Topical corticosteroids are of limited benefit in CD.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G6 (AGA 2021) and G8 (ECCO 2020) share the view that steroids are a bridge, not a destination. G8 in particular emphasizes early introduction of steroid-sparing agents. The ACG guideline's phrasing "should be used sparingly" (R18) is stronger than its "weak" GRADE rating suggests — the panel clearly wants to reduce steroid exposure.

**My assessment:** Corticosteroids remain the fastest-acting treatment for severe symptoms, but they are the clinical equivalent of a payday loan: quick relief with accumulating long-term costs. The 3-month taper limit is a practical benchmark that every clinician should track. The mismatch between symptom relief and mucosal healing is the fundamental problem — this is where the "symptoms ≠ inflammation" principle from the diagnosis section becomes directly actionable.

### Claim 13: Thiopurines are effective for steroid-sparing and maintenance but NOT for short-term induction; TPMT testing is recommended (R19-R22)

**Recommendation 19:** Azathioprine (1.5-2.5 mg/kg/day) and 6-mercaptopurine (0.75-1.5 mg/kg/day) are NOT more effective than placebo to induce short-term symptomatic remission and should NOT be used in this manner. **Strong recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 20:** Thiopurines are effective and should be considered for use for steroid-sparing. **Strong recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 21:** Azathioprine and 6-mercaptopurine are effective therapies and should be considered for maintenance of remission. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 22:** TPMT testing should be considered before initial use of azathioprine or 6-mercaptopurine. **Strong recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** The slow onset of thiopurines (8-12 weeks to clinical response) makes them unsuitable for acute induction. Their role is in steroid-sparing and maintenance. Adverse effects include allergic reactions, pancreatitis, myelosuppression, nausea, infections, hepatotoxicity, and malignancy (non-melanoma skin cancer, lymphoma). TPMT testing identifies patients at risk for severe myelosuppression. Routine monitoring of WBC and liver chemistries is required.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G6 (AGA 2021) provides more detailed guidance on thiopurine monitoring and the role of TPMT vs. NUDT15 testing. G8 (ECCO 2020) discusses thiopurine use more extensively in the context of combination therapy. The key divergence point: G6 and G8 both reflect the trend toward earlier biologic use and away from thiopurine monotherapy, which is only beginning to emerge in this 2018 guideline.

**My assessment:** This is a nuanced set of recommendations. R19 (don't use for induction) is critical: thiopurines take too long. R20-R21 (use for steroid-sparing and maintenance) reflect the established evidence base. R22 (TPMT testing) is "strong, low evidence" — the recommendation is strong because the consequences of missing TPMT deficiency are catastrophic, even though the evidence for universal testing is observational. The challenge for clinicians: this guideline was published just as the field was pivoting from thiopurine-first to biologic-first strategies.

### Claim 14: Methotrexate is effective for steroid-dependent CD (R23)

**Recommendation 23:** Methotrexate (up to 25 mg once weekly IM or SC) is effective and should be considered for use in alleviating signs and symptoms in patients with steroid-dependent Crohn's disease and for maintaining remission. **Conditional recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Parenteral methotrexate 25 mg/week is effective for induction of remission in steroid-dependent CD and for maintenance. After 4 months of steroid-free remission on 25 mg/week, the dose may be lowered to 15 mg/week. Oral methotrexate data for maintenance are lacking. Low-dose oral methotrexate (12.5 mg/week) is effective for preventing immunogenicity to biologic agents. Contraindicated in pregnancy (teratogenic); men should avoid conception during use and for 3 months after stopping.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G6 (AGA 2021) similarly supports MTX as a second-line immunomodulator. G8 (ECCO 2020) discusses MTX more extensively, including its role as combination therapy with anti-TNF agents. All guidelines agree on the pregnancy contraindication.

**My assessment:** MTX is underutilized relative to thiopurines, partly due to the injection requirement and pregnancy concerns, but partly due to less familiarity among gastroenterologists. The conditional recommendation (vs. strong for thiopurines) reflects the smaller evidence base. The dose reduction from 25 mg to 15 mg after 4 months is a practical pearl not widely known.

### Claim 15: Anti-TNF agents are effective for steroid-refractory and thiopurine-refractory CD; combination therapy with infliximab + azathioprine is superior to either alone (R24-R26)

**Recommendation 24:** Anti-TNF agents (infliximab, adalimumab, certolizumab pegol) should be used to treat CD that is resistant to treatment with corticosteroids. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 25:** Anti-TNF agents should be given for CD refractory to thiopurines or methotrexate. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 26:** Combination therapy of infliximab with immunomodulators (thiopurines) is more effective than treatment with either immunomodulators alone or infliximab alone in patients who are naive to those agents. **Strong recommendation, high level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** R26 is one of only five recommendations with "high" quality evidence, based on the landmark SONIC trial (Colombel 2010). Anti-TNF agents are rapid in onset (benefit often within 2 weeks), effective for induction of response, remission, and mucosal healing. They appear more effective when given earlier in the disease course (within 2 years of onset). Pre-treatment screening required: latent TB (tuberculin skin test ± IGRA), hepatitis B (HBsAg, HBsAb, HBcAb), and assessment for other latent infections (histoplasmosis, blastomycosis). Vaccination status should be reviewed and updated before initiation.

**Biosimilars (Summary Statements 43-44):** Biosimilar infliximab and adalimumab are effective for *de novo* induction and maintenance. Insufficient data exist to support switching between biosimilars in stable patients. A large phase 4 trial (NOR-SWITCH) showed non-inferiority of switching from originator to CT-P13.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G6 (AGA 2021) now recommends early biologic therapy in moderate-severe CD, which is a stronger position than this 2018 ACG guideline. G8 (ECCO 2020) provides detailed algorithms for biologic selection and sequencing. The SONIC trial data (R26) is the single most important evidence in CD therapeutics and is cited by all subsequent guidelines.

**My assessment:** R26 (combination therapy) is the crown jewel of the guideline — "high" quality evidence, strong recommendation. The SONIC trial showed that infliximab + azathioprine achieved steroid-free remission in 57% vs. 44% for infliximab alone and 30% for azathioprine alone at week 26. However, the guideline is cautious about *continuing* an immunomodulator when anti-TNF is added after immunomodulator failure — direct evidence is lacking. This is a major uncertainty that later guidelines attempt to address with TDM-based approaches.

### Claim 16: Vedolizumab and natalizumab are effective; natalizumab is restricted by PML risk (R27-R29)

**Recommendation 27:** For patients with moderately to severely active CD and objective evidence of active disease, anti-integrin therapy (vedolizumab) with or without an immunomodulator is more effective than placebo and should be considered for induction of symptomatic remission. **Strong recommendation, high level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 28:** Natalizumab is more effective than placebo and should be considered for induction of symptomatic response and remission in patients with active CD. **Strong recommendation, high level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 29:** Natalizumab should be used for maintenance of natalizumab-induced remission only if serum anti-JC virus antibody is negative. Testing should be repeated every 6 months and treatment stopped if positive. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Vedolizumab (gut-selective α4β7 integrin inhibitor): No cases of PML reported. May have slower onset than anti-TNF agents, particularly in anti-TNF-experienced patients. A network meta-analysis (Hazlewood 2015) suggested adalimumab or infliximab + azathioprine may be more effective than vedolizumab for induction and maintenance — but no head-to-head trials exist.

Natalizumab (non-selective α4 integrin inhibitor): Effective but carries PML risk as high as 1:100 in JC virus antibody-positive patients with prior immunosuppression and >2 years of use. Restricted to JC virus antibody-negative patients.

**My assessment:** Vedolizumab's "high" evidence rating reflects the GEMINI trials (Sandborn 2013). The slower onset is clinically relevant — don't expect vedolizumab to work as quickly as anti-TNF in a hospitalized patient. The positioning relative to anti-TNF agents is the key clinical question this guideline cannot answer: no head-to-head data exist. The guideline states vedolizumab can be used before anti-TNF failure ("not mandatory or advocated" that patients fail anti-TNF first), but the pragmatic reality is that most payers require anti-TNF failure first.

### Claim 17: Ustekinumab is effective for moderate-to-severe CD, including in anti-TNF failures and anti-TNF-naive patients (R30)

**Recommendation 30:** Ustekinumab should be given for moderate-to-severe CD patients who have failed previous treatment with corticosteroids, thiopurines, methotrexate, or anti-TNF inhibitors, or who have had no prior exposure to anti-TNF inhibitors. **Strong recommendation, high level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Based on the UNITI trials (Feagan 2016). Ustekinumab, an anti-p40 antibody inhibiting IL-12 and IL-23, demonstrated efficacy in both anti-TNF-failure and conventional therapy-failure populations. The psoriasis safety database shows an excellent safety profile without apparent increase in serious infections or malignancies. No head-to-head studies compare ustekinumab with vedolizumab or anti-TNF agents.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** This was the newest biologic at the time this guideline was published. G6 (AGA 2021) and G8 (ECCO 2020) have more mature positioning data for ustekinumab, including real-world evidence. The guideline's "can be used in anti-TNF-naive patients" statement was forward-looking for 2018.

**My assessment:** The UNITI data is solid (high quality evidence), but the positioning question remains unanswered by direct comparison trials. The favorable safety profile is a genuine differentiator, particularly for patients concerned about infection/malignancy risk. The guideline's inclusion of anti-TNF-naive patients in the indication was progressive — ustekinumab was initially positioned primarily as a second-line biologic.

### Claim 18: Cyclosporine, mycophenolate mofetil, and tacrolimus should NOT be used for Crohn's disease (R31)

**Recommendation 31:** Cyclosporine, mycophenolate mofetil, and tacrolimus should not be used for Crohn's disease. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Cyclosporine, despite efficacy in UC, has not been demonstrated to be effective in CD (two negative RCTs). Mycophenolate mofetil was inferior to azathioprine in a small RCT. Tacrolimus has only small uncontrolled case series with suggestion of benefit for luminal disease but insufficient evidence. Thalidomide may be effective in severe CD but should be used only in exceptional circumstances due to serious adverse effects (sedation, constipation, peripheral neuropathy, severe birth defects).

**My assessment:** This is a clinically important negative recommendation, particularly for hospital-based practice where cyclosporine is occasionally used as salvage therapy for severe UC. The guideline clearly states: what works in UC doesn't necessarily work in CD. The note on tacrolimus creates a minor inconsistency — R39 (later) recommends tacrolimus for short-term fistulizing disease treatment, so the molecule isn't entirely useless in CD, just not for luminal disease.

## Severe/Fulminant Disease

### Claim 19: IV corticosteroids are first-line for severe/fulminant disease; anti-TNF agents (especially infliximab) may be effective (R32-R34)

**Recommendation 32:** Intravenous corticosteroids should be used to treat severe or fulminant Crohn's disease. **Conditional recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 33:** Anti-TNF agents (infliximab, adalimumab, certolizumab pegol) can be considered to treat severely active Crohn's disease. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 34:** Infliximab may be administered to treat fulminant Crohn's disease. **Conditional recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Parenteral methylprednisolone 40-60 mg/day is effective for acute severe disease. Important caveats: pivotal anti-TNF trials excluded patients with CDAI >450 (the most severe patients). Clinical experience suggests some severely symptomatic patients respond. For fulminant disease, infliximab (weight-based dosing, generally producing higher drug exposure) may be effective, whereas adalimumab and certolizumab pegol efficacy in fulminant cases is less certain. Corticosteroids can also be used as pre-medication before infliximab infusion to reduce anti-drug antibody formation.

**My assessment:** The distinction between "severe" and "fulminant" disease is clinically important but poorly defined. The guideline's caution about adalimumab/certolizumab in fulminant disease reflects the fixed-dose limitation vs. infliximab's weight-based dosing. This is a practical clinical pearl: if you have a truly sick inpatient, infliximab is the anti-TNF of choice, both because of dosing flexibility and because it's the only anti-TNF studied in the inpatient setting.

## Fistulizing Crohn's Disease

### Claim 20: Infliximab is the best-studied agent for perianal fistulas; combination with seton placement and antibiotics improves outcomes (R35-R43)

**Recommendation 35:** Infliximab is effective and should be considered in treating perianal fistulas in Crohn's disease. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 36:** Infliximab may be effective and should be considered in treating enterocutaneous and rectovaginal fistulas in Crohn's disease. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 37:** Adalimumab and certolizumab pegol may be effective and should be considered in treating perianal fistulas. **Strong recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 38:** Thiopurines (azathioprine, 6-mercaptopurine) may be effective and should be considered in treating fistulizing CD. **Strong recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 39:** Tacrolimus can be administered for short-term treatment of perianal and cutaneous fistulas. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 40:** Antibiotics (imidazoles) may be effective and should be considered in treating simple perianal fistulas. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 41:** The addition of antibiotics to infliximab is more effective than infliximab alone. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 42:** Drainage of abscesses (surgically or percutaneously) should be undertaken before treatment of fistulizing CD with anti-TNF agents. **Conditional recommendation, very low level of evidence (expert opinion).**

**Recommendation 43:** Placement of setons increases the efficacy of infliximab and should be considered in treating perianal fistulas. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Fistulas occur in ~1/3 of CD patients, with perianal being the most common location. Classification: simple (distal to dentate line, single tract) vs. complex (transsphincteric, suprasphincteric, multiple tracts). Key management principles:
- **Exclude abscess before immunosuppression** (cross-sectional imaging). If present: drain first.
- **Simple fistulas:** May respond to fistulotomy or antibiotics alone. No treatment needed for asymptomatic simple fistulas.
- **Complex fistulas:** Combined medical-surgical approach. Seton placement + anti-TNF (infliximab best evidence). Seton + infliximab demonstrated better healing, longer closure duration, and lower recurrence.
- **Internal fistulas:** Enteroenteric fistulas may be asymptomatic and not require surgery. Major symptomatic fistulas (gastrocolic, coloduodenal) often require surgery if medical therapy fails.
- **Rectovaginal fistulas:** Medical therapy first (immunomodulator ± anti-TNF), then surgical repair (fistula excision + mucosal advancement flap) when mucosa is healed.

Infliximab is the only anti-TNF studied in a prospective RCT for fistula closure (Present 1999, Sands 2004 — ACCENT II). Adalimumab fistula data are from *post hoc* analyses and secondary endpoints. Certolizumab, vedolizumab, and ustekinumab fistula data are from *post hoc* analyses only — no controlled studies demonstrating unequivocal benefit.

**My assessment:** This is the most clinically detailed section of the guideline, reflecting the complexity of fistula management. The emphasis on combined medical-surgical care (R42-R43) is crucial: anti-TNF therapy without adequate source control can be dangerous. The evidence hierarchy for fistula efficacy is: infliximab (RCT-level) > adalimumab (secondary endpoint) > certolizumab/vedolizumab/ustekinumab (*post hoc* only). The tacrolimus recommendation (R39) for short-term use is notable — it's the one exception to R31's blanket rejection of calcineurin inhibitors for CD.

## Maintenance Therapy of Luminal Crohn's Disease

### Claim 21: Thiopurines or methotrexate should be started after corticosteroid-induced remission (R44-R45)

**Recommendation 44:** Once remission is induced with corticosteroids, a thiopurine or methotrexate should be considered. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 45:** Patients who are steroid-dependent should be started on thiopurines or methotrexate with or without anti-TNF therapy. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Three scenarios for thiopurine use after steroid induction: (1) at first corticosteroid course, (2) after repeated courses/steroid dependence, (3) as concomitant medication with anti-TNF. Scenario 1 is supported by pediatric data (Markowitz 2000) but an adult RCT (Cosnes 2013) could not demonstrate benefit in time in clinical remission. Scenario 2 (steroid-dependent patients) has the strongest evidence. Scenario 3 (combination with anti-TNF) is addressed in R50.

**My assessment:** The steroid-sparing imperative is clear: steroids work fast but are toxic, and the goal is to get patients off steroids and onto a sustainable maintenance strategy. The ambiguity about scenario 1 (start thiopurine at first steroid course in adults) reflects the Cosnes 2013 trial and the emerging evidence that early biologic therapy may outperform early thiopurine therapy. This is an area where G6 (AGA 2021) will likely have a more definitive position.

### Claim 22: 5-ASA and corticosteroids are NOT effective for maintenance; budesonide should not be used beyond 4 months (R46-R48)

**Recommendation 46:** Oral 5-aminosalicylic acid has not been demonstrated to be effective for maintenance of medically induced remission and is not recommended for long-term treatment. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 47:** Corticosteroids are not effective for maintenance of medically induced remission and should not be used for long-term treatment. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 48:** Budesonide should not be used to maintain remission of Crohn's disease beyond 4 months. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Mesalamine: 11 placebo-controlled trials with mixed results — 4 showed benefit, 7 did not. Five meta-analyses: therapeutic advantage <10%, NNT >15. The totality of evidence supports the negative recommendation. Corticosteroids: Meta-analysis of 403 patients showed no difference from placebo at 6, 12, and 24 months, with significantly more adverse events. Budesonide: Six RCTs — 12-month relapse rates of 40-74%, not significantly different from placebo. One study showed reduced relapse at 3 months (early benefit only). Four meta-analyses show mixed results with no consistent maintenance benefit.

**My assessment:** These three negative recommendations complete the therapeutic housecleaning: 5-ASA doesn't work for induction OR maintenance in CD; steroids don't work for maintenance; budesonide is only for induction. The strong GRADE ratings ("moderate" quality of evidence) make these some of the most definitive statements in CD therapeutics. Clinicians should feel confident stopping these agents in patients where they've been inappropriately continued.

### Claim 23: Anti-TNF agents maintain anti-TNF-induced remission; combination therapy reduces immunogenicity (R49-R50)

**Recommendation 49:** Anti-TNF therapy (infliximab, adalimumab, certolizumab pegol) should be used to maintain remission of anti-TNF-induced remission. **Strong recommendation, high level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 50:** Anti-TNF monotherapy is effective at maintaining anti-TNF-induced remission, but because of the potential for immunogenicity and loss of response, combination with azathioprine/6-MP or methotrexate should be considered. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Multiple RCTs (ACCENT I, CHARM, PRECiSE) demonstrate maintenance efficacy. A meta-analysis of 21 studies (5,356 patients) found anti-TNF therapy did not increase risk of serious infection, malignancy, or death vs. placebo (though lymphoma and TB were noted at higher rates in individual trials). Combination therapy reduces immunogenicity; however, some evidence (SONIC *post hoc* analysis) suggests that maintaining robust trough levels with monotherapy may achieve the same effect. Risks of combination therapy: higher lymphoma risk (especially hepatosplenic T-cell lymphoma in young males on thiopurine + anti-TNF), though no higher risk of opportunistic infections vs. either agent alone.

**My assessment:** R49 and R50 are the maintenance counterparts to R24-R26. The "high" evidence for anti-TNF maintenance is well-established. R50 introduces the therapeutic tension: combination therapy is more effective but carries more risk. The emergent TDM paradigm (referenced but not detailed in this guideline) offers a potential resolution: use combination therapy initially, then consider withdrawing the immunomodulator if trough levels are consistently adequate. This is where later guidelines will provide more granular guidance.

### Claim 24: Vedolizumab, natalizumab, and ustekinumab maintain their respective induced remissions (R51-R53)

**Recommendation 51:** Vedolizumab should be used for maintenance of vedolizumab-induced remission. **Conditional recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 52:** Natalizumab should be considered for maintaining natalizumab-induced remission of CD patients only if JC virus is negative. **Conditional recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 53:** Ustekinumab should be used for maintenance of ustekinumab-induced response. **Conditional recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** All three agents have positive maintenance RCT data (GEMINI 2 for vedolizumab, ENCORE/ENABLE for natalizumab, UNITI-IM for ustekinumab). Natalizumab's PML restriction is re-emphasized. Vedolizumab may be used as monotherapy but combination with an immunomodulator may be considered to reduce immunogenicity. Ustekinumab maintenance is subcutaneous (90 mg q8w).

**My assessment:** These conditional recommendations (vs. strong for anti-TNF maintenance) reflect the smaller evidence base and, in natalizumab's case, the safety restriction. The guideline doesn't address the practical question: should patients who achieve remission on one biologic be maintained indefinitely, or is there a role for treatment de-escalation? This is addressed in later guidelines.

## Postoperative Crohn's Disease

### Claim 25: Smoking cessation is the only modifiable risk factor; risk stratification guides prophylaxis intensity (R54)

**Recommendation 54:** All patients who have Crohn's disease should quit smoking. **Conditional recommendation, very low level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Three greatest risk factors for postoperative recurrence: (1) active tobacco smoking (especially women and heavy smokers), (2) penetrating disease (fistulas, abscesses, perforation), (3) two or more prior surgeries. Other risk factors: shorter diagnosis-to-surgery interval (<10 years), ileocolonic location, perianal fistula, more severe disease leading to surgery, longer resected segment, preoperative corticosteroid need. **Risk stratification:**
- **Low risk:** Non-smoker, no penetrating disease, first surgery → no prophylactic treatment, colonoscopy at 6 months post-op
- **Moderate risk:** Non-smoker, penetrating disease, first surgery, no prior medications → thiopurines ± metronidazole, colonoscopy at 6 months; add anti-TNF if endoscopic recurrence
- **High risk:** Prior resection within 10 years, or progression to surgery despite immunomodulator/biologic → postoperative anti-TNF ± immunomodulator, colonoscopy at 6 months

**My assessment:** The risk-stratified approach is clinically practical but based on expert consensus rather than randomized evidence comparing strategies. The "very low" evidence for R54 reflects that smoking cessation RCTs in postoperative CD don't exist — but the observational data is so consistent that the recommendation is still appropriate.

### Claim 26: Mesalamine is minimally effective; imidazole antibiotics have short-term benefit but poor tolerability (R55-R56)

**Recommendation 55:** Mesalamine is of limited benefit in preventing postoperative CD, but in addition to no treatment is an option for patients with an isolated ileal resection and no risk factors for recurrence. **Conditional recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 56:** Imidazole antibiotics (metronidazole and ornidazole) at doses between 1 and 2 g/day can be used after small intestinal resection to prevent recurrence. **Conditional recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Mesalamine: Cochrane analysis — reduced clinical recurrence (RR 0.76, NNT 12) and severe endoscopic recurrence (RR 0.50, NNT 8) vs. placebo. Meta-analysis of 11 RCTs (1,282 patients): reduced relapse (RR 0.86, NNT 13). Sulfasalazine specifically did not prevent relapse. Metronidazole (20 mg/kg/day): reduced severe endoscopic recurrence at 3 months and clinical recurrence at 1 year, but benefit lost when stopped. Nearly 50% intolerant to antibiotics. Combining metronidazole × 3 months + azathioprine × 12 months reduces endoscopic recurrence at 1 year vs. metronidazole alone.

**My assessment:** Mesalamine's postoperative role is marginal — NNT of 12-13 means treating many patients for minimal benefit. The guideline's endorsement of "no treatment" as an option for truly low-risk patients is more honest. Antibiotics have a real effect but intolerability limits practical use. The metronidazole + azathioprine bridge strategy (antibiotics for early protection while thiopurine kicks in) is an elegant clinical solution.

### Claim 27: Thiopurines prevent clinical and endoscopic recurrence but not severe endoscopic recurrence; anti-TNF agents are the most effective prophylaxis for high-risk patients (R57-R59)

**Recommendation 57:** Thiopurines may be used to prevent clinical and endoscopic recurrence and are more effective than mesalamine or placebo. However, they are NOT effective at preventing severe endoscopic recurrence. **Strong recommendation, moderate level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 58:** In high-risk patients, anti-TNF agents should be started within 4 weeks of surgery to prevent postoperative CD recurrence. **Conditional recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Recommendation 59:** Although data are lacking in postoperative CD, anti-TNF therapy should be combined with an immunomodulator to decrease immunogenicity and decrease loss of response. **Conditional recommendation, very low level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Thiopurines: Cochrane analysis — reduced clinical recurrence (RR 0.59, NNT 7) and severe endoscopic recurrence (RR 0.64, NNT 4) vs. placebo, superior to mesalamine. However, one meta-analysis found no significant reduction in clinical recurrence (RR 0.88, p=0.53 at year 1). Adverse events leading to withdrawal were higher with thiopurines (RR 2.57). Anti-TNF agents: Network meta-analysis of 21 controlled trials — anti-TNF monotherapy most effective intervention, reducing clinical relapse (RR 0.04) and endoscopic relapse (RR 0.01) vs. placebo. The PREVENT trial (Regueiro 2016) showed infliximab reduced endoscopic but not clinical recurrence. Authors recommend anti-TNF as first-line prophylaxis for high-risk patients.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** This is the area of greatest anticipated divergence with G6 (AGA 2021) and G8 (ECCO 2020). The AGA 2017 surgical resection guideline (Nguyen 2017, referenced) provides a more detailed algorithm. The PREVENT trial's negative primary endpoint (clinical recurrence) despite positive endoscopic findings is a major point of discussion.

**My assessment:** The postoperative section is the most rapidly evolving area in CD management. R58 (anti-TNF within 4 weeks) is based on limited data — primarily one small RCT (Regueiro 2009) and the international PREVENT trial. The "low" evidence quality is honest. The paradox of PREVENT (endoscopic benefit without clinical benefit) raises the question: does treating endoscopic recurrence prevent clinical recurrence, or are we overtreating? R59 (combination therapy in postoperative setting) is essentially extrapolation from luminal disease — there are no postoperative combination trials.

## Surgery

### Claim 28: Intra-abdominal abscess requires antibiotics and drainage; surgery is not a failure of medical therapy (R60)

**Recommendation 60:** An intra-abdominal abscess should be treated with antibiotics and a drainage procedure, either radiographically or surgically. **Conditional recommendation, low level of evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Intra-abdominal abscess typically results from a sinus tract or fistula, often with an associated stricture. Most CD abscesses are accessible to US- or CT-guided percutaneous drainage. After drainage, most patients benefit from delayed surgical resection (avoiding emergent surgery with diverting ostomy). Some patients, especially new diagnosis without stricturing disease, may respond to drainage + medical therapy alone. No studies directly compare percutaneous drainage + delayed surgery vs. medical therapy alone.

**Surgery indications (Summary Statements 51-53):** Intractable hemorrhage, perforation, persisting/recurrent obstruction, abscess, dysplasia/cancer, or medically refractory disease. Small bowel obstruction from fibrostenotic stricture is the most common indication. Stricturoplasty is a bowel-preserving option for patients at risk of short gut syndrome. Early surgical consultation is appropriate — "surgery is not considered to be a failure of medication."

**My assessment:** The "surgery is not a failure" statement is an important philosophical correction to the common narrative that the goal is always to avoid surgery. For some patients with limited ileocecal disease, early resection may be preferable to years of escalating medical therapy. The guideline doesn't provide formal guidance on when to choose surgery over medical therapy escalation — this remains an area of clinical judgment that later guidelines attempt to systematize.

## Therapeutic Drug Monitoring (Not a Formal Recommendation)

**Context:** TDM was "beyond the scope" of this guideline. The guideline references the 2017 AGA TDM guideline and endorses the approach of checking drug levels and anti-drug antibodies in patients with loss of response. Three failure scenarios:
1. **Mechanistic failure:** Therapeutic drug levels, no antibodies, active mucosal ulceration → switch to another class
2. **Non-immune pharmacokinetic failure:** Subtherapeutic trough, absent antibodies (rapid clearance due to high inflammatory burden) → dose escalation
3. **Immune-mediated failure:** Low/undetectable trough, high anti-drug antibodies → switch within class or to another class

Suggested minimal therapeutic trough targets: infliximab >7.5 μg/mL, adalimumab >5 μg/mL, certolizumab pegol >20 μg/mL (from the 2017 AGA guideline).

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G6 (AGA 2021) will have a dedicated TDM section building on the 2017 AGA guideline. G8 (ECCO 2020) provides detailed TDM algorithms. The ACG 2018 guideline's decision to defer TDM to a separate guideline was reasonable at the time but leaves a significant practical gap.

### Overall Guideline Strengths
- **Comprehensiveness:** 60 GRADE recommendations covering the full clinical spectrum
- **Honesty about evidence quality:** "Very low" and "low" used where appropriate — the panel doesn't inflate ratings
- **Negative recommendations:** R11 (no mesalamine), R46 (no 5-ASA maintenance), R47 (no steroid maintenance), R13-R15 (no antibiotics/antimycobacterials for luminal disease), R31 (no cyclosporine/MMF/tacrolimus for CD) — these change practice
- **Fistulizing disease section:** The most detailed clinical guidance in any CD guideline at the time, with combined medical-surgical approach
- **Risk-stratified postoperative approach:** Practical algorithm balancing treatment intensity against recurrence risk
- **SONIC data (R26):** One of only five "high" quality recommendations — correctly identifies the strongest evidence in CD therapeutics

### Overall Guideline Weaknesses
- **Therapeutic drug monitoring:** Explicitly deferred to a separate guideline — creates a practical gap for readers
- **Health maintenance:** Vaccinations, bone health, cancer screening largely deferred to the separate ACG Preventive Care guideline (Farraye 2017)
- **No treatment algorithm diagram:** Unlike ECCO guidelines, no visual algorithm for treatment sequencing by risk stratum
- **Special populations:** No sections on pregnancy, elderly, or perianal Crohn's specifically — notable omissions for a comprehensive guideline
- **Biologic positioning:** Cannot answer the most common clinical question: "which biologic first?" No head-to-head data exist, but the guideline could provide more structured decision-making frameworks
- **Limited diet/lifestyle guidance:** Diet discussed only briefly; no formal recommendations

### Strongest Section
**Fistulizing disease (R35-R43) and postoperative prevention (R54-R59).** These are the most clinically detailed and practically useful sections, with specific guidance on abscess drainage, seton timing, antibiotic combination, and risk-stratified prophylaxis.

### Weakest Section
**Diet and lifestyle.** The guideline acknowledges dietary therapies may have a role but provides no formal recommendation. This is the most common patient question and the guideline is virtually silent. The lack of special population sections (pregnancy, elderly) is also a gap.

### Most Controversial Claim
**R11 (mesalamine should not be used for active CD).** Despite being "strong, moderate evidence," this recommendation directly contradicts widespread community practice. Many gastroenterologists continue to prescribe mesalamine for CD. The persistence of this practice pattern despite multiple guidelines recommending against it is a case study in guideline implementation failure.

### Key Take-Home Messages
1. **CD is progressive** — 70-80% will develop complications. Treat to prevent, not just to relieve symptoms.
2. **Symptoms ≠ inflammation** — always confirm with objective testing before escalating therapy.
3. **Mesalamine doesn't work for CD** — stop prescribing it (strong, moderate evidence).
