# AGA Clinical Practice Update on SIBO: Expert Review (Quigley, Murray, Pimentel, 2020)

An AGA Clinical Practice Update -- distinct from a formal AGA guideline. It uses "Best Practice Advice" (BPA) statements rather than GRADE. Commissioned "to provide timely guidance on a topic of high clinical importance." Published the same year (2020) as the ACG SIBO guideline with Mark Pimentel as a co-author on BOTH documents. Despite the shared authorship, the AGA document is markedly more skeptical in tone and more historically grounded. Where the ACG guideline provides detailed antibiotic dosing tables with efficacy ranges, the AGA document emphasizes limitations, unknowns, and the inadequacy of the evidence base.

**Critical context:** This is not a competing guideline from a rival society. It is a complementary document from the same year, with overlapping authorship, but from a different institutional perspective. The differences in tone and emphasis reveal genuine tensions within the field, not merely inter-society rivalry.

## The Associated Conditions Table: AGA's organizational contribution

The AGA provides a comprehensive Table 1 organizing SIBO-associated conditions by pathophysiological mechanism. This is better organized than anything in the ACG guideline:

1. **Abnormal small intestinal motility:** diabetic autonomic neuropathy, scleroderma, amyloidosis, hypothyroidism, idiopathic pseudo-obstruction, acromegaly, gastroparesis, myotonic dystrophy, chronic opiate use, motility-suppressing drugs
2. **Anatomic abnormalities:** small intestinal diverticulosis, surgical alterations (Billroth II, bariatric, end-to-side anastomosis), strictures (Crohn's, radiation, surgical), blind loops, gastrocolic/jejunocolic fistula, ileocecal valve resection
3. **Hypochlorhydria:** postsurgical, long-term acid suppression
4. **Immune deficiency:** inherited (CVID), acquired (AIDS, severe malnutrition)
5. **Multifactorial:** chronic pancreatitis, celiac disease, diabetes mellitus, tropical sprue, Crohn's disease, cystic fibrosis, intestinal failure, radiation enteropathy, liver disease, end-stage renal disease, the elderly
6. **Relationship to SIBO unclear:** rosacea, interstitial cystitis, restless legs syndrome, Parkinson's disease, erosive esophagitis, severe obesity, irritable bowel syndrome

**My assessment:** This table is the most useful single reference in either SIBO guideline for clinical differential diagnosis. The "relationship unclear" category is particularly valuable -- it lists conditions where patients and alternative practitioners commonly invoke SIBO but where evidence is insufficient to establish a link. The placement of IBS in the "unclear" category is a notable statement in itself.

## Guideline Assessment

| BPA | Statement | Comparison to ACG SIBO |
|-----|-----------|----------------------|
| BPA 1 | Definition lacks precision and consistency | More skeptical than ACG |
| BPA 2 | Symptoms: bloating, diarrhea; steatorrhea in severe cases | Disagrees on bloating vs diarrhea primacy |
| BPA 3 | Insufficient evidence for fecal calprotectin | Not addressed in ACG |
| BPA 4 | Lab: elevated folate, B12 deficiency | Agrees |
| BPA 5 | Normal small bowel microbiome poorly understood | ACG acknowledges but less emphasis |
| BPA 6 | SIBO-IBS link remains controversial | More skeptical than ACG |
| BPA 7 | Treat underlying cause + nutrition + antibiotics | Agrees |
| BPA 8 | Rifaximin-IBS response: SIBO role needs confirmation | More cautious than ACG |
| BPA 9 | Limited antibiotic database; therapy is empiric | More cautious; no efficacy table |

**Strongest section:** The associated conditions table (Table 1) and the practical recurrence management guidance (no routine post-treatment breath testing, rotating antibiotic regimens).

**Weakest section:** Antibiotic guidance. Providing a list of antibiotics without comparative efficacy data is less clinically useful than the ACG guideline's Table 5.

**Key divergence from ACG SIBO:**
1. **Diarrhea vs bloating:** AGA says diarrhea is more predictive; ACG says bloating is most common. Genuine tension.
2. **SIBO-IBS certainty:** AGA calls it "controversial"; ACG says "little controversy." The same evidence, different framing.
3. **Antibiotic specificity:** ACG provides efficacy percentages; AGA provides only a drug list. ACG is more actionable.
4. **Calprotectin:** AGA addresses it (insufficient evidence); ACG does not mention it. AGA fills a gap.
5. **Insurance/practical:** AGA notes rifaximin isn't FDA-approved for SIBO; ACG omits this practical reality.
6. **Post-treatment testing:** AGA says don't retest if symptoms respond; ACG Key Concept 15 says "retesting after treatment may correlate with symptom improvement."

**The Pimentel paradox:** Mark Pimentel is a co-author on both documents. The differences in tone and emphasis between them cannot be attributed to different authorship -- they reflect different institutional framing by the two societies' review committees. The ACG guideline passed through a SIBO-focused committee; the AGA update passed through the broader Clinical Practice Updates Committee. This is a natural experiment in how institutional context shapes guideline output from the same evidence base.
