# BSG Consensus Guidelines on the Management of Inflammatory Bowel Disease in Adults (Lamb et al., 2019)

The first unified BSG guideline covering both ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease in a single document, replacing separate 2011 BSG guidelines. It is unique among major international IBD guidelines for its scale (168 recommendations across both diseases), its explicit integration of NICE technology appraisals and NHS service delivery frameworks, and its use of a modified eDelphi consensus process involving 81 multidisciplinary clinicians and patients. The guideline was developed over 2+ years, informed by a systematic review of 88,247 publications, and addresses pharmacological, non-pharmacological, surgical, nutritional, and service delivery interventions. It explicitly incorporates cost considerations, UK funding pathways, and primary care integration — aspects largely absent from North American guidelines.

---

## Scope and Methodology

The guideline was commissioned by the BSG Clinical Services and Standards Committee to replace the 2011 iteration. A Guideline Development Group (GDG) was convened with representation from eight stakeholder organizations: BSG, Association of Coloproctology of Great Britain and Ireland (ACPGBI), Royal College of Nursing (RCN), British Society of Paediatric Gastroenterology Hepatology and Nutrition (BSPGHAN), British Dietetic Association (BDA), British Society of Gastrointestinal and Abdominal Radiology (BSGAR), Primary Care Society for Gastroenterology (PCSG), and the patient charity Crohn's and Colitis UK.

**Systematic review and Delphi process:**
- 54 thematic questions generated, producing 414 associated clinical questions structured by PICO/PEO
- Systematic searches of Medline and EMBASE (March 2017, updated March 2018, with focused top-up searches to June 2019)
- 88,247 publications after deduplication included in the evidence base
- Modified eDelphi process: 81 clinicians and patients voted on draft statements using a custom-built online platform
- Statements achieving ≥80% consensus after two rounds were GRADE-assessed by two independent, blinded GDG members
- Statements with insufficient PICO/PEO conformity or low-quality evidence were presented as Good Practice Recommendations (GPRs)
- The 20 highest-priority research questions were patient-ranked via two electronic surveys (2,379 responses from Crohn's and Colitis UK)

**GRADE application:** Quality of evidence rated high, moderate, low, or very low. Strength of recommendation rated strong ("benefits clearly outweigh risks") or weak/conditional ("benefits, risks and burdens are conditional, closely balanced or uncertain"). The nomenclature distinguishes: "immunosuppressive drugs" (corticosteroids, thiopurines, methotrexate, calcineurin inhibitors, JAK inhibitors, biologics), "immunomodulators" (thiopurines and methotrexate), and "biologics" (monoclonal antibodies).

**NICE integration:** NICE technology appraisals for biologics (infliximab, adalimumab, golimumab, vedolizumab, ustekinumab) are explicitly referenced. Annual review of biologic therapy is recommended per NICE TA guidance. NICE Quality Standards for IBD mandate MDT management. Cost is explicitly listed as a factor in biologic choice. NICE-accredited Delphi methodology was used.

**Key methodological note:** Economic analyses were NOT performed. Health economics and drug costs were "not assessed as part of the guideline, although cost would be mentioned as an important consideration when there is a choice of treatments." This is less formal than NICE's own cost-effectiveness modeling but pragmatically acknowledges real-world NHS constraints.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** The methodology is more elaborate than ACG's (which used traditional GRADE without Delphi) and comparable to ECCO's consensus approach. The inclusion of patients as voting members of the eDelphi consensus group (not just as reviewers) was progressive for 2019. The 88,247-publication systematic review is the largest evidence base of any IBD guideline to date.

## Ulcerative Colitis

### Diagnosis, Assessment, and Treatment Targets

**Statement 1 (strong, very low):** When UC is diagnosed by sigmoidoscopy, full ileocolonoscopy is recommended to delineate disease extent, severity, and exclude Crohn's disease (100% agreement).

**Statement 2 (weak, very low):** Symptomatic remission combined with mucosal healing should be the treatment target (95.7% agreement). The guideline endorses the STRIDE paradigm: clinical remission (absence of rectal bleeding + normal bowel habit) combined with endoscopic remission (Mayo endoscopic subscore ≤1). Histological remission is acknowledged as associated with improved outcomes but not yet a validated treatment target due to lack of standardized definition and prospective validation. The UCEIS is endorsed as the preferred endoscopic severity score, with UCEIS ≥7 predicting need for colectomy in ASUC. The Mayo score remains the standard for clinical trials.

**My assessment:** The guideline's position on treatment targets is more cautious than subsequent STRIDE-II (2021), which elevated histological healing to an adjunctive measure. However, the BSG appropriately notes the evidence gap and the practical implications of treating asymptomatic patients based on endoscopic findings alone.

### Mild-to-Moderate UC: Induction Therapy

**Statement 3 (strong, high):** Mild-to-moderate UC should be managed with oral 5-ASA 2-3 g/day, with the addition of 5-ASA enemas rather than oral treatment alone (95.6% agreement). Combined oral + rectal 5-ASA is superior to monotherapy even in pancolitis (RR no remission 0.65, 95% CI 0.47-0.91).

**Statement 4 (weak, low):** Patients flaring on 5-ASA should receive dose escalation to 4-4.8 g/day orally alongside 5-ASA enemas (82.2% agreement). The ASCEND trials support higher doses for moderate disease; 4.8 g/day achieves more rapid symptom resolution and higher mucosal healing rates.

**Statement 5 (strong, very low):** Patients on 5-ASA should be monitored for nephrotoxicity: baseline renal function, repeat at 2-3 months, then annually (90.9% agreement). A UK study of 151 patients with 5-ASA-associated renal impairment found permanent renal replacement therapy needed in 15 patients. Median time to impairment was 3 years. Frequency estimated at 1 in 4,000 patient-years. An HLA-region genetic association was identified (p=1×10⁻⁷).

**Statement 12 (strong, high):** Mild-to-moderate ulcerative proctitis should be treated with 1 g 5-ASA suppository (100% agreement). Rectal 5-ASA is superior to rectal corticosteroids. Once-daily dosing at bedtime maximizes retention.

**Statement 6 (strong, high):** Patients failing or intolerant to 5-ASA should be treated with oral prednisolone. Topically-acting oral corticosteroids (budesonide MMX — strong, moderate-quality; beclomethasone dipropionate — weak, moderate-quality) are alternatives for those wishing to avoid systemic corticosteroids (93.2% agreement).

**Budesonide MMX:** Pooled analysis of CORE I and II trials: combined clinical + endoscopic remission 17.7% vs 6.2% placebo at week 8. Endoscopic healing 27.6% vs 17.1%. The 6 mg dose was not significantly better than placebo. Ileal-release budesonide is NOT effective in UC (inferior to placebo and 5-ASA in systematic review).

**Beclomethasone dipropionate:** A second-generation corticosteroid, 5 mg/day for 4 weeks then alternate weekly for 4 weeks, non-inferior to prednisolone but without significant difference in steroid-related adverse events. More expensive than prednisolone. Not available in the US — a key UK/European distinction.

**My assessment:** The sequential approach (5-ASA escalation → budesonide MMX → prednisolone) mirrors ACG 2019 but the inclusion of beclomethasone dipropionate as an option is unique to European guidelines. The absolute remission rate of budesonide MMX (17.7%) is modest — most patients with genuine 5-ASA failure will need systemic steroids.

### Moderate-to-Severe UC and Biologics

**Statement 7 (strong, high):** Moderate-to-severe UC should be treated with oral prednisolone 40 mg daily, weaning over 6-8 weeks (100% agreement). Patients not responding after 2 weeks should be considered for treatment escalation or admission. Doses above 40-60 mg/day provide no additional benefit. Approximately 50% experience short-term corticosteroid-related adverse events.

**Statement 9 (strong, high/moderate):** Patients requiring ≥2 corticosteroid courses in the past year, or who become corticosteroid-dependent or refractory, require escalation to thiopurines (moderate quality), anti-TNF therapy (high quality), vedolizumab (high quality), or tofacitinib (high quality). Choice should be determined by clinical factors, patient choice, cost, likely adherence, and local infusion capacity (96.6% agreement).

**Statement 10 (strong, high):** Vedolizumab can be used in chronic active UC for induction and maintenance, including in anti-TNF-naive patients.

**Statement 11 (strong, high):** Tofacitinib can be used in induction and maintenance of UC in patients where anti-TNF treatment has failed (91.1% agreement).

**Key divergence from ACG 2019:** The BSG limits tofacitinib to post-anti-TNF failure (Statement 11), whereas the ACG 2019 makes strong recommendations for tofacitinib in anti-TNF-naive patients. The BSG's more conservative positioning anticipated the post-marketing safety concerns that led to the FDA's 2021 JAK inhibitor black-box warning. This is one of the most important differences between the two 2019 guidelines.

**Biologic choice factors:** The guideline does not prescribe a fixed sequencing algorithm. Patient preference, cost, adherence, safety profile, and speed of response are all listed as determinants. The NHS context means NICE technology appraisals effectively determine access — a drug must be NICE-approved for the specific indication before NHS funding is available.

**Anti-TNF data:**
- Infliximab (ACT-1/2): Week 8 clinical response 67% (5 mg/kg) vs 33% placebo. Steroid-free remission 22% at week 30.
- Adalimumab (ULTRA 1/2): Week 8 remission 19-21% vs 9-11% placebo in anti-TNF-naive. Week 52 remission 22% vs 12% placebo.
- Golimumab (PURSUIT): 42.8% clinical response at week 6 in anti-TNF-naive patients.
- UC-SUCCESS: Infliximab + azathioprine combination superior to infliximab monotherapy (40% vs 22% steroid-free remission at week 16).

**Comparison against other guidelines:** The BSG's "all options on the table" approach (Statements 9-11 list thiopurines, anti-TNF, vedolizumab, and tofacitinib as co-equal options post-steroid failure) differs from ACG 2019's stronger recommendations for individual agents without comparative sequencing guidance. ECCO 2022 provides the most detailed algorithm. The BSG's explicit cost consideration reflects the single-payer NHS system.

### Maintenance Therapy for UC

**Statement 8 (strong, high):** Oral 5-ASA should be standard maintenance therapy. Choice of formulation should consider patient preference, adherence, and cost. Once-daily dosing is effective and may improve adherence (100% agreement). There is little to choose between formulations in terms of efficacy.

**Maintenance after advanced therapy:** Continue the agent that induced remission. Corticosteroids are contraindicated for maintenance (Statement 38 for CD, extrapolated to UC context). 5-ASA has no proven additive maintenance benefit when patients are on biologics.

**Stopping 5-ASA in prolonged remission:** Patients with >2 years of clinical, endoscopic, and histological remission on 5-ASA have lower relapse risk regardless of treatment. A study randomizing such patients to mesalazine 1.2 g/day vs placebo found no significant difference (18% vs 26% relapse at 1 year). However, the potential chemopreventive benefit of 5-ASA for colorectal cancer complicates the decision to stop.

### Acute Severe Ulcerative Colitis (ASUC)

This is the most protocolized section of the guideline, reflecting the life-threatening nature of ASUC.

**Statement 15 (strong, low):** ASUC (modified Truelove and Witts: ≥6 bloody stools/day plus at least one of: temperature >37.8°C, pulse >90 bpm, Hb <105 g/L, or CRP >30 mg/L) requires hospital admission for intensive management (100% agreement).

**Statement 16 (strong, very low):** Urgent assessment includes blood tests (FBC, CRP, U&E, LFTs, magnesium), stool culture, *C. difficile* assay, abdominal X-ray or CT, and flexible sigmoidoscopy (97.9% agreement).

**Statement 17 (strong, high/moderate):** IV methylprednisolone 60 mg daily or hydrocortisone 100 mg 6-hourly, plus prophylactic low-molecular weight heparin (97.8% agreement). VTE risk is 2-3 fold higher in hospitalized IBD patients.

**GPR 1:** Corticosteroid treatment should not be delayed pending stool culture and *C. difficile* assay results (95.6% agreement).

**Predicting steroid failure — the day 3 assessment:**
- **Oxford (Travis) index:** Stool frequency >8/day OR 3-8/day + CRP >45 mg/L → 85% risk of colectomy
- **Ho index:** Composite score of stool frequency, colonic dilatation ≥5.5 cm, albumin <30 g/L
- **Lindgren index:** Stool frequency + (0.14 × CRP) >8 → 72% risk
- **Gibson index:** CRP/albumin ratio >0.85 + stool frequency >3 → 74% risk
- **UCEIS ≥7:** Almost all patients require rescue therapy

The response plateau is at 3-5 days. Extending IV steroids beyond 7-10 days adds toxicity without benefit. Overall steroid response rate: 67% (meta-analysis of 1,991 patients). Mortality: 1%.

**Statement 18 (strong, high):** Patients failing IV steroids by day 3 should receive rescue therapy with IV infliximab or ciclosporin (for patients who have not previously failed thiopurines) (97.8% agreement).

**Ciclosporin:** 2 mg/kg/day IV (not 4 mg/kg — equivalent efficacy, fewer adverse events). Target trough 150-250 ng/mL. Responders convert to oral (twice the IV dose, divided twice daily, target trough 100-200 ng/mL). Oral ciclosporin continued for several months as bridging therapy. Long-term outcomes superior with thiopurine maintenance (40% → 66% colectomy-free survival). Major adverse events: nephrotoxicity (6.3%), seizures (3.6%), anaphylaxis (0.9%), death (1.8%).

**Infliximab:** 5 mg/kg at weeks 0, 2, and 6. Long-term follow-up of the Swedish trial: colectomy rate 50% (infliximab) vs 76% (placebo) at 3 years. Combination with azathioprine reduces immunogenicity and improves outcomes.

**Head-to-head evidence — CYSIF trial (Laharie 2012):** 115 patients randomized to ciclosporin or infliximab. Treatment failure at day 98: 60% (ciclosporin) vs 54% (infliximab) — not statistically different. Colectomy at 98 days: 17% vs 21%. No difference in colectomy-free survival at 1 and 5 years. Mucosal healing rates: 47% vs 45%.

**CONSTRUCT trial (2019):** 270 patients randomized. No difference in quality-adjusted survival, colectomy rates (29% vs 30% at 3 months; 35% vs 45% at 1 year), time to colectomy, serious adverse events, or death. Infliximab was associated with greater cost.

**Statement 19 (weak, low):** Patients treated with infliximab for ASUC should receive maintenance infliximab after discharge.

**My assessment:** The CYSIF and CONSTRUCT trials provide the strongest head-to-head evidence in UC therapeutics and justify genuine equipoise between infliximab and ciclosporin. However, real-world UK practice heavily favors infliximab due to provider familiarity and the simplicity of continuing it as maintenance. The guideline's algorithm (Figure 2) is the most clinically useful ASUC management framework of any guideline. The emphasis on day 3 surgical consultation for non-responders cannot be overstated — delayed surgery in ASUC increases postoperative mortality.

### Surgery and Pouchitis

**Statement 20 (strong, low):** ASUC patients with disease not controlled by medical therapy should undergo colectomy — surgery should not be delayed (97.9% agreement).

**Statement 21 (weak, low):** Surgical resection should be discussed with ASUC patients not responding to maximal medical therapy to avoid emergency colectomy and its complications. Laparoscopic colectomy is safe in experienced hands.

**Statement 22 (weak, very low):** Pouch surgery (restorative proctocolectomy with IPAA) should be discussed with UC patients requiring colectomy. A two- or three-stage procedure is appropriate depending on clinical status (95.7% agreement).

**Pouchitis management:**
- **Statement 23 (strong, moderate):** Acute pouchitis: 2-week course of ciprofloxacin or metronidazole (100% agreement)
- **Statement 24 (weak, low):** Chronic pouchitis: combination antibiotic therapy or prolonged courses
- **Statement 25:** Chronic refractory pouchitis: biologic therapy (anti-TNF, vedolizumab)
- **GPR 6:** Ileoanal pouch patients should be seen in a dedicated surgical/pouch clinic (97.7% agreement)

**Fertility after IPAA:** Statement 26 notes that restorative proctocolectomy with IPAA reduces female fertility. Laparoscopic approach may reduce this risk. Patients should receive counselling about fertility implications before surgery.

**My assessment:** The inclusion of pouchitis management is a strength of the BSG guideline. The ACG UC 2019 guideline notably omits pouchitis entirely — a significant gap for a document covering the full UC lifecycle. The BSG's surgical section strikes the right balance: surgery is framed as appropriate therapy rather than failure, staged procedures are emphasized for sick patients, and the fertility implications of IPAA are explicitly addressed (a counselling point frequently missed in surgical consent).

## Crohn's Disease

### Diagnosis and Imaging

**Statement 27 (weak, very low):** Adult CD patients do not routinely require upper GI endoscopy unless they have upper GI symptoms (91.1% agreement). Asymptomatic mild upper GI changes have no clinical significance in adults.

**Statement 28 (weak, moderate):** Cross-sectional imaging (MRI, CT, ultrasound) has largely replaced barium fluoroscopy. Emphasis on MR enterography and ultrasound to avoid ionizing radiation (97.9% agreement).

**Statement 29 (weak, very low):** No clear superiority of one cross-sectional modality over another for stricture diagnosis. Colonic/anastomotic strictures require endoscopy and biopsy to exclude malignancy (100% agreement).

**Statement 30 (weak, moderate):** Capsule endoscopy has greater sensitivity for mucosal small bowel CD than radiological imaging but minor changes may be insufficient in isolation to diagnose CD, particularly with recent NSAID use (90.9% agreement).

**Statement 31 (weak, low):** Capsule retention risk is low in suspected CD without obstructive symptoms. In known stricturing disease, patency capsule should precede capsule endoscopy (93.5% agreement). Retention risk: 3.6% in suspected CD, 8.2% in established CD, 2.7% after negative patency capsule or cross-sectional imaging.

### Mild-to-Moderate Ileocolonic CD

**Statement 32 (strong, high):** Mild-to-moderate ileocaecal CD: ileal-release budesonide 9 mg once daily for 8 weeks (100% agreement). As effective as prednisolone (40 mg tapering) in mild-moderate disease (CDAI <300) with significantly fewer side effects. In more severe disease (CDAI >300), budesonide is inferior to prednisolone (RR 0.52).

**Statement 33 (strong, high):** Active Crohn's colitis: 8-week course of systemic corticosteroids (100% agreement). Prednisolone 40 mg daily, tapering by 5 mg weekly.

**Statement 34 (weak, low):** Exclusive Enteral Nutrition (EEN) may be considered as an alternative to corticosteroids to induce remission in adults, with appropriate dietetic support. EEN is as effective as corticosteroids in paediatric CD (73% remission on intention-to-treat basis) but evidence is weaker in adults. Polymeric feeds are as effective as elemental feeds (Statement 35, strong, moderate). A minimum of 4-6 weeks is recommended; 10 days achieves symptomatic relief but mucosal healing takes up to 8 weeks.

**Statement 36 (weak, low):** Laparoscopic resection should be considered as an alternative to medical therapy for localized ileocaecal CD (97.3% agreement). The guideline explicitly frames surgery as a valid primary treatment option for limited ileocaecal disease — not just a last resort.

**Statement 42 (strong, moderate):** Mesalazine should NOT be used for induction or maintenance of remission in CD (97.7% agreement). This strong negative recommendation is consistent with ACG 2018.

**My assessment:** The prominence of EEN and the explicit surgical option for localized ileocaecal disease are two areas where the BSG guideline diverges from North American practice patterns. EEN is first-line in UK paediatric CD and is increasingly used in adults with appropriate dietetic support — though the guideline acknowledges that limited dietetic access within MDTs restricts uptake. The laparoscopic resection option (Statement 36) reflects the European tradition of earlier surgical intervention for limited disease, supported by the LIR!C trial and cost-effectiveness data.

### Moderate-to-Severe CD: Biologics and Immunomodulators

**Statement 37 (strong, high):** Moderate-to-severely active CD should be treated with systemic corticosteroids or biologic therapy. Early introduction of biologic therapy should be considered, particularly in patients with poor prognostic factors (95.6% agreement).

**Statement 38 (strong, high):** Corticosteroids should NOT be used as maintenance therapy (100% agreement).

**Statement 39 (strong, low/moderate):** Patients responding to prednisolone should have early introduction of thiopurines (low quality) or methotrexate (moderate quality) to minimize flare risk as prednisolone is withdrawn (93.3% agreement).

**Statement 40 (strong, low):** Azathioprine or mercaptopurine may be used for maintenance of remission in CD (100% agreement). Slow onset (8-12 weeks) makes them unsuitable for induction.

**Statement 41 (weak, moderate):** Methotrexate (≥15 mg weekly, subcutaneous preferred for bioavailability) may be used for maintenance (88.4% agreement). Oral methotrexate has inferior bioavailability at higher doses.

**Statement 43 (strong, very low):** Patients refractory to immunomodulators despite dose optimization should be considered for biologic therapy. Choice between anti-TNF, ustekinumab, and vedolizumab should be individualized — considering patient preference, cost, likely adherence, safety data, and speed of response (95.7% agreement).

**Statement 44 (strong, high):** Combination therapy of infliximab with a thiopurine is superior to infliximab monotherapy for induction and maintenance of remission — based on the SONIC trial (97.7% agreement).

**Statement 45 (weak, moderate):** Combination of infliximab with methotrexate may be used to reduce immunogenicity (90.5% agreement).

**PANTS study data:** The UK PANTS 3-year observational cohort (1,601 CD patients) showed immunogenicity rates at week 54 of 26% for originator infliximab and 28% for biosimilar (CT-P13). Immunomodulator co-therapy reduced immunogenicity risk (HR 0.37, p<0.0001). HLA-DQA1*05 carriers are at higher risk of anti-drug antibody formation — combination therapy is particularly beneficial in this subgroup.

**Adalimumab:** The evidence for combination therapy with adalimumab is weaker than for infliximab. The DIAMOND trial showed similar remission rates at weeks 26 and 52 for adalimumab monotherapy vs combination with azathioprine. However, combination therapy increases trough levels and reduces immunogenicity — the PANTS study showed HR 0.34 for immunogenicity reduction. The clinical benefit of combination with adalimumab may be longer-term.

**Choice of anti-TNF agent:** The guideline states there is "little to choose between adalimumab and infliximab in efficacy in luminal CD" — practical considerations (mode/frequency of administration) and the relative need for combination therapy drive choice. Certolizumab pegol is NOT licensed for IBD in the UK and is not discussed.

**Vedolizumab (Statement 46, strong, moderate):** Can be used for induction and maintenance in anti-TNF-naive and anti-TNF-exposed CD patients. Gut-selective mechanism. No PML cases reported. May have slower onset than anti-TNF agents.

**Ustekinumab (Statement 47, strong, high):** Can be used for induction and maintenance. Safety profile favorable — psoriasis registry data show serious infection rate of 0.93/100 patient-years vs 1.9-2.9 for anti-TNF therapies. Infusion reactions in 3.4%.

**Statement 48 (strong, high):** Choice of biologic after anti-TNF failure should be based on clinical factors, drug levels and anti-drug antibody status (TDM), and patient preference.

**Statement 49 (weak, very low):** Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may be considered for carefully selected patients with severe refractory CD failing all conventional therapies. Leucocyte apheresis is not recommended (Statement 50).

**My assessment:** The BSG's approach to biologic sequencing is pragmatic: list all options, factor in cost (NHS-constrained), and emphasize individualization. This differs from the AGA's more prescriptive conditional recommendations and reflects the reality that NICE technology appraisals, not guideline authors, ultimately determine which biologics are funded at which line of therapy in the NHS. The PANTS study data (a UK-specific contribution) provide the most robust real-world immunogenicity evidence available at the time.

### Perianal Crohn's Disease

The perianal CD section emphasizes combined medical-surgical management:

- **Assessment:** EUA (examination under anaesthesia), MRI pelvis, and endoscopy. All three modalities together provide optimal accuracy.
- **Seton insertion (Statement 51):** Complex perianal fistulae should have seton drainage before anti-TNF therapy.
- **Infliximab (Statement 52, strong, moderate):** Best evidence for fistula closure (ACCENT II trial). Combination of seton + infliximab yields better outcomes than either alone.
- **Adalimumab (Statement 53, weak, low):** Fistula data from post-hoc analyses — weaker evidence than infliximab.
- **Vedolizumab and ustekinumab (Statement 54):** Limited data; fistula outcomes from post-hoc analyses only.
- **Surgical options:** Fistulotomy for simple low fistulae; fistulectomy + advancement flap for complex fistulae; LIFT procedure; defunctioning stoma for severe refractory disease.
- **Stem cell therapy (Statement 55, weak, low):** Allogeneic adipose-derived stem cells (darvadstrocel) may be considered for complex perianal fistulae refractory to conventional therapy — a novel option not available in most other guidelines at the time.

### Postoperative Crohn's Disease

**Statement 69 (strong, moderate):** All patients smoking after intestinal resection should be actively encouraged to stop (100% agreement). Smoking is the strongest modifiable risk factor for postoperative recurrence (OR 2.5 for endoscopic, 2.0 for clinical recurrence).

**Statement 70 (weak, low):** Ileocolonoscopy at 6 months post-surgery to assess the neoterminal ileum — treatment escalation if Rutgeerts i2 or above (89.2% agreement). Faecal calprotectin and/or cross-sectional imaging may be used if ileocolonoscopy is not possible.

**Statement 71 (weak, low):** Patients with significant risk factors for recurrence (particularly smoking) or with recurrent disease at 6-month colonoscopy may be started on thiopurines or anti-TNF therapy (94.4% agreement).

**Statement 72 (strong, moderate):** Mesalazine should NOT be given to prevent postoperative recurrence (97.2% agreement). A Cochrane review showed no benefit over placebo.

**Risk stratification:** High-risk features include smoking, penetrating disease, ≥2 prior surgeries, perianal disease, extensive small bowel disease (>50 cm), granulomas in resection specimen, and myenteric plexitis.

**Evidence summary:** The TOPPIC trial showed thiopurines improved outcomes in smokers but did not confirm a wider postoperative role (no overall difference in clinical or endoscopic recurrence at 3 years). Anti-TNF therapy (infliximab, adalimumab) reduces endoscopic recurrence (30.6% vs 60% for infliximab in the largest RCT) but clinical recurrence reduction is harder to demonstrate due to low event rates. The guideline's algorithm (Figure 3) stratifies by risk: low-risk → no prophylaxis, colonoscopy at 6 months; high-risk → anti-TNF ± immunomodulator.

**Non-inflammatory causes of post-surgical symptoms:**
- **Bile salt malabsorption (Statement 68, strong, moderate):** Common after ileal resection (>80%). Treat empirically with bile acid sequestrants (colestyramine, colesevelam). SeHCAT scan reserved for diagnostic uncertainty.
- **Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth:** Prevalence ~30% post-resection. Empirical antibiotic treatment (metronidazole, ciprofloxacin, rifaximin).
- **GPR 13:** Patients with recurrent symptoms but no active inflammation should have alternative diagnoses investigated.

**My assessment:** The postoperative section reflects the UK's pragmatic approach: risk-stratify, use the cheapest effective option, and don't overtreat low-risk patients. The explicit advice to NOT use mesalazine postoperatively (Statement 72) is stronger than ACG 2018's conditional recommendation. The acknowledgement of bile salt malabsorption as a common cause of post-surgical diarrhoea (>80% prevalence post-ileal resection) is a practical pearl frequently overlooked in guidelines that focus exclusively on inflammatory recurrence.

## Common Disease Considerations

### Infection Screening and Prevention

**Statement 78 (weak, very low):** Blood tests for TPMT status and viral screen (HBV, HCV, HIV, VZV) may be arranged once diagnosis is confirmed for all CD and moderate-to-severe UC patients (88.9% agreement).

**Statement 79:** Patients commencing immunomodulators or biologics should undergo screening for HBV, HCV, HIV, and VZV. TB screening (chest X-ray + IGRA) is essential prior to anti-TNF therapy.

**Statement 80 (strong):** Prior to commencing anti-TNF therapy, patients should be screened for latent TB using clinical risk stratification, chest X-ray, and IGRA. Tuberculin skin tests have high false-negative rates in immunosuppressed patients.

**Statement 75 (weak, low):** *C. difficile* infection in ASUC is associated with significantly increased risk of colectomy and should be treated with oral vancomycin (93.2% agreement).

**Statement 76 (weak, low):** Patients with moderate-to-severe colitis, particularly steroid-refractory, should have colonic biopsies for CMV — H&E staining with immunohistochemistry and/or quantitative tissue PCR (93.3% agreement).

**Statement 77 (strong, very low):** CMV reactivation in hospitalized colitis patients may be treated with IV ganciclovir 5 mg/kg twice daily while continuing corticosteroids/rescue therapy. In systemic CMV disease, all immunosuppression should be stopped (88.4% agreement).

### Thiopurine Management

**Statement 84 (strong):** All patients should have TPMT status checked before starting thiopurines. TPMT deficiency (homozygous) is a contraindication; heterozygotes require reduced doses.

**Statement 85 (strong):** Thiopurines should be started at low dose and escalated with clinical and laboratory monitoring (FBC, LFTs). Dose optimization guided by clinical response and thiopurine metabolite measurement if available.

**Statement 86 (strong):** Women with IBD should be counselled about thiopurine therapy risks in pregnancy — thiopurines are considered low risk and should generally be continued during pregnancy to maintain remission.

**Statement 87:** Thiopurine metabolites (6-TGN and 6-MMP) can be measured to guide dose optimization in non-responders or to investigate myelotoxicity/hepatotoxicity.

**Statement 88 (weak, low):** Low-dose thiopurines (25-33% of standard dose) in combination with allopurinol can be used to optimize thiopurine metabolism in patients who hyper-methylate (high 6-MMP/low 6-TGN pattern).

### Therapeutic Drug Monitoring

**Statement 92 (weak, low):** Treatment options for failure of initial anti-TNF therapy (dose escalation, interval shortening, switch within class, or switch out of class) may be informed by clinical context and measurement of serum drug and anti-drug antibody concentrations (97.7% agreement).

**Statement 93 (weak, moderate):** Patients with secondary loss of response to anti-TNF therapy may have serum drug and anti-drug antibody concentrations measured to inform treatment changes (97.6% agreement).

**TDM framework (Figure 4):** The guideline provides a pragmatic algorithm distinguishing:
1. **Mechanistic failure:** Therapeutic drug levels, absent antibodies, active inflammation → switch out of class
2. **Non-immune pharmacokinetic failure:** Low trough, absent antibodies → dose escalation
3. **Immune-mediated failure:** Low/undetectable trough, high-titre antibodies → switch within class (or out of class if previously anti-TNF-responsive for prolonged period)

**Statement 94 (weak, very low):** Pretreatment screening and monitoring for vedolizumab and ustekinumab should follow anti-TNF recommendations due to insufficient long-term safety data (95.3% agreement).

**GPR 16:** Patients on immunomodulators or biologics should have an annual review of treatment — as mandated by NICE technology appraisals for biologic therapies (97.7% agreement).

### Biosimilars

**Statement 91 (strong, moderate):** Biosimilar infliximab (CT-P13) may be used for induction and maintenance in CD and UC. The PANTS study showed no clinically meaningful differences between originator and biosimilar infliximab in efficacy or immunogenicity. Switching from originator to biosimilar in stable patients is supported by NOR-SWITCH and real-world data.

### Corticosteroid Management

**Statement 98 (strong):** Prolonged corticosteroid use should be avoided. All patients on corticosteroids for >3 months should be considered for steroid-sparing therapy.

**Statement 99 (strong):** All patients receiving a prolonged course of corticosteroids (prednisolone ≥20 mg/day for ≥3 weeks, or repeated courses) should be assessed for corticosteroid-induced osteoporosis and offered bone protection (bisphosphonates, calcium, vitamin D).

**GPR 18:** Patients receiving corticosteroids for >3 months should be assessed for adrenal suppression before withdrawal.

**GPR 19:** Patients on prolonged corticosteroids should be given a steroid treatment card and counselled about the risks of abrupt cessation.

### Perioperative Management

**Statement 105 (strong, very low):** IBD patients should have assessment and optimization of physical condition prior to elective surgery, including imaging, abscess drainage, treatment of sepsis, correction of anaemia, treatment of malnutrition, and physical mobilization (97.3% agreement).

**Statement 106 (strong, very low):** Nutritional status should be assessed preoperatively; if at risk of malnutrition, nutritional support (oral supplements, enteral or parenteral nutrition) should be provided (97.3% agreement).

**Statement 107 (weak, very low):** Patients with penetrating/stricturing CD or malnutrition may benefit from exclusive or partial enteral nutrition for ≥6 weeks preoperatively (97.2% agreement).

**Statement 108 (strong):** Prior to elective surgery, corticosteroids should be stopped if possible or brought to the lowest manageable dose. Anti-TNF therapy for CD should be discontinued where possible preoperatively (6-8 weeks for infliximab, 4 weeks for adalimumab). For UC surgery, stopping anti-TNF is less critical.

**Statement 109 (strong):** IBD patients who have been on corticosteroids within 12 months of surgery should receive perioperative corticosteroid cover (IV hydrocortisone equivalent to their oral dose until able to resume oral prednisolone). There is no value in increasing steroid dose to cover surgical stress. Anaesthetists should administer a single dose (e.g., dexamethasone 4 mg IV/IM) at induction for patients on >5 mg prednisolone.

**Statement 110 (strong):** UC patients requiring colectomy should be offered IPAA (restorative proctocolectomy) where appropriate. A staged approach reduces complications.

**Statement 111 (strong):** For patients aged 16-40, laparoscopic surgery should be offered where expertise exists. Laparoscopic IPAA may reduce the impact on female fertility.

**Statement 112 (weak):** Where it is not possible to perform a primary anastomosis after resection, a temporary or permanent stoma should be considered.

### Pregnancy and Biologics

**Statement 128 (weak, very low):** IBD patients receiving anti-TNF therapy should be counselled about risks and benefits of continuing treatment throughout pregnancy. For patients with active disease or high risk of relapse, it may be advisable to continue throughout. For those with inactive disease who wish to discontinue, it may be reasonable to stop at the start of the third trimester (97.7% agreement).

**Key recommendations:**
- Active IBD prior to conception is associated with poor pregnancy outcomes (premature delivery, low birth weight, spontaneous abortion) — disease remission before conception is the goal.
- Anti-TNF therapy in the third trimester increases transplacental transfer; infliximab levels are more variable than adalimumab in maternal blood during pregnancy.
- Low levels of biologics are detectable in breast milk but breastfed infants of mothers on biologics have similar infection rates and milestone achievement to non-exposed infants.
- Vedolizumab, ustekinumab, and tofacitinib: limited pregnancy data. Until further evidence is available, the same practice as for anti-TNF drugs should be applied.

**Statement 129 (weak, very low):** BCG vaccination should be withheld until at least 6 months after birth, and rotavirus vaccine should not be given, for infants exposed in utero to biologic therapies. Non-live vaccinations may be given according to standard schedules (97.7% agreement).

### Colorectal Cancer Surveillance

**Statement 125 (strong, very low):** IBD patients with colonic disease should be offered ileocolonoscopy 8 years after symptom onset to screen for neoplasia, determine disease extent, and decide on ongoing surveillance frequency (89.1% agreement). Surveillance commences at diagnosis if PSC is present.

**Risk-stratified surveillance intervals (2010 BSG update, endorsed by this guideline):**
- **Lower risk:** 5 years — extensive colitis with no active endoscopic/histological inflammation, or left-sided colitis, or Crohn's colitis affecting <50% of colon
- **Intermediate risk:** 3 years — extensive colitis with mildly active inflammation, post-inflammatory polyps, family history of CRC in first-degree relative aged ≥50
- **Higher risk:** 1 year — extensive colitis with moderate/severe inflammation, stricture, dysplasia in last 5 years, PSC (including post-transplant), family history of CRC in first-degree relative aged <50

**Detection methods:** The guideline defers to the 2015 SCENIC international consensus for detailed dysplasia detection recommendations. High-definition white-light colonoscopy is recommended over standard-definition. Chromoendoscopy is recommended when using standard-definition scopes. Targeted biopsies are preferred over random biopsies. For endoscopically invisible dysplasia (confirmed by GI pathologist), referral to an endoscopist with expertise in IBD surveillance using HD-chromoendoscopy is suggested.

**Statement 126 (strong, moderate):** UC/IBD-U patients with left-sided or more extensive disease should take mesalazine ≥2 g daily to reduce colorectal cancer risk (95.5% agreement). This is a uniquely strong chemoprevention recommendation not found in ACG guidelines at the same evidence level.

**Statement 127 (weak, low):** Thiopurines may reduce CRC risk, but evidence for methotrexate, calcineurin inhibitors, anti-TNF, and anti-integrin agents is lacking — they cannot be recommended solely for chemoprevention (93% agreement).

**My assessment:** The BSG's surveillance recommendations are aligned with SCENIC but notably stronger on 5-ASA chemoprevention (Statement 126). This reflects the UK's long-standing practice of recommending mesalazine for CRC risk reduction, supported by observational data. The risk-stratified interval approach (1-5 years) is more nuanced than ACG's simpler "1-3 years" and better reflects the spectrum of CRC risk in UC.

### Smoking, Psychology, and Lifestyle

**Statement 116 (strong, low):** Smokers with Crohn's disease should be encouraged to stop — smokers have higher risk of flare, surgery, and postoperative recurrence (100% agreement).

**Statement 117 (weak, very low):** UC patients who smoke should be encouraged to stop. There is an increased risk of flare after stopping, and patients should be made aware that medication increases may be required (95.7% agreement).

**Statement 118 (weak, very low):** Psychological therapies (CBT, hypnotherapy, mindfulness meditation) may be offered to interested patients, particularly those with psychological symptoms, as adjunctive therapy for symptom control and quality of life (91.1% agreement).

**Statement 119 (weak, very low):** Psychological interventions may be useful for IBD patients with pain where no physical cause can be found (93.5% agreement).

**GPR 22:** IBD patients with pain should be investigated for stricturing disease, abscesses, or uncontrolled inflammation. In the absence of an obvious cause, other factors should be considered including adhesions, visceral hypersensitivity, functional bowel disorder, depression/anxiety, sleep disturbance, stress, and psychosocial factors (95.7% agreement).

**GPR 23:** Long-term opioid use is associated with poor outcomes in IBD and should be discouraged (100% agreement).

**GPR 24:** IBD patients should be asked about fatigue — it is common and often not reported. Fatigue affects 40-72% of IBD patients and persists in 30-50% of those with inactive disease. Anaemia, iron deficiency (even without anaemia), vitamin D deficiency, sleep disturbance, and psychological comorbidity should all be assessed.

**Statement 104 (weak, low):** A low-FODMAP diet may be used to treat functional bowel symptoms in IBD (84.4% agreement).

**Statement 123 (weak, low):** Short-term use of NSAIDs is acceptable in IBD. The guideline is less restrictive than ACG 2018, which recommended avoidance (strong, low).

**Statement 124 (weak, low):** Faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) may be considered for *C. difficile* infection in IBD but is not recommended as primary therapy for IBD outside clinical trials.

### Vaccination

**Statement 82 (strong):** A vaccination history should be taken at diagnosis and prior to starting immunosuppressive therapy.

**Statement 83 (strong):** IBD patients receiving immunosuppressive therapy should receive annual inactivated influenza vaccine and pneumococcal vaccination according to national guidelines. Live vaccines are contraindicated during immunosuppressive therapy and for at least 3 months after stopping.

**Statement 81 (weak):** IBD patients who have travelled to or originate from TB-endemic areas should be screened for latent TB.

**Statement 130 (weak, very low):** Genetic testing for monogenic IBD should be considered in individuals with very early onset of disease (infancy to age 5) or early onset with atypical features including susceptibility to unusual/frequent infections.

### Nutrition

**Statement 121 (strong):** The mainstay of nutritional management is dietary counselling to ensure adequate nutritional intake and to address specific deficiencies.

**Statement 122 (strong):** For IBD-related malnutrition, oral nutritional supplements are first-line; enteral nutrition (NG or PEG) if oral intake insufficient; parenteral nutrition if enteral route is contraindicated or insufficient.

**GPR 25:** IBD patients should undergo nutritional assessment at diagnosis and periodically thereafter. Dietetic expertise should be integral to the IBD MDT.

## Service Delivery — The UK/NHS Framework

This section is the most distinctive feature of the BSG guideline and has no direct equivalent in ACG or AGA guidelines.

### The IBD Multidisciplinary Team (MDT)

**Statement 132 (strong, very low):** Regular MDT meetings should be held to discuss IBD patients with complex needs (97.9% agreement).

**GPR 27:** The IBD MDT should include a core membership of gastroenterologist, colorectal surgeon, IBD specialist nurse, radiologist, dietitian, histopathologist, and pharmacist — all with IBD expertise. The MDT should have a designated coordinator and be able to access advice from stoma nurse, paediatric gastroenterologist, hepatologist, nutrition support team, psychologist, primary care physician, ophthalmologist, rheumatologist, dermatologist, obstetrician, and social worker (97.9% agreement).

The guideline provides a concentric-circle MDT model (Figure 7): the inner circle (present at all meetings) consists of gastroenterologist, surgeon, IBD nurse, radiologist, dietitian, histopathologist, and pharmacist. The middle circle includes professionals who attend when possible. The outer circle contributes through combined clinics or shared protocols. Trainees sit in a grey circle as part of their education.

**GPR 28:** For each patient discussed, a formal record of management decisions should be kept (100% agreement).

### IBD Specialist Nurses

**GPR 32:** Clinical nurse specialists are a vital part of the IBD team — their role includes cost-effective patient education, disease management, therapy monitoring, patient support, continuity of care, audit, and rapid access for advice and review during flares (100% agreement).

The evidence for IBD nurse specialists shows: significant reduction in hospital admissions, emergency attendances, and clinic visits. A Spanish study of >1,200 calls showed a specialist nurse could manage two-thirds independently; nearly 90% were resolved by telephone alone. Norwegian RCT: nurse follow-up outcomes were as good as gastroenterologist follow-up over 1 year, with shorter time from symptom onset to treatment in the nurse-led clinic.

### Service Standards and Access

**Rapid assessment:** Suspected IBD patients should be seen by a specialist within 4 weeks of referral; severe symptoms may require assessment within days. Relapsing patients should have access to the IBD team within 5 working days. Radiological or endoscopic investigations should be performed within 4 weeks (or 24 hours in acute/emergency situations).

**Telephone advice lines:** IBD telephone/email services should be available — these provide cost-effective access to specialist advice without requiring clinic appointments. A Scottish study estimated savings of >£42,000 over 5 months from avoided consultant and GP appointments following introduction of a telephone advice line.

**Statement 134 (strong, very low):** Telephone or video virtual clinics should be available as alternatives to face-to-face appointments for stable patients (97.9% agreement).

**Alternatives to clinic attendance:** Remote follow-up (telephone clinics, email, web-based systems) reduces clinic visits from 2-3 to 1 per year without worsening quality of life, relapse rates, or hospital admissions.

### Electronic Patient Data and Audit

**GPR 29:** IBD units should have quality improvement and audit processes embedded within regular clinical activity (100% agreement).

**GPR 30:** IBD services should collect data on adverse events and near misses. The service should participate in local and national audit and engage in ongoing quality improvement (100% agreement).

**GPR 31:** Patient data on biologic and immunomodulator treatment should be recorded for clinical management and to monitor adverse events and outcomes for both local and national audit. Biological treatments should be recorded by brand name where biosimilars are available (97.8% agreement).

The UK National IBD Audit is highlighted as a powerful tool that enabled services to compare practice and catalyzed service improvement.

### Primary Care Integration

**GPR 33 (Integrated Care):** Clear and comprehensive communication between hospital and primary care is essential. Information sharing should include: diagnosis, current medications, monitoring requirements, treatment targets, action plan for flare management, and when and how to contact the IBD team.

Primary care is often the first point of contact for deteriorating IBD patients. The guideline recommends that primary care clinicians be informed of: the patient's baseline disease activity, current treatment and monitoring schedule, specific symptoms that should trigger referral back to secondary care, and vaccination status.

### Self-Management

**Statement 135 (weak, moderate):** IBD patients who wish to participate in self-management and have stable disease may be offered guided self-management as part of their care.

A UK RCT of 700 patients showed self-management led to fewer hospital visits (difference −1.04, p<0.001) without increased primary care visits. The "myIBDcoach" telemedicine system (Netherlands RCT, 909 patients) demonstrated reduced outpatient clinic attendance (1.55 vs 2.34 visits/year, p<0.0001) and fewer hospital admissions (0.05 vs 0.10, p=0.046) with equivalent quality of care.

**My assessment:** The service delivery section is the BSG guideline's most distinctive contribution. It is the only major IBD guideline to specify: (a) exact MDT composition with a concentric model, (b) minimum service standards (4-week referral-to-assessment, 5-working-day flare access), (c) explicit recognition of IBD nurse specialists as cost-effective providers, (d) telephone advice line infrastructure as a service requirement, and (e) guided self-management as a formal care pathway. These are aspirational standards for the NHS — they define what an IBD service should look like, not just what treatments to use. ACG/AGA guidelines have no equivalent section. ECCO comes closest with its quality-of-care standards.

## Guideline-Level Assessment

### Recommendations Summary

Of the 168 total recommendations:
- **135 GRADE statements:** Covering UC (Statements 1-26), CD (Statements 27-74), and common disease considerations (Statements 75-135)
- **33 Good Practice Recommendations:** Covering areas where GRADE methodology was inapplicable

**GRADE quality distribution:** The majority of recommendations are supported by "low" or "very low" quality evidence — reflecting the IBD evidence base. Only a handful have "high" quality: 5-ASA for UC induction/maintenance, anti-TNF and vedolizumab for UC induction, infliximab + thiopurine combination in CD (SONIC), budesonide for ileocaecal CD, and infliximab/ciclosporin for ASUC rescue. This is consistent with other IBD guidelines.

**Strong vs weak distribution:** Strong recommendations predominate where the benefit-risk ratio is clearly favorable (5-ASA for UC, corticosteroids for active disease, biologics for refractory disease, avoidance of steroids for maintenance, colectomy for refractory ASUC). Weak recommendations predominate where evidence is indirect, choices are preference-sensitive, or cost considerations apply.

### Unique Strengths

1. **Single-document scope:** The only major international guideline covering UC and CD in one integrated document, enabling consistent cross-disease recommendations (infection screening, drug monitoring, service delivery).

2. **NHS/NICE integration:** Explicit incorporation of NICE technology appraisals, NHS funding pathways, and UK service infrastructure makes this directly implementable in the NHS — unlike ACG/AGA/ECCO guidelines that require translation to local health systems.

3. **Service delivery framework:** The MDT, IBD nurse, telephone advice line, virtual clinic, audit, primary care integration, and self-management recommendations have no equivalent in North American guidelines. This reflects the NHS as an integrated system.

4. **Patient participation:** Patients from Crohn's and Colitis UK were voting members of the eDelphi consensus group — not just reviewers. The 20 research priorities were patient-ranked via 2,379 survey responses. This is more formal patient involvement than any comparator guideline.

5. **Pragmatic cost-consciousness:** The guideline explicitly lists cost as a factor in biologic choice and notes that NICE technology appraisals determine access. This honest acknowledgment of resource constraints is absent from US guidelines.

6. **Pouchitis management included:** Unlike ACG UC 2019, which omitted pouchitis entirely.

7. **Largest evidence base:** 88,247 publications — the most comprehensive systematic review of any IBD guideline.

8. **PANTS study integration:** The UK-specific PANTS 3-year observational cohort provides real-world immunogenicity data that enriches the biologic recommendations beyond trial data alone.

### Comparison Against US Guidelines (ACG 2018/2019)

| Domain | BSG 2019 | ACG 2018/2019 |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope** | UC + CD in one document | Separate guidelines |
| **Recommendations** | 168 (135 + 33 GPRs) | 60 (CD); 49 (UC) |
| **Methodology** | GRADE + eDelphi (81 voters) | GRADE (panel of 5-6) |
| **Patient involvement** | Voting members of consensus group | No formal role |
| **Systematic review size** | 88,247 publications | Not quantified |
| **NICE/health system** | Explicit NHS/NICE framework | No health system integration |
| **Cost** | Listed as factor in drug choice | Not discussed |
| **Service delivery** | Full section (MDT, nurses, audit, primary care) | Absent |
| **Tofacitinib positioning** | Post-anti-TNF failure only | Anti-TNF-naive OK (strong rec) |
| **Beclomethasone** | Included as option | Not available in US |
| **EEN in adults** | Promoted with caveats | Briefly mentioned |
| **Certolizumab** | Not licensed in UK, not discussed | Included (three recommendations) |
| **Surgery for localized CD** | Explicitly endorsed as primary option | Mentioned as last resort |
| **Pouchitis** | Covered | Not covered in ACG UC 2019 |

| **Biosimilars** | CT-P13 endorsed with PANTS data | Briefly mentioned (NOR-SWITCH) |

### Weakest Sections

1. **Sequencing of advanced therapies:** Like ACG guidelines, the BSG lists all options without a prescriptive algorithm. The cost factor is acknowledged but not operationalized into a decision framework. ECCO 2022 provides the most detailed algorithm by comparison.

2. **Dietary therapy beyond EEN:** The guideline acknowledges interest in elimination diets (CD-TREAT, LOFFLEX) but provides no actionable recommendations — honest given the evidence, but leaves clinicians without answers for the most common patient question.

3. **Histological healing:** Acknowledged as associated with improved outcomes but not endorsed as a treatment target. STRIDE-II (2021) would partially close this gap.

4. **Newer biologics in pregnancy:** Vedolizumab, ustekinumab, and tofacitinib data are limited to case reports/series — the recommendation to follow anti-TNF practice is pragmatic but evidence-free.

### Most Controversial Claims

1. **Statement 11 (tofacitinib post-anti-TNF failure only):** More conservative than ACG 2019 but vindicated by post-marketing safety data. This was the right call in retrospect.

2. **Statement 126 (mesalazine ≥2 g/day for CRC chemoprevention):** A stronger recommendation than any other guideline. The evidence is observational and confounded by mucosal healing — is it the drug or the healed mucosa that reduces risk?

3. **Statement 36 (laparoscopic resection as alternative to medical therapy for localized ileocaecal CD):** Challenges the "biologics-first" paradigm. Supported by cost-effectiveness data but may reflect UK surgical culture more than evidence superiority.

4. **Statement 4 (5-ASA dose escalation before steroids):** Weaker recommendation than ACG (weak vs strong) for pushing to 4.8 g/day — reflects uncertainty about whether dose escalation meaningfully changes outcomes.

### Historical Significance

The BSG 2019 guideline represents several transitions in IBD management: from separate UC/CD guidelines to a unified document, from purely clinical recommendations to integrated service delivery standards, and from clinician-only guideline development to formal patient participation. It was published at the same inflection point as ACG 2019 — just as the therapeutic landscape was expanding beyond anti-TNF monotherapy to include vedolizumab, ustekinumab, and tofacitinib. Its conservative positioning of tofacitinib (post-anti-TNF failure only) proved prescient. The guideline's most lasting contribution may be its service delivery framework — the explicit specification of what an IBD service should look like, which has been adopted as the de facto standard for UK IBD units and influenced service design internationally.

### Cross-Reference Priorities

- **ACG Crohn's 2018 (Lichtenstein):** Compare treatment algorithms — BSG is more surgery-positive and cost-conscious. Major divergence on mesalazine and antibiotic roles.
- **ACG UC 2019 (Rubin):** Compare directly — both published in 2019. Key differences: tofacitinib positioning, beclomethasone inclusion, pouchitis coverage, service delivery.
- **ECCO Crohn's 2020 (Torres):** Compare surgical thresholds and EEN prominence — ECCO/BSG are more aligned than either is with ACG.
- **ECCO UC Therapeutics 2022 (Raine):** The most current international comparator. Will differ on tofacitinib (more conservative post-2021), inclusion of ustekinumab for UC (not in BSG 2019 or ACG 2019), and histological healing as a target.
- **STRIDE-II (2021):** Updates the treatment targets cited in this guideline. Key change: histological remission elevated to adjunctive measure.
- **SCENIC (2015):** Dysplasia detection consensus deferred to by BSG's surveillance section.
- **NICE Technology Appraisals:** Individual TAs for infliximab (TA163/TA329), adalimumab (TA329), golimumab (TA329), vedolizumab (TA342), ustekinumab (TA456/TA633), and tofacitinib (TA547) — these are the actual gatekeepers of biologic access in the NHS, referenced but not detailed in the guideline.
