# AGA Clinical Practice Guidelines on Moderate to Severe Ulcerative Colitis (Feuerstein et al., 2020)

The first formal AGA guideline focused specifically on moderate-to-severe UC, using full GRADE methodology. Published January 2020 in *Gastroenterology*, accompanied by a comprehensive technical review. The guideline was updated mid-development to incorporate the VARSITY trial (vedolizumab vs. adalimumab), UNIFI trial (ustekinumab), and the July 2019 FDA safety update restricting tofacitinib to post-TNF failure. These late-breaking additions fundamentally shaped the final recommendations. The guideline addresses 11 PICO questions: 7 for outpatient moderate-severe UC, 4 for hospitalized ASUC. Only one recommendation (R1: treat with biologics) is strong; all others are conditional or "no recommendation." The guideline explicitly excludes surgical management and positions itself alongside the AGA's separate TDM guideline (Feuerstein et al. 2017) and mild-moderate UC guideline (Ko et al. 2018).

**Critical context:** This guideline's scope is narrower than the ACG UC 2019 guideline (Rubin et al.), which covers the full spectrum of UC from diagnosis through cancer surveillance. The AGA 2020 guideline assumes the diagnosis is established and focuses exclusively on medical management of moderate-to-severe disease. It was published ~12 months after the ACG guideline, incorporating newer trial data (VARSITY, UNIFI) and a major FDA safety action. Where the ACG guideline lists anti-TNFs as a class with vedolizumab as an alternative, the AGA uses network meta-analysis to rank individual agents. This difference in methodological approach — class-based vs. agent-specific comparison — is the central point of divergence between the two guidelines.

## Disease Definitions and Scope

### Claim 1: The guideline explicitly defines moderate-severe UC by objective criteria — Truelove & Witts and Mayo Clinic score — and targets a high-risk population

**Recommendation:** Not a graded recommendation (definitional, scope-setting). Moderate-severe UC is defined by: (a) corticosteroid dependence or refractoriness, (b) severe endoscopic disease (presence of ulcers), or (c) high risk of colectomy. Mayo Clinic score 6-12 with endoscopic subscore ≥2. ASUC is defined as hospitalized patients with ≥6 bloody bowel movements/day AND at least one marker of systemic toxicity (HR >90, temperature >37.8°C, Hb <10.5 g/dL, and/or ESR >30 mm/h). Predictors of aggressive disease: young age at diagnosis (<40), extensive disease, deep ulcers, extra-intestinal manifestations, early corticosteroid need, elevated inflammatory markers.

**GRADE rating:** Not graded (scope-setting).

**Evidence summary:** Population-based cohort data cited: 15% of UC patients have an aggressive course; 20% of these require hospitalization. 5- and 10-year cumulative colectomy risk: 10-15%, concentrated in moderate-severe patients. Hospitalized ASUC patients have short-term colectomy rates of 25-30%. The guideline uses a dual definition (Truelove & Witts severity + Mayo endoscopic severity) that is more specific than the ACG guideline's broader severity categories.

**Comparison against ACG UC 2019 (G2):** Both use Mayo score and Truelove & Witts. The ACG guideline addresses the full severity spectrum (mild, moderate, severe) within a single document; the AGA splits mild-moderate and moderate-severe into separate guidelines. The ACG guideline's hospitalized ASUC section is more comprehensive — it addresses VTE prophylaxis, C. difficile testing, and nutrition (TPN), none of which the AGA guideline covers.

**My assessment:** The dual-criteria definition (Mayo + Truelove & Witts) creates more specific entry criteria for clinical trials but may not perfectly map to clinical practice where "moderate-severe" is often a gestalt. The separate guideline for mild-moderate UC (Ko et al. 2018) means a clinician managing a patient who escalates from mild to moderate disease must consult two different AGA documents. This is less convenient than the ACG's single-document approach.

## Recommendation 1: Biologics and Small Molecules Over No Treatment

### Claim 2: All approved biologics and tofacitinib are superior to placebo for induction and maintenance — this is the guideline's only strong recommendation

**Recommendation 1:** "In adult outpatients with moderate-severe UC, the AGA recommends using infliximab, adalimumab, golimumab, vedolizumab, tofacitinib or ustekinumab over no treatment." **Strong recommendation, moderate quality evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** 16 RCTs pooled. Induction (6-8 weeks) RR vs. placebo: infliximab 2.85 (2.11-3.86), adalimumab 1.62 (1.15-2.29), golimumab 2.49 (1.58-3.93), vedolizumab 2.22 (1.36-3.64), tofacitinib 3.22 (2.03-5.08), ustekinumab 2.91 (1.72-4.94). Maintenance (30-54 weeks) RR vs. placebo: infliximab 2.25 (1.67-3.05), adalimumab 2.28 (1.52-3.42), golimumab 1.88 (1.32-2.68), vedolizumab 2.31 (1.63-3.28), tofacitinib 5mg BID 3.09 (1.99-4.79), ustekinumab 1.83 (1.33-2.49). All well-tolerated; SAE rates not significantly different from placebo. Evidence rated down for imprecision (<200 events). Tofacitinib dose nuance: induction 10mg BID × 8 weeks (extendable to 16 weeks in partial responders); maintenance 5mg BID with option to escalate to 10mg BID if loss of response — but the higher dose carries an FDA warning for increased pulmonary embolism and all-cause mortality.

**Comparison against ACG UC 2019 (G2):** The ACG has separate recommendations for each class: anti-TNF induction (strong, moderate), vedolizumab maintenance (strong, moderate), tofacitinib induction (strong, moderate). The AGA bundles everything into one "treat with something" recommendation — a philosophical choice that says the first-order question is "treat or don't treat," and the second-order question is "which agent" (addressed in R2a-c). Both arrive at the same bottom line: biologic therapy is effective and recommended.

**My assessment:** This is the least controversial recommendation in the guideline — every agent beats placebo. The single strong recommendation anchors the document. The nuance is in the tofacitinib dosing footnote: the FDA safety signal for 10mg BID (pulmonary embolism, all-cause mortality) means the higher maintenance dose should be used with caution. Compare this to the ACG guideline which lists tofacitinib 10mg BID for induction without the FDA caveat (published before the July 2019 update).

## Recommendation 2: Positioning Biologics by Treatment History

### Claim 3: In biologic-naïve patients, infliximab or vedolizumab are preferred over adalimumab — based on network meta-analysis and the VARSITY head-to-head trial

**Recommendation 2a:** "In biologic-naïve patients, the AGA suggests using infliximab or vedolizumab rather than adalimumab for induction of remission." **Conditional recommendation, moderate quality evidence.** Comment: patients valuing subcutaneous convenience may reasonably choose adalimumab.

**Recommendation 2b:** Tofacitinib in biologic-naïve patients: "No recommendation, knowledge gap." FDA (July 2019) restricts tofacitinib to post-TNF failure; use in naïve patients only in clinical/registry studies.

**Evidence summary:** VARSITY trial (vedolizumab vs. adalimumab head-to-head): clinical remission at week 52 — 34.2% vedolizumab vs. 24.3% adalimumab (RR 1.41; 1.10-1.81) among biologic-naïve patients. Network meta-analysis of 15 RCTs (3747 biologic-naïve patients): infliximab superior to adalimumab (OR 2.10; 1.16-3.79). Tofacitinib trials were excluded from NMA because they used a stricter outcome definition (rectal bleeding score of zero vs. 0 or 1 in other trials). Important caveat: in the NMA-included trials, anti-TNF treatment was not optimized to target drug concentrations — the guideline acknowledges that "the efficacy of infliximab, adalimumab and golimumab may be comparable in patients who achieve adequate drug concentrations."

**Comparison against ACG UC 2019 (G2):** This is the single most important divergence between the two guidelines. The ACG treats anti-TNFs as a class (R22: "we recommend anti-TNF therapy using adalimumab, golimumab, or infliximab") and positions vedolizumab as an alternative. The AGA uses network meta-analysis to rank individual agents, concluding infliximab > adalimumab and vedolizumab > adalimumab. The ACG approach reflects class-effect thinking with shared mechanism of action; the AGA approach reflects evidence-based ranking with different trial outcomes. The TDM caveat (comparable efficacy at adequate drug concentrations) partially reconciles the two positions — optimized adalimumab may perform as well as infliximab, but the published trial data don't show it.

**My assessment:** The AGA's agent-specific ranking is methodologically sophisticated but clinically fragile. Network meta-analysis of placebo-controlled trials is sensitive to differences in trial populations, outcome definitions, and concomitant medication use. The tofacitinib exclusion from NMA illustrates the problem — a different outcome definition was enough to disqualify an entire drug class. The VARSITY head-to-head data are more reliable than the NMA-derived ORs, but VARSITY only compared two agents. The TDM caveat is the key: if you achieve therapeutic drug levels, the anti-TNFs may be equivalent. This means the guideline's ranking may reflect dosing inadequacy rather than intrinsic drug superiority. The ACG's class-based approach is less informative but avoids over-interpreting fragile indirect comparisons.

### Claim 4: In infliximab-exposed patients (especially primary non-responders), ustekinumab or tofacitinib are preferred over vedolizumab or adalimumab

**Recommendation 2c:** "In patients with prior infliximab exposure, particularly primary non-response, the AGA suggests using ustekinumab or tofacitinib rather than vedolizumab or adalimumab for induction of remission." **Conditional recommendation, low quality evidence.** Comment: patients prioritizing safety may reasonably choose vedolizumab.

**Evidence summary:** Separate NMA of 7 RCTs in 1580 patients with prior TNF exposure (infliximab and golimumab excluded — they only studied biologic-naïve). Tofacitinib vs. adalimumab: OR 11.05 (1.79-68.41). Ustekinumab vs. adalimumab: OR 10.71 (2.01-57.20). Tofacitinib vs. vedolizumab: OR 6.18 (1.00-38.00). Ustekinumab vs. vedolizumab: OR 5.99 (1.13-31.76). All comparisons rated down for intransitivity (prior treatment exposure is an effect modifier; study-level data didn't report proportion with >1 TNF exposure or multi-class exposure). VARSITY subset (~21% with prior non-adalimumab TNF exposure): no significant difference vedolizumab vs. adalimumab at week 52 (20.3% vs. 16.0%). The guideline explicitly limits these recommendations to infliximab-exposed patients — benefit of switching in adalimumab- or golimumab-exposed patients is uncertain.

**Comparison against ACG UC 2019 (G2):** The ACG recommends vedolizumab (strong, moderate) or tofacitinib (strong, moderate) after anti-TNF failure — but does not rank them against each other. The ACG's Key Concept 24 says primary non-responders to anti-TNF should switch out of class rather than cycle within the anti-TNF class. The AGA's OR point estimates (6-11×) are dramatic but the confidence intervals are enormous (lower bound 1.00-2.01), reflecting the thin evidence base. Both guidelines agree on out-of-class switching; the AGA goes further by ranking the out-of-class options.

**My assessment:** The OR magnitude for tofacitinib and ustekinumab over adalimumab (OR ~11) is implausibly large and almost certainly reflects confounding by indication in the underlying trials (sicker patients, more refractory disease, differential prior exposure). The wide CIs make these estimates nearly uninterpretable. The practical message — ustekinumab or tofacitinib over a second anti-TNF after primary infliximab failure — aligns with the ACG's out-of-class switching principle, but the ranking of ustekinumab/tofacitinib over vedolizumab is on very thin ice. The VARSITY subgroup analysis showing no difference between vedolizumab and adalimumab in TNF-exposed patients directly contradicts the NMA's OR of 5.99 for ustekinumab vs. vedolizumab. The evidence doesn't support ranking vedolizumab below ustekinumab/tofacitinib in this population; the VARSITY data suggest equipoise.

## Recommendations 3-4: Immunomodulators — When to Use, When to Avoid

### Claim 5: Thiopurine monotherapy is ineffective for induction but effective for maintenance; methotrexate monotherapy is ineffective for both

**Recommendation 3a:** Against thiopurine monotherapy for induction. **Conditional, very low quality.**

**Recommendation 3b:** For thiopurine monotherapy for maintenance (in patients who achieved remission, typically with corticosteroids). **Conditional, low quality.**

**Recommendation 3c:** Against methotrexate monotherapy for induction or maintenance. **Conditional, low quality.**

**Evidence summary:** Thiopurine induction: 5 trials (3 vs. placebo, 2 vs. 5-ASA). Most patients were corticosteroid-dependent. RR for corticosteroid-free remission 1.25 (1.01-1.56) but evidence rated very low due to serious risk of bias, imprecision, indirectness (variable outcome definitions, 4-52 weeks, unclear if remission was corticosteroid- or thiopurine-induced). Thiopurine maintenance: 7 trials (4 vs. placebo, 3 vs. 5-ASA). RR for preventing relapse 0.61 (0.49-0.77), rated low quality. Methotrexate: the pivotal METEOR trial showed no significant difference vs. placebo for corticosteroid-free remission (RR 1.31; 0.89-1.94). Maintenance similarly negative (RR 1.01; 0.79-1.29). Both rated very low quality.

**Recommendation 4a:** Biologic monotherapy over thiopurine monotherapy for induction. **Conditional, low quality.**

**Recommendation 4b:** No recommendation on biologic vs. thiopurine for maintenance. **Knowledge gap.**

**Evidence summary:** Based on UC-SUCCESS (infliximab vs. azathioprine vs. combination, terminated prematurely). No difference in corticosteroid-free remission at week 16 between infliximab and azathioprine (RR 0.96; 0.53-1.72), but infliximab superior for endoscopic remission. No head-to-head maintenance trials. The panel's logic: since AGA recommends biologics over placebo (moderate quality) and recommends against thiopurines for induction (very low quality), biologics > thiopurines for induction follows. But for maintenance, both strategies work over no treatment (PICO 1 and 3b), and no comparative data exist — hence the knowledge gap.

**Comparison against ACG UC 2019 (G2):** The ACG has fewer immunomodulator-specific recommendations. It recommends thiopurines for maintenance after corticosteroid-induced remission (consistent with AGA 3b). It does not have a specific recommendation against methotrexate for UC — this is an AGA contribution. The ACG's strength: it addresses when to use thiopurines as part of combination therapy (R24: infliximab + thiopurine). The AGA's strength: it explicitly closes the door on methotrexate monotherapy for UC, which the ACG leaves unaddressed.

**My assessment:** The methotrexate recommendation (3c) is the most clinically impactful negative recommendation in this guideline. Many clinicians extrapolate methotrexate's proven efficacy in Crohn's disease to UC — this guideline says don't. The METEOR trial was well-designed and negative. The thiopurine maintenance recommendation (3b) survives on old, methodologically weak trials — the same evidence that would not support a modern drug approval. But the RR of 0.61 for relapse prevention is clinically meaningful, and in resource-limited settings (or in patients who refuse biologics), thiopurine monotherapy remains a reasonable maintenance option. The knowledge gap on biologic vs. thiopurine maintenance (4b) is honest — we simply don't know if stepping down from a biologic to a thiopurine is safe in UC.

## Recommendation 5: Combination Therapy — Extrapolating UC-SUCCESS

### Claim 6: Combination biologic + immunomodulator therapy is recommended over either monotherapy, but the evidence is almost entirely infliximab-specific and the extrapolation to other biologics is acknowledged as indirect

**Recommendations 5a-b:** "Combine TNFα antagonists, vedolizumab, or ustekinumab with thiopurines or methotrexate rather than biologic monotherapy" **AND** "rather than thiopurine monotherapy." **Both conditional, low quality.**

**Evidence summary:** All based on UC-SUCCESS (infliximab + azathioprine vs. infliximab vs. azathioprine, biologic-naïve patients, terminated prematurely). Combination vs. infliximab monotherapy: RR 1.78 (1.08-1.94) for corticosteroid-free remission at week 16. Combination vs. azathioprine monotherapy: RR 1.70 (1.04-2.78). No maintenance data from RCTs. Observational French study (n=82): continuing combination superior to de-escalating to infliximab monotherapy (very low quality). The panel explicitly extrapolates from infliximab to vedolizumab and ustekinumab, citing the pharmacokinetic rationale (immunomodulators increase trough concentrations and decrease immunogenicity), while acknowledging that "immunogenicity of newer biologic agents may be lower than infliximab."

**Comparison against ACG UC 2019 (G2):** This is the most important point of AGREEMENT — both guidelines recommend combination therapy — but with a critical difference in scope. The ACG limits its combination recommendation specifically to infliximab + thiopurine (R24: "When infliximab is used... we recommend combination therapy with a thiopurine"). The AGA extends the recommendation to ALL biologics (TNFα antagonists, vedolizumab, ustekinumab). The ACG's position is more evidence-bound (only infliximab has trial data). The AGA's position is more clinically pragmatic (the pharmacokinetic rationale should apply to all biologics) but acknowledges the extrapolation as indirect and rates the evidence as low quality for non-infliximab agents.

**My assessment:** The AGA's extrapolation is the most aggressive inferential move in the guideline. The logic is: (1) immunomodulators increase biologic trough levels and reduce anti-drug antibodies, (2) this should benefit all biologics, (3) therefore combine. But the magnitude of benefit depends on immunogenicity — and vedolizumab and ustekinumab have substantially lower immunogenicity rates than infliximab. The added benefit of an immunomodulator may be proportionally smaller for these agents. The ACG's infliximab-only scope is more conservative but more defensible. The clinical reality is that many patients on vedolizumab or ustekinumab are on monotherapy, and the guideline's recommendation to add an immunomodulator will change practice based on pharmacokinetic theory rather than clinical trial data.

## Recommendations 6-7: Treatment Strategy — Step-Up vs. Early Biologics, and the Role of 5-ASA

### Claim 7: Early biologics are suggested over gradual step-up after 5-ASA failure in high-risk patients — but the evidence is entirely indirect

**Recommendation 6:** "Early use of biologic agents with or without immunomodulator therapy, rather than gradual step up after failure of 5-aminosalicylates." **Conditional, very low quality.**

**Evidence summary:** No studies directly compared early biologics vs. step-up therapy. Three studies compared thiopurines vs. 5-ASA in corticosteroid-exposed patients; thiopurines achieved higher corticosteroid-free remission rates. Extrapolating from PICOs 3 and 4: biologics > thiopurines > 5-ASA. The guideline reasons that 5-ASAs have not been studied in moderate-severe disease (their evidence base is in mild-moderate), and that "delaying effective treatment... may be harmful due to ongoing untreated active disease, increasing risk of UC-related complications, hospitalization, colectomy and overall inferior quality of life."

**Recommendation 7:** "Against continuing 5-ASA in patients who have achieved remission with biologics and/or immunomodulators or tofacitinib, after prior 5-ASA failure." **Conditional, very low quality.**

**Evidence summary:** One RCT: azathioprine + olsalazine vs. azathioprine alone — no difference in relapse at 2 years (RR 1.02; 0.77-1.34). Subgroup analysis of biologic/tofacitinib RCTs: no difference in induction or maintenance of remission between patients on vs. not on concomitant 5-ASA at trial entry (induction RR 0.94; 0.74-1.18; maintenance RR 0.92; 0.78-1.09). The guideline explicitly states it did not factor in chemoprevention (5-ASA and colorectal cancer risk), citing recent data that "sustained remission is protective regardless of the type of therapy used."

**Comparison against ACG UC 2019 (G2):** The ACG guideline does not have a specific recommendation on stopping 5-ASA after biologic escalation. This is a unique AGA contribution. The ACG recommends 5-ASA for mild-moderate UC and for maintenance of medically-induced remission — but is silent on whether to continue 5-ASA once the patient is on a biologic. In practice, many clinicians continue 5-ASA (partly for chemoprevention, partly out of inertia). The AGA says stop it.

**My assessment:** Recommendation 7 (stop 5-ASA) is the most practice-changing take-home message for many community gastroenterologists. The evidence is thin (very low quality) but the logic is sound: if 5-ASA failed to control the disease in the first place, continuing it after escalation adds cost, pill burden, and potential side effects without demonstrable benefit. The chemoprevention question is the main counterargument, and the guideline's dismissal of it (citing Rubin 2013 on inflammation as the true risk factor) may not satisfy all clinicians. In patients with extensive colitis and longstanding disease, many will still continue mesalamine for perceived chemopreventive benefit, guideline or not.

## Recommendations 8-11: Hospitalized Acute Severe UC

### Claim 8: IV methylprednisolone 40-60 mg/day is sufficient; higher doses add toxicity without efficacy

**Recommendation 8:** "Use IV methylprednisolone 40-60 mg/day rather than higher doses." **Conditional, very low quality.**

**Evidence summary:** No head-to-head trials of different corticosteroid doses. Turner et al. systematic review: mean dose across studies was 68 mg/day (range 40-100 mg); on meta-regression controlling for baseline severity, no correlation between dose and colectomy risk (R²<0.01). Different regimens (once daily vs. divided vs. continuous infusion) showed no superiority. Corticosteroid trials should last 3-5 days; continued use beyond 7 days is ineffective in non-responders.

**Comparison against ACG UC 2019 (G2):** The ACG recommends methylprednisolone 60 mg/day or hydrocortisone 100 mg TID-QID (strong recommendation, low quality). Both agree on 3-5 day assessment window. The ACG's recommendation is "strong" despite low quality evidence; the AGA's is "conditional" — reflecting different institutional thresholds for strong recommendations. The ACG dose (60 mg/day) is at the upper end of the AGA range (40-60 mg/day).

**My assessment:** The 40-60 mg/day range gives clinicians flexibility but may create confusion — 40 mg and 60 mg are different doses. The ACG's firm 60 mg/day recommendation is simpler to implement. Both guidelines agree that dose escalation beyond this range is futile, which is the clinically important point. The lack of correlation between dose and colectomy (R²<0.01) is decisive — if higher doses worked, we would have seen a signal.

### Claim 9: Adjunctive antibiotics should not be used routinely in ASUC without infection

**Recommendation 9:** "Against adjunctive antibiotics in ASUC without infections." **Conditional, very low quality.**

**Evidence summary:** 4 RCTs, heterogeneous antibiotics, 5-10 day courses. Overall RR for colectomy 0.79 (0.46-1.35). Excluding the oral vancomycin trial (which used an insensitive C. difficile test), RR 0.95 (0.55-1.64). Quality rated very low due to risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency.

**Comparison against ACG UC 2019 (G2):** The ACG also recommends against routine antibiotics (strong recommendation, low quality). Both guidelines agree. The ACG's recommendation is "strong" vs. AGA's "conditional" — same pattern of the ACG using strong recommendations more liberally. The ACG additionally addresses C. difficile testing and vancomycin treatment (recommendations 38-39), which the AGA does not cover.

**My assessment:** Uncontroversial. Both guidelines agree. The exclusion of the vancomycin trial from the primary analysis is appropriate — subclinical C. difficile may have confounded the result.

### Claim 10: Infliximab and cyclosporine are equivalent rescue therapies for corticosteroid-refractory ASUC

**Recommendation 10:** "Use infliximab or cyclosporine in corticosteroid-refractory ASUC." **Conditional, low quality.**

**Evidence summary:** Infliximab vs. placebo: single RCT (n=45), single 5 mg/kg dose, RR for 90-day colectomy 0.44 (0.22-0.87). Cyclosporine vs. placebo: single small RCT, RR 0.61 (0.18-2.01) — trend favoring cyclosporine but not statistically significant. Cyclosporine 2 mg/kg/day comparable to 4 mg/kg/day with better safety. Two head-to-head trials (CYSIF, CONSTRUCT): no difference in short-term colectomy between infliximab and cyclosporine (RR 1.00; 0.72-1.40). Long-term CYSIF follow-up (median 4.5 years): colectomy-free survival 61.5% (cyclosporine) vs. 65.1% (infliximab), p=0.97. Notable: after initial randomization period, 45.7% of cyclosporine patients later used infliximab (at 1 year), rising to 57.1% (at 5 years); only 4 infliximab patients switched to cyclosporine. Drug survival favors infliximab in practice.

**Comparison against ACG UC 2019 (G2):** Both guidelines recommend infliximab or cyclosporine as rescue therapy. The ACG's recommendation is "strong" (moderate quality) vs. AGA's "conditional" (low quality) — reflecting the ACG's higher confidence in the infliximab trial data. The ACG additionally addresses surgical consultation at 3-5 days (R38), specific surgical indications (R40: toxic megacolon, perforation, hemorrhage), and the fact that infliximab/cyclosporine do not increase post-operative complications (R41) — all of which the AGA does not cover. The AGA focuses purely on medical decision-making.

**My assessment:** The long-term CYSIF data are the most clinically informative: cyclosporine is a bridge, not a destination. Most cyclosporine-treated patients eventually transition to infliximab. This reflects the practical reality that cyclosporine requires therapeutic drug monitoring, has significant toxicity (nephrotoxicity, hypertension, seizures), and is not a maintenance therapy. Infliximab can be continued as maintenance if effective. The AGA doesn't explicitly say "prefer infliximab over cyclosporine" — but the practical implication of the long-term data is that infliximab is the preferred rescue agent in most scenarios, with cyclosporine reserved for infliximab-refractory patients or those with contraindications to anti-TNF therapy. Both guidelines' equipoise (RR 1.00) reflects short-term outcomes; long-term treatment patterns tell a different story.

### Claim 11: Intensive infliximab dosing in ASUC — the guideline punts

**Recommendation 11:** "No recommendation on routine use of intensive vs. standard infliximab dosing in ASUC." **Knowledge gap.**

**Evidence summary:** No RCTs. 5 observational studies. "Intensive" regimens included shortened intervals (<2 weeks, dose stacking) and/or higher doses (10 mg/kg). Meta-analysis: no significant difference in short-term colectomy (RR 1.61; 0.74-3.52). Subgroup: upfront 10 mg/kg induction superior to dose stacking with 5 mg/kg (RR 0.24; 0.08-0.68). Biological rationale: high inflammatory burden causes accelerated infliximab consumption and fecal wasting; hypoalbuminemia reduces drug exposure. But all observational data are confounded by disease severity — sicker patients get intensive regimens.

**Comparison against ACG UC 2019 (G2):** The ACG does not address intensive vs. standard infliximab dosing — this is a question that only emerged after the ACG guideline was published. The AGA's identification of this as a knowledge gap is forward-looking. The ACG does recommend accelerated infliximab dosing for Crohn's disease but is silent on UC.

**My assessment:** This is the most honest recommendation in the guideline. "We don't know" is the right answer when the evidence is observational, confounded, and inconsistent. The biological rationale for accelerated dosing is strong (fecal wasting, hypoalbuminemia, high inflammatory burden), but the evidence doesn't support routine use. The subgroup signal favoring upfront 10 mg/kg over reactive dose stacking (RR 0.24) is intriguing but comes from a subgroup analysis of observational data. This is the most important research gap identified for hospitalized ASUC. In practice, many IBD centers use accelerated infliximab dosing in ASUC based on the biological rationale and the CYSIF/CONSTRUCT data showing suboptimal colectomy-free survival with standard dosing (~65% at 5 years). The guideline's "no recommendation" appropriately leaves this to clinical judgment.

| R9 | Against adjunctive antibiotics in ASUC | Conditional | Very low |
| R10 | Infliximab or cyclosporine in steroid-refractory ASUC | Conditional | Low |
| R11 | Intensive vs. standard infliximab dosing in ASUC | No recommendation | Knowledge gap |

**Only ONE strong recommendation in the entire guideline.** This is striking — a guideline with 16 recommendation statements reaches "strong" on only one. It reflects the GRADE methodology's stringency and the genuine uncertainty in the UC evidence base. Compared to the ACG UC 2019 guideline, which has multiple strong recommendations (anti-TNF induction, vedolizumab maintenance, tofacitinib induction, hospitalized ASUC management), the AGA is systematically more conservative in its strength assignments.

### Key Divergences from ACG UC 2019 (G2 — Rubin et al.)

| Topic | AGA 2020 (Feuerstein) | ACG 2019 (Rubin) |
|-------|----------------------|-------------------|
| **Biologic choice in naïve** | Ranked: IFX/VDZ > ADA | Class-based: any anti-TNF or VDZ |
| **Tofacitinib in naïve** | Restricted to trials (FDA update) | Recommends post-TNF failure only |
| **Ustekinumab** | Included (R1, R2c, R5a-b) | Not included (pre-approval) |
| **Combination therapy** | Extrapolated to ALL biologics | Specific to infliximab + thiopurine |
| **Methotrexate** | Recommends AGAINST (R3c) | Not specifically addressed |
| **Stop 5-ASA after escalation** | Recommends stopping (R7) | Not addressed |
| **Hospitalized ASUC scope** | Medical only (steroids, IFX/CsA) | Comprehensive (VTE, CDI, nutrition, surgery) |
| **Surgical consultation** | Out of scope | Explicit recommendations (R38, R40, R41) |
| **Colectomy as management** | Only as outcome measure | Actively recommended when refractory |
| **Strength of recommendations** | 1 strong, 12 conditional, 3 no rec | Multiple strong (≥8) |
| **NMA used for positioning** | Yes (agent-specific ranking) | No (class-based approach) |

**Strongest sections:** (1) The biologic positioning algorithm (R2a-c), which provides a clear, evidence-graded decision pathway that clinicians can follow. Despite the fragility of the NMA-based rankings, the stepwise logic (naïve → TNF-exposed) is clinically intuitive. (2) The combination therapy rationale (R5a-b), which explicitly addresses the immunogenicity/pharmacokinetic logic while acknowledging the limits of extrapolation. (3) The ASUC infliximab dosing knowledge gap (R11), which is the most intellectually honest statement in the guideline and identifies the most pressing research question.

**Weakest sections:** (1) The early biologics recommendation (R6), which is rated very low quality and based on a chain of indirect comparisons (biologics > thiopurines > 5-ASA) without a single direct study. This is more expert opinion than evidence. (2) The extrapolation of combination therapy to vedolizumab and ustekinumab (R5a), which assumes the pharmacokinetic benefit applies equally to agents with lower immunogenicity — a reasonable assumption but untested. (3) The hospitalized ASUC section's narrow scope — omitting VTE prophylaxis, C. difficile management, and surgical consultation means the guideline cannot serve as a standalone reference for managing the hospitalized UC patient.

**Most controversial claim:** The ranking of ustekinumab/tofacitinib over vedolizumab in TNF-exposed patients (R2c). The OR point estimates are very large (6-11×) but the lower CI bounds touch or approach 1.0, and the VARSITY head-to-head data show no difference between vedolizumab and adalimumab in this population. The recommendation reads as more confident than the evidence warrants.

**The GRADE stringency pattern:** Compared to the ACG guideline, the AGA uses "conditional" where the ACG uses "strong" for the same clinical question (e.g., hospitalized UC management, corticosteroid dosing). This is not a substantive disagreement — it reflects the AGA's more conservative GRADE interpretation. The ACG guideline was published first and set a baseline; the AGA guideline, published a year later with the same GRADE methodology, systematically downgrades strength. This pattern mirrors what we observed with the SIBO guidelines (ACG SIBO 2020 vs. AGA SIBO 2020), where the AGA document was also more cautious — suggesting an institutional difference in GRADE interpretation, not just case-specific evidence assessment.

### Cross-References

- **G2 (ACG UC 2019 — Rubin et al.):** Primary comparison. Same disease, same year window, different scope and strength assignments. The ACG guideline covers the full UC spectrum; the AGA splits mild-moderate and moderate-severe. The ACG is more procedurally prescriptive (surgery, VTE, CDI); the AGA is more pharmacologically nuanced (agent-specific ranking, NMA, combination extrapolation). Read together for complementary perspectives.

- **AGA TDM IBD 2017 (Feuerstein et al.):** Referenced explicitly in the guideline for therapeutic drug monitoring of biologics. The TDM guideline covers when to measure drug levels and antibodies, which directly informs the biologic positioning debate — if optimized adalimumab is equivalent to infliximab (as the AGA 2020 guideline suggests in its TDM caveat), then TDM becomes the key to making adalimumab a viable first-line option.

- **AGA Mild-Moderate UC 2018 (Ko et al.):** The companion AGA guideline for less severe disease. Patients crossing from mild-moderate to moderate-severe need both documents.

- **ECCO UC 2022 (Raine et al.):** The European perspective on UC management, published after both US guidelines. References both and provides a third framework for biologic positioning and severity assessment. Will be compared separately.

- **ACG SIBO 2020 (Pimentel et al.) and AGA SIBO 2020 (Quigley et al.):** Methodological comparison. Both pairs (SIBO guidelines and UC guidelines) show the same pattern: ACG guideline published first with more strong recommendations; AGA published second with more conditional recommendations and more explicit uncertainty acknowledgment. This is a systematic institutional difference worth tracking across all US gastroenterology guidelines.
