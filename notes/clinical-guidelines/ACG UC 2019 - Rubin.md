# ACG Clinical Guideline: Ulcerative Colitis in Adults (Rubin et al., 2019)

The definitive US clinical practice guideline on UC from the American College of Gastroenterology, replacing the prior 2010 ACG UC guideline. Authored by leading IBD specialists with a dedicated GRADE methodologist (Sauer), this guideline introduces a unified severity classification, a treat-to-target framework with mucosal healing as the objective, and a treatment algorithm that selects therapy based on disease activity, extent, AND prognostic factors -- a departure from the traditional extent-only approach. It covers the full UC lifecycle: diagnosis, induction, maintenance, hospitalized severe disease, and colorectal cancer prevention. Of 49 recommendations, 14 are strong (mostly for well-established interventions like 5-ASA, anti-TNF, and avoidance of steroids for maintenance); the rest are conditional. The guideline was published just as tofacitinib received FDA approval (June 2018), making this the first major UC guideline to incorporate a JAK inhibitor. It predates ustekinumab's UC approval (October 2019) by 8 months.

---

## Diagnosis, Assessment, and Prognosis

### Claim 1: UC diagnosis requires endoscopic confirmation with histology and exclusion of infectious mimics -- C. difficile testing is mandatory, serologic antibody testing is useless

**Recommendation 1:** "We recommend stool testing to rule out Clostridioides difficile in patients suspected of having UC." Strong recommendation, very low quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 2:** "We recommend against serologic antibody testing to establish or rule out a diagnosis of UC." Strong recommendation, very low quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 3:** "We recommend against serologic antibody testing to determine the prognosis of UC." Strong recommendation, very low quality of evidence.

**Key Concept 1-3:** Suspect UC with hematochezia and urgency; exclude infectious etiologies at diagnosis; confirm with colonoscopy including ileal intubation and biopsies of affected and unaffected areas by a GI-trained pathologist.

**Evidence summary:** The prevalence of CDI among newly diagnosed or relapsing IBD ranges from 5% to 47%, with a 4-fold increase in mortality when CDI complicates UC. PCR and ELISA testing are standard. Comprehensive PCR-based multiplex pathogen panels are increasingly used, but C. diff remains the predominant infectious determinant of adverse outcomes. pANCA testing has pooled sensitivity too low for clinical utility, and no serologic panel predicts disease course with sufficient accuracy.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G5 (AGA UC 2020) similarly recommends infectious testing at diagnosis but does not make a specific C. diff recommendation. G9 (ECCO UC Therapeutics 2022) also emphasizes exclusion of enteric infections before diagnosing UC.

**My assessment:** These are three of the few "strong" recommendations with "very low" evidence quality -- a paradox that reflects clinical consensus trumping formal evidence: everyone agrees you should test for C. diff and skip pANCA, even though no RCT will ever randomize patients to "no C. diff testing." The strong/very-low combination is appropriately used for actions where the consequences of NOT doing them (missed C. diff with immunosuppression initiation) are catastrophic.

### Claim 2: Disease severity must incorporate four domains: patient-reported outcomes, inflammatory burden, disease course, and disease impact -- not just bowel frequency

**Key Concept 6:** "Definitions of disease severity are needed to guide treatment decisions; definitions should be based on (i) patient-reported outcomes (bleeding and normalization of bowel habits), (ii) inflammatory burden (endoscopic assessment including extent and severity and markers of inflammation), (iii) disease course (need for hospitalization, need for steroids, and failure to respond to medications), and (iv) disease impact (functionality and QoL)."

**Evidence summary:** The guideline proposes a new ACG UC Activity Index (Table 4) that stratifies into Remission, Mild, Moderate-Severe, and Fulminant using stools/day, blood in stools, urgency, hemoglobin, ESR/CRP, fecal calprotectin, endoscopic Mayo subscore, and UCEIS. The Truelove and Witts criteria (1955) remain the foundation for defining ASUC (≥6 bloody stools/day plus systemic toxicity). The guideline also endorses the UCEIS (validated 2012) over the Mayo endoscopic subscore for quantifying endoscopic severity, noting superior intra- and inter-observer reliability. Poor prognostic factors (Table 8) include: age <40 at diagnosis, extensive colitis, severe endoscopic disease (Mayo 3, UCEIS ≥7), hospitalization for colitis, elevated CRP, and low serum albumin.

**My assessment:** The four-domain severity concept is the guideline's most important conceptual advance. It explicitly says: a patient with "mild" current symptoms who was hospitalized last year and is steroid-dependent should be treated as moderate-severe. This closes a dangerous gap where rigid severity definitions let high-risk patients languish on step therapy. The UCEIS endorsement is forward-looking (most trials still use Mayo) but practically helpful for endoscopists.

## Goals for Managing UC

### Claim 3: Mucosal healing (Mayo endoscopic subscore 0 or 1) is the treatment target -- but histologic healing is not yet a validated endpoint

**Recommendation 4:** "We suggest treating patients with UC to achieve mucosal healing (defined as resolution of inflammatory changes (Mayo endoscopic subscore 0 or 1)) to increase the likelihood of sustained steroid-free remission and prevent hospitalizations and surgery." Conditional recommendation, low quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 5:** "We suggest fecal calprotectin as a surrogate for endoscopy when endoscopy is not feasible or available to assess for mucosal healing." Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.

**Key Concept 10:** Deep remission = symptomatic remission + endoscopic healing. Steroid-free remission is the minimum acceptable goal.

**Key Concept 13:** "Histological healing is associated with some improved clinical outcomes but has not yet been validated prospectively as an end point of treatment."

**Evidence summary:** The STRIDE consensus (2015) established composite endpoints (resolution of rectal bleeding + normalization of bowel habits + Mayo 0-1) as treatment targets. Endoscopic improvement (Mayo 0 or 1) rather than complete healing (Mayo 0) may be sufficient. FC pooled sensitivity for endoscopic inflammation in UC is 87.3%, specificity 77.1%, AUC 0.91 -- but optimum cutoffs vary widely (50-250 mg/g). Histologic inflammation (mucosal neutrophils) predicts clinical relapse and CRC risk, but no validated histologic index exists for clinical use.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G5 (AGA UC 2020) aligns strongly on mucosal healing as the treatment target and also demurs on histologic healing as a formal endpoint. G9 (ECCO 2022) goes further, endorsing histologic remission as an adjunctive target in selected patients (especially after biologic therapy), reflecting the European trend toward histologic endpoints. STRIDE-II (2021, postdating this guideline) elevated histologic healing from "not a target" to "an adjunctive measure of remission."

**My assessment:** The guideline's position on histologic healing was conservative but appropriate for 2019. Since publication, the evidence base has shifted significantly: multiple studies now correlate histologic remission with reduced relapse, hospitalization, and CRC risk. STRIDE-II (2021) partially closed this gap. The FC recommendation as a surrogate is pragmatic but the "very low quality of evidence" rating for R5 reflects that no prospective trial has used FC-guided treatment escalation as a primary strategy. This is a major evidence gap in 2019 that remains partially unfilled.

### Claim 4: Anxiety and depression screening should be routine in UC care

**Key Concept 17:** "Patients with UC should be screened for coexistent anxiety and depressive disorders, and when identified, patients should be provided with resources to address these conditions."

**Evidence summary:** Active UC is associated with higher rates of anxiety, depression, and impaired social/career function. The guideline cites Regueiro et al. (2017) on the bidirectional relationship between psychosocial comorbidities and IBD outcomes, but does not make a graded recommendation. This is appropriately left as a key concept since screening is a process measure, not a therapeutic intervention amenable to GRADE.

**My assessment:** This key concept was ahead of its time in a GI guideline and reflects Rubin's longstanding interest in the brain-gut axis. By 2024 standards it undersells the evidence: multiple studies now show that untreated depression/anxiety independently predicts IBD flares and healthcare utilization. The AGA's 2021 guideline on mental health in IBD would later strengthen this recommendation.

## Management of Mildly Active UC: Induction

### Claim 5: Rectal 5-ASA (1 g/d) is first-line for proctitis; combined oral + rectal 5-ASA is superior to oral alone for left-sided disease

**Recommendation 6:** "In patients with mildly active ulcerative proctitis, we recommend rectal 5-ASA therapies at a dose of 1 g/d for induction of remission." Strong recommendation, high quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 7:** "In patients with mildly active left-sided UC, we recommend rectal 5-ASA enemas at a dose of at least 1 g/d preferred over rectal steroids for induction of remission." Strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 8:** "In patients with mildly active left-sided UC, we suggest rectal 5-ASA enemas at a dose of at least 1 g/d combined with oral 5-ASA at a dose of at least 2 g/d compared with oral 5-ASA therapy alone for induction of remission." Conditional recommendation, low quality of evidence.

**Evidence summary:** Meta-analysis of 11 RCTs: 5-ASA superior to placebo for induction (RR failure to remit 0.79; NNT=6). For proctitis/left-sided: rectal 5-ASA pooled OR 8.30 for symptomatic remission, 5.31 for endoscopic remission vs placebo. Rectal 5-ASA superior to rectal steroids (OR 1.65). Combination oral + rectal 5-ASA: RR induction failure 0.65 (95% CI 0.47-0.91) vs oral alone. No significant differences by dose (1 g vs 4 g rectal) or formulation (liquid, gel, foam, suppository).

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G5 (AGA UC 2020) makes essentially identical recommendations: rectal 5-ASA for proctitis, combined for left-sided, oral for extensive. G9 (ECCO 2022) similarly endorses 5-ASA as first-line with combined therapy for distal disease.

**My assessment:** The "high quality of evidence" for R6 (rectal 5-ASA for proctitis) is one of only two high-quality ratings in the entire guideline -- a testament to the maturity of the 5-ASA evidence base after decades of use. The conditional rating for combination therapy (R8) is due to heterogeneity in the 4 RCTs rather than weak effect -- the NNT is compelling. The preference for 5-ASA over rectal steroids is well-supported, but the guideline appropriately notes rectal steroids remain an option for patients who cannot retain enemas or are 5-ASA intolerant.

### Claim 6: Once-daily 5-ASA dosing is as effective as split dosing; switching between 5-ASA formulations in nonresponders is futile

**Recommendation 13:** "In patients with mildly active UC of any extent, we suggest using a low dose (2-2.4 g/d) of 5-ASA compared with a higher dose (4.8 g/d), as there is no difference in the remission rate." Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 15:** "In patients with mildly to moderately active UC of any extent using 5-ASA to induce remission, we recommend either once-daily or more frequently dosed oral 5-ASA based on patient preference to optimize adherence, as efficacy and safety are no different." Strong recommendation, moderate quality evidence.

**Recommendation 12:** "In patients with mildly active UC who fail to reach remission with appropriately dosed 5-ASA (at least 2 g/d oral 5-ASA and/or at least 1 g/d rectal 5-ASA), we suggest against changing to an alternate 5-ASA formulation to induce remission. Alternative therapeutic classes should be considered." Conditional recommendation, low quality of evidence.

**Evidence summary:** Meta-analysis: low dose 2-2.4 g vs 4.8 g/d 5-ASA showed RR 0.91 (CI 0.85-0.98) -- the higher dose had a small but statistically significant advantage. Subgroup analysis suggested moderate-disease patients may benefit from 4.8 g/d. Once-daily vs split dosing: no efficacy difference (RR nonremission 0.95, CI 0.82-1.10). Community nonadherence rates reach 68% in patients on ≥4 prescription medications. No formal switch studies between 5-ASA formulations exist; meta-analyses show therapeutic equivalence.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G5 (AGA UC 2020) aligns on once-daily dosing and the futility of switching formulations. The low-vs-high dose recommendation is more controversial: the subgroup signal that moderate disease benefits from 4.8 g/d complicates the "low dose is fine" message.

**My assessment:** R15 (once-daily dosing) is among the most clinically actionable recommendations -- adherence in real-world practice is ~40%, and once-daily dosing demonstrably improves it. R12 (don't switch 5-ASA formulations) is the most important negative recommendation for mild UC: it prevents wasteful cycling through mesalamine brands when patients need escalation. R13 (low dose sufficient) has the weakest evidence quality and the most clinical tension -- many experts would push to 4.8 g/d before declaring 5-ASA failure.

### Claim 7: Budesonide MMX is preferred over systemic steroids for 5-ASA-refractory mild-moderate UC; systemic steroids are reserved for failure or severe disease

**Recommendation 9:** "In patients with mildly active left-sided UC who are intolerant or nonresponsive to oral and rectal 5-ASA at appropriate doses, we recommend oral budesonide MMX 9 mg/d for induction of remission." Strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 14:** "In patients with mildly to moderately active UC not responding to oral 5-ASA, we recommend the addition of budesonide MMX 9 mg/d to induce remission." Strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 11:** "In patients with UC of any extent who fail to respond to 5-ASA therapy, we recommend oral systemic corticosteroids to induce remission." Strong recommendation, low quality of evidence.

**Evidence summary:** CORE I trial: budesonide MMX 9 mg achieved combined clinical + endoscopic remission in 17.9% vs 7.4% placebo (P=0.0143). In 5-ASA-refractory patients: budesonide MMX + 5-ASA superior to 5-ASA + placebo (P=0.049). Systemic corticosteroids: meta-analysis RR failure to remit 0.65 (0.45-0.93) vs placebo. Typical prednisone starting dose: 40-60 mg/d; no benefit beyond 60 mg/d. Response expected within 5-7 days.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G5 (AGA UC 2020) similarly positions budesonide MMX before systemic steroids. G9 (ECCO 2022) endorses the same sequencing but also discusses beclomethasone dipropionate (not available in the US) as an alternative topical steroid.

**My assessment:** The sequencing of budesonide MMX before prednisone is a major step forward from the 2010 guideline. The high first-pass metabolism of budesonide means dramatically fewer steroid side effects -- a critical advantage for the predominantly young UC population. However, the absolute remission rate (17.9%) is modest; many patients will still need prednisone. The guideline appropriately separates mild-moderate (budesonide MMX OK) from severe (systemic steroids needed).

## Management of Mildly Active UC: Maintenance

### Claim 8: 5-ASA maintenance therapy works; corticosteroids are contraindicated for maintenance

**Recommendation 16:** "In patients with mildly active ulcerative proctitis, we recommend rectal 5-ASA at a dose of 1 g/d to maintain remission." Strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 17:** "In patients with mildly active left-sided or extensive UC, we recommend oral 5-ASA therapy (at least 2 g/d) for maintenance of remission." Strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 18:** "We recommend against systemic corticosteroids for maintenance of remission in patients with UC." Strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence.

**Evidence summary:** Meta-analysis of 11 trials: oral 5-ASA RR of relapse 0.65 (0.55-0.76) vs placebo. High-to-standard dose (≥2 g/d) superior to low dose (<2 g/d): RR relapse 0.79 (0.64-0.97). Topical 5-ASA RR relapse 0.60 (0.49-0.73) vs placebo. Corticosteroids are ineffective for maintenance and carry significant cumulative toxicity; the guideline emphasizes the shortest possible steroid course with early initiation of steroid-sparing therapy.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G5 and G9 both strongly recommend against steroids for maintenance and endorse 5-ASA maintenance. No divergence.

**My assessment:** The steroid maintenance recommendation is appropriately strong -- this is settled medicine since the 1960s. The more nuanced question the guideline sidesteps: what to do with patients who repeatedly flare when 5-ASA is stopped? The "lifelong 5-ASA" paradigm has adherents and skeptics; the guideline suggests indefinite maintenance for most but doesn't define discontinuation criteria.

## Management of Moderately to Severely Active UC: Induction

### Claim 9: Anti-TNF therapy (infliximab, adalimumab, golimumab) is high-quality evidence for induction; combination therapy with infliximab + thiopurine is superior to monotherapy

**Recommendation 22:** "In patients with moderately to severely active UC, we recommend anti-TNF therapy using adalimumab, golimumab, or infliximab for induction of remission." Strong recommendation, high quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 24:** "When infliximab is used as induction therapy for patients with moderately to severely active UC, we recommend combination therapy with a thiopurine." Strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence for azathioprine.

**Key Concept 23:** "Robust data on combination anti-TNF and immunomodulator therapy in moderately to severely active UC exist only for infliximab and thiopurines."

**Evidence summary:** All three anti-TNF agents demonstrated superiority over placebo in registration trials (ACT-1/2 for infliximab, ULTRA-2 for adalimumab, PURSUIT for golimumab). Network meta-analyses show a non-significant trend toward higher remission with infliximab vs adalimumab or golimumab, but no head-to-head trials exist. The UC-SUCCESS trial: combination infliximab + azathioprine superior to either monotherapy for steroid-free remission at 16 weeks. Observational data: adalimumab associated with higher hospitalization rates vs infliximab in Danish cohort. Methotrexate combination data is extrapolated from CD; no direct UC evidence.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G5 (AGA UC 2020) makes the same strong recommendation for anti-TNF therapy. The combination therapy recommendation is infliximab-specific in both guidelines. G9 (ECCO 2022) also endorses combination therapy but discusses the growing role of vedolizumab and tofacitinib as alternatives, potentially before anti-TNF.

**My assessment:** The "high quality of evidence" rating for anti-TNF induction (one of only two in the entire guideline) reflects the large, well-designed phase 3 programs. However, the absence of head-to-head trials means the choice among anti-TNF agents remains largely based on patient preference (subcutaneous vs IV), payer formulary, and provider familiarity. The combination therapy recommendation is strong for infliximab but the guideline's silence on whether adalimumab or golimumab should also be combined with an immunomodulator is a notable gap -- most experts extrapolate from the infliximab and CD data and recommend combination for all anti-TNF agents.

### Claim 10: Thiopurines and methotrexate are NOT effective for induction -- do not use as monotherapy in active disease

**Recommendation 21:** "In patients with moderately to severely active UC, we recommend against monotherapy with thiopurines or methotrexate for induction of remission." Strong recommendation, low quality of evidence.

**Evidence summary:** Thiopurines are slow-acting (8-12 weeks to onset) and do not induce remission in active UC. The European METEOR trial: parenteral methotrexate 25 mg/week showed numerically higher steroid-free remission at week 16 but did not reach statistical significance vs placebo. Two meta-analyses of methotrexate induction were negative.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G5 and G9 both agree: thiopurines and methotrexate are maintenance agents, not induction agents. This is uncontroversial.

**My assessment:** This is a "strong" recommendation that should have been stronger in the previous guideline. The persistence of thiopurine monotherapy for active UC in community practice represents guideline nonadherence. The "low quality" rating reflects the small, older trials rather than equipoise about efficacy -- we know these drugs don't work fast enough for active disease.

### Claim 11: Vedolizumab and tofacitinib are effective for induction, including in anti-TNF-exposed patients; both earn strong recommendations

**Recommendation 25:** "In patients with moderately to severely active UC, we recommend vedolizumab for induction of remission." Strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 26:** "In patients with moderately to severely active UC who have previously failed anti-TNF therapy, we recommend vedolizumab for induction of remission." Strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 27:** "In patients with moderately to severely active UC, we recommend tofacitinib 10 mg orally b.i.d. for 8 weeks to induce remission." Strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 28:** "In patients with moderately to severely active UC who have previously failed anti-TNF therapy, we recommend tofacitinib for induction of remission." Strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence.

**Evidence summary:** GEMINI 1: vedolizumab week 6 clinical remission 16.9% vs 5.4% placebo (P=0.001); mucosal healing 40.9% vs 24.8%. Post-hoc analysis confirmed efficacy in anti-TNF failures. OCTAVE 1 and 2: tofacitinib 10 mg BID week 8 remission 18.5% and 16.6% vs 8.2% and 3.6% placebo. Half of OCTAVE patients had prior anti-TNF failure. Herpes zoster: 5.1% with tofacitinib 10 mg BID vs 0.5% placebo. The guideline makes no recommendations about sequencing among these agents due to absence of head-to-head trials.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** This is where G2 (ACG 2019) differs most from G5 (AGA 2020). ACG makes strong recommendations for both vedolizumab and tofacitinib without preferring one over anti-TNF. AGA (2020) uses conditional recommendations and explicitly recommends infliximab or vedolizumab BEFORE tofacitinib in anti-TNF-naive patients, citing the JAK inhibitor's safety profile. G9 (ECCO 2022) similarly places tofacitinib after biologic failure. The ACG's equipoise reflects the timing -- these authors wrote the guideline as OCTAVE data were fresh and before the post-marketing safety signals (thrombosis, MACE) that led to FDA black-box warnings in 2021.

**My assessment:** The "strong" ratings for vedolizumab and tofacitinib in anti-TNF-naive patients were aggressive for 2019 and would likely be softened today. The tofacitinib recommendation in particular has aged poorly: the FDA's 2021 safety warning (increased risk of MACE, thrombosis, malignancy with JAK inhibitors in the ORAL Surveillance trial in RA patients) has pushed tofacitinib later in treatment algorithms. This is not a flaw in the guideline -- it reflects the evidence available in 2019 -- but it underscores the rapid evolution of the advanced therapy landscape.

## Management of Moderately to Severely Active UC: Maintenance

### Claim 12: Continue the agent that induced remission; corticosteroids, methotrexate, and 5-ASA have no maintenance role after advanced therapy induction

**Recommendation 30:** "In patients with previously moderately to severely active UC who have achieved remission but previously failed 5-ASA therapy and are now on anti-TNF therapy, we recommend against using concomitant 5-ASA for efficacy of maintenance of remission." Conditional recommendation, low quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 31:** "We recommend against systemic corticosteroids for maintenance of remission in patients with UC." Strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 32:** "For patients with previously moderately to severely active UC now in remission due to corticosteroid induction, we suggest thiopurines for maintenance of remission compared with no treatment or corticosteroids." Conditional recommendation, low quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 33:** "In patients with previously moderately to severely active UC now in remission, we recommend against using methotrexate for maintenance of remission." Conditional recommendation, low quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 34-36:** Continue anti-TNF, vedolizumab, or tofacitinib for maintenance after induction with the respective agent. All strong, moderate quality.

**Key Concept 30:** "Most clinical trials and available data demonstrate a benefit of using the steroid-sparing therapy that induces remission to maintain that remission."

**Evidence summary:** The MERIT-UC trial (2018): parenteral methotrexate 25 mg/week not superior to placebo for maintenance after steroid induction (66% relapse vs 63%). Thiopurines: meta-analysis shows RR 0.6 (0.37-0.95) for preventing relapse after steroid induction; NNT=5. GEMINI 1 maintenance: vedolizumab every 8 weeks maintained remission in 40% vs 16% placebo at week 52. OCTAVE Sustain: tofacitinib 10 mg BID maintained remission in 40.6% vs 11% placebo at week 52.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G5 (AGA 2020) aligns on all points. The thiopurine maintenance recommendation is notably conditional and based on steroid-induced remission specifically -- the guideline does not recommend thiopurines as maintenance after biologic induction (unlike in CD). The methotrexate recommendation against use is consistent across all guidelines. G9 (ECCO 2022) offers slightly more support for thiopurine monotherapy in selected patients.

**My assessment:** The MERIT-UC trial (published just months before this guideline) definitively closed the door on methotrexate for UC -- a finding that separates UC from CD where methotrexate has a clear maintenance role. The recommendation against concomitant 5-ASA with biologics (R30) is cost-conscious and evidence-based: a pooled analysis of clinical trial data showed no added benefit. The conditional recommendation for thiopurine monotherapy after steroid induction acknowledges the limited evidence while preserving an option for patients who cannot access or decline biologics.

## Therapeutic Drug Monitoring and Loss of Response

### Claim 13: Measure drug levels and antibodies in anti-TNF loss of response; primary nonresponders with adequate drug levels should switch mechanism, not cycle within class

**Recommendation 29:** "In patients with moderately to severely active UC who are responders to anti-TNF therapy and now losing response, we suggest measuring serum drug levels and antibodies (if there is not a therapeutic level) to assess the reason for loss of response." Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.

**Key Concept 24:** "Patients who are primary nonresponders to an anti-TNF (defined as lack of therapeutic benefit after induction despite adequate drug levels) should be evaluated and considered for alternative mechanisms of disease control (e.g., in a different class of therapy) rather than cycling to another drug within the anti-TNF class."

**Key Concept 25:** "In patients with moderately to severely active UC who had an initial response but subsequently lost efficacy to one anti-TNF therapy, we recommend alternative anti-TNF therapy (but not the biosimilar to the original brand) compared with no treatment for induction of remission."

**Evidence summary:** Up to 20% of patients are primary nonresponders to anti-TNF; an additional 10-15% lose response annually. Causes include: inadequate drug levels (increased clearance from high inflammatory burden, fecal drug loss, neutralizing antibodies), concurrent infection, or mechanistic failure. The approach integrates clinical evaluation, objective inflammation confirmation (endoscopy, CRP, FC), exclusion of enteric infections, and therapeutic drug monitoring.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G5 (AGA 2020) makes a stronger recommendation for therapeutic drug monitoring in active disease. G9 (ECCO 2022) has the most detailed algorithm for reactive TDM. The distinction between primary nonresponse (switch class) and secondary loss of response (optimize or switch within class) is consistent across all guidelines.

**My assessment:** The "very low quality of evidence" for R29 is honest -- no prospective RCT randomizes patients to TDM-guided vs empiric dose escalation in UC (though such trials exist in CD). The conceptual framework (primary nonresponse = mechanism failure; secondary LOR = pharmacokinetic problem) is sound and clinically useful. The recommendation against using a biosimilar to the failed originator (KC 25) is obvious -- they're the same molecule -- but worth stating given physician confusion about biosimilars.

## Acute Severe UC: Hospital Management

### Claim 14: IV methylprednisolone 60 mg/d or hydrocortisone 100 mg TID-QID for 3-5 days is the standard; nonresponders need rescue therapy or surgery -- do not delay

**Recommendation 42:** "In patients with ASUC, we recommend a total of 60 mg/d of methylprednisolone or hydrocortisone 100 mg 3 or 4 times per day to induce remission." Strong recommendation, low quality of evidence.

**Key Concept 34:** Flexible sigmoidoscopy within 72 hours (preferably 24 hours) of admission to assess severity and biopsy for CMV.

**Key Concept 38:** "In patients failing to adequately respond to medical therapy by 3-5 days or with suspected toxicity, surgical consultation should be obtained."

**Evidence summary:** ASUC defined as ≥6 bloody stools/day plus systemic toxicity (Truelove and Witts). Up to 25% of UC patients develop ASUC; 40% require colectomy. IV steroid response rate: 67% (meta-analysis of 1,948 patients). No benefit to doses above methylprednisolone 60 mg/d. Response plateau at 3-5 days; additional response after 7 days unlikely. The Oxford index: >8 stools on day 3 OR 3-8 stools + CRP >45 mg/L predicted colectomy in 85%. UCEIS ≥7 had higher PPV for colectomy than Mayo endoscopic score 3. CMV affects up to one-third of steroid-refractory ASUC; biopsies from ulcer bases with immunohistochemistry are the diagnostic standard. Delayed surgery associated with increased postoperative complications.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G5 and G9 both endorse the 3-5 day IV steroid window with early surgical consultation. The Oxford index is cited by all. The CMV evaluation recommendation varies slightly -- G9 is more aggressive about empiric ganciclovir in refractory cases.

**My assessment:** The ASUC section is the guideline's most protocolized and practically useful. The 3-5 day steroid window with mandatory surgical consultation at day 3 for nonresponders is the single most important clinical decision rule in UC -- missing this window increases postoperative mortality. The strong recommendation for VTE prophylaxis (R37) with low-quality evidence reflects the 2-3 fold increased VTE risk in hospitalized IBD patients, which persists despite active bleeding. The "against TPN for bowel rest" recommendation (R41) is appropriately strong given two negative RCTs.

### Claim 15: Both infliximab and cyclosporine are effective rescue therapies with similar outcomes; choice depends on provider experience and patient-specific factors

**Recommendation 43:** "In patients with ASUC failing to adequately respond to intravenous corticosteroids by 3-5 days we recommend medical rescue therapy with infliximab or cyclosporine." Strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence.

**Key Concept 39:** "The choice between infliximab and cyclosporine should be based on provider experience with the agent, history of previous failure of immunomodulator or anti-TNF therapy, and serum albumin."

**Evidence summary:** The CYSIF trial (Laharie 2012, n=115): treatment failure at day 98 was 60% (cyclosporine) vs 54% (infliximab), non-significant difference. Long-term follow-up: 5-year colectomy-free survival 61.5% (cyclosporine) vs 65.1% (infliximab), P=0.97. CONSTRUCT trial (n=270): no differences in quality-adjusted survival, colectomy, time to colectomy, or adverse events. ENEIDA registry (n=740): similar efficacy; lower adverse events with cyclosporine. Cyclosporine dose: 2 mg/kg (not 4 mg/kg -- similar efficacy, fewer adverse events). Target levels 200-400 ng/mL. Infliximab in ASUC: RCT (Jarnerot 2005) showed 29% vs 67% colectomy at 3 months (P=0.017). Accelerated infliximab dosing may improve short-term colectomy rates but not long-term outcomes. Post-hoc analysis: infliximab favored in patients with albumin <23 g/L.

**Key Concept 41:** "Infliximab and cyclosporine do not increase postoperative complications of colectomy, and surgery should not be deferred based on this exposure."

**Evidence summary (cont.):** Sequential salvage (cyclosporine → infliximab or vice versa): colectomy rate ~60% at 12 months, severe adverse events in 16% including 1 death. Calcineurin inhibitor + vedolizumab bridge: case series of 11 patients, 55% steroid-free remission at week 14, 45% at 1 year.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G5 (AGA 2020) is less prescriptive, recommending either infliximab or cyclosporine without preference. G9 (ECCO 2022) provides the most detailed rescue therapy algorithm, including considerations for accelerated infliximab dosing and the calcineurin-vedolizumab bridge.

**My assessment:** The CYSIF and CONSTRUCT trials provide the best head-to-head data in all of UC guideline-land and justify the equipoise between infliximab and cyclosporine. However, real-world practice heavily favors infliximab due to provider familiarity and the simplicity of continuing it as maintenance. Cyclosporine use is concentrated at a handful of expert centers. The guideline's advice to choose based on "provider experience" is pragmatic but effectively means most US gastroenterologists will use infliximab. The calcineurin-vedolizumab bridge data (KC 46, R46) is the most innovative ASUC strategy -- using cyclosporine or tacrolimus for rapid induction while vedolizumab's slower onset takes over -- but the evidence is limited to small case series.

## Colorectal Cancer Prevention

### Claim 16: Screening colonoscopy starts at 8 years after diagnosis (immediately if PSC); surveillance intervals 1-3 years based on risk factors; high-definition white-light or chromoendoscopy are both acceptable

**Recommendation 47:** "We suggest colonoscopic screening and surveillance to identify neoplasia in patients with UC of any extent beyond the rectum." Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 48:** "When using standard-definition colonoscopes in patients with UC undergoing surveillance, we recommend dye spray chromoendoscopy with methylene blue or indigo carmine to identify dysplasia." Strong recommendation, low quality of evidence.

**Recommendation 49:** "When using high-definition colonoscopes in patients with UC undergoing surveillance, we suggest white-light endoscopy with narrow-band imaging or dye spray chromoendoscopy with methylene blue or indigo carmine to identify dysplasia." Conditional recommendation, low quality of evidence.

**Key Concept 44:** Surveillance at 1-3 year intervals based on combined risk factors and previous findings.

**Key Concept 47:** "It is unclear whether segmental random biopsies are still required during surveillance colonoscopy in UC."

**Evidence summary:** Cumulative CRC risk in UC: 1% at 10 years, 2% at 20 years, 5% beyond 20 years. St. Mark's surveillance program: CRC incidence 4.7 per 1,000 patient-years. Risk declining over time. CRC risk factors: disease duration, inflammatory activity, young age at diagnosis, extensive colitis, PSC, family history of CRC. No prospective study demonstrates mortality benefit from surveillance. A Cochrane analysis of 3 small studies found no significant mortality reduction. For PSC-UC: annual surveillance starting at diagnosis. Dye spray chromoendoscopy detects more dysplasia than standard-definition white light. With high-definition scopes: a 2018 RCT (Bisschops) showed similar detection between HD-NBI and HD-chromoendoscopy.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** The SCENIC consensus (2015) recommended chromoendoscopy over white-light for all surveillance -- a stronger position than R49. G5 (AGA 2020) similarly suggests either HD-white-light or chromoendoscopy. G9 (ECCO 2022) recommends chromoendoscopy as preferred but allows HD-white-light as an alternative. The 8-year screening start is consistent across guidelines. The 1-3 year surveillance interval is broader than earlier guidelines (which often said 1-2 years for extensive disease) and reflects risk-stratification principles.

**My assessment:** This section navigates the tension between the SCENIC consensus (strong chromoendoscopy recommendation) and practical realities (limited adoption, time burden, emerging HD data). The conditional recommendation for R49 effectively says: "if you have HD scopes, use them however you prefer; if you have SD scopes, you must use dye spray." This is a reasonable compromise. The acknowledgement that random biopsies may no longer be necessary (KC 47) reflects a major shift -- the "DALM" era of mandatory random biopsies and automatic colectomy for any dysplasia is over. The guideline's endorsement of endoscopic resection and surveillance (rather than automatic colectomy) for discrete, fully resected dysplastic lesions is the most important clinical change in the CRC section.

### Claim 17: No medication has proven chemopreventive efficacy to replace colonoscopic surveillance; 5-ASA and thiopurine chemoprevention data are inconsistent

**Key Concept 52:** "No medical therapy has demonstrated sufficient prevention of dysplasia or CRC to avoid colonoscopic surveillance in UC."

**Evidence summary:** Case-control and cohort studies suggest protective effects of 5-ASA and thiopurines for dysplasia/CRC, but results are inconsistent, and effect sizes diminish when adjusted for degree of inflammation. The guideline explicitly rejects the notion that medication can substitute for colonoscopic surveillance.

**My assessment:** This is an important negative statement. Some clinicians have argued that patients in deep remission on biologics might need less frequent surveillance. The guideline rejects this: inflammation control may reduce risk but does not eliminate it, and surveillance remains mandatory.

## Surgical Considerations

### Claim 18: Colectomy should be offered early for refractory disease; restorative proctocolectomy with IPAA is standard; staged procedures reduce complications

**Key Concept 32:** "We suggest elective proctocolectomy in patients with UC failing maximal medical management."

**Key Concept 27:** "Obtain consultation with a surgeon and consider colectomy in patients with moderately to severely active UC who are refractory or intolerant to medical therapy."

**Evidence summary:** Indications for colectomy: ASUC refractory to medical therapy, chronic refractory UC, or dysplasia/carcinoma. Early postoperative complications: 9-65%. Late complications: 17-55%. Overall mortality: 1%. Preoperative corticosteroids increase infectious risk. Anti-TNF does not increase postoperative complications in UC (unlike CD where the data are mixed). Preliminary data: vedolizumab does not increase postoperative infectious complications. Staged IPAA: initial subtotal colectomy with end ileostomy, followed by pouch creation after nutritional optimization and steroid weaning.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G5 and G9 both endorse early surgical consultation. The staged approach recommendation is universal. G9 provides more detailed discussion of pouch surveillance after IPAA.

**My assessment:** The guideline appropriately frames surgery as a therapeutic option, not a failure. The key surgical messages: (1) don't delay surgery in ASUC -- it increases complications; (2) staged procedures in the sick patient reduce morbidity; (3) biologic exposure does not meaningfully increase surgical risk in UC. Pouchitis management (a common post-IPAA complication) is not addressed in this guideline, reflecting the medical focus of the document. This is a notable gap -- the ECCO guidelines include pouchitis management separately.

**Overall GRADE assessment:** Of 49 recommendations, 14 are strong (29%) and 35 are conditional (71%). Only 2 recommendations have "high" quality evidence (R6: rectal 5-ASA for proctitis; R22: anti-TNF for induction). The overwhelming majority cluster at "moderate" or "low" quality. This is not a weakness of the guideline -- it reflects the UC evidence base, which has many effective therapies but few large, rigorous comparative-effectiveness trials. The 54 key concept statements fill the GRADE gap, functioning as expert-consensus recommendations where formal grading is impossible.

**Strongest sections:**
1. **ASUC management** (R37-R46, KC 33-41): Protocolized, evidence-based, and directly actionable. The 3-5 day steroid window, Oxford index, CYSIF/CONSTRUCT data, and surgical triggers are the standard of care.
2. **5-ASA dosing and maintenance** (R6-R18): Decades of data consolidated into clear, practical recommendations. The once-daily dosing and anti-formulation-switching messages directly improve care.

**Weakest sections:**
1. **Diet and complementary therapies** (KC 20-21): Acknowledges FMT, probiotics, and curcumin as needing more study but provides no guidance. This is honest but leaves clinicians without answers for the most common patient questions.
2. **Pouchitis management:** Completely absent. Post-IPAA pouchitis affects up to 50% of patients and is a major gap in a comprehensive UC guideline.
3. **Sequencing of advanced therapies:** The guideline makes strong recommendations for individual agents but offers no algorithm for choosing among anti-TNF, vedolizumab, and tofacitinib. The key concept about "organ-selective before systemic" is suggestive but not prescriptive.

**Cross-reference priorities:**
- **G5 (AGA UC 2020 - Feuerstein):** Compare directly. Both US guidelines from consecutive years. Major difference: AGA uses conditional recommendations and explicitly sequences infliximab/vedolizumab before tofacitinib; ACG makes strong recommendations for all three. AGA additionally covers 5-ASA dosing, rectal therapy, and scope of monitoring in more granular detail.
- **G9 (ECCO UC Therapeutics 2022 - Raine):** The most current international guideline. Will differ on: (a) tofacitinib positioning (more conservative post-FDA warning), (b) inclusion of ustekinumab (not in ACG 2019), (c) histologic healing as an adjunctive target, (d) more detailed therapeutic drug monitoring algorithms, and (e) pouchitis management.
- **STRIDE-II (2021):** Directly updates the treatment targets from STRIDE-I cited in this guideline. Key change: histologic remission elevated from non-target to adjunctive measure.
- **SCENIC (2015):** The dysplasia detection consensus that this guideline partially walks back (allowing HD-WLE as equivalent to chromoendoscopy).

**Most controversial claims:**
1. **Strong recommendations for tofacitinib in anti-TNF-naive patients (R27):** Contested by AGA 2020 and ECCO 2022. Post-2021 safety data would likely downgrade this.
2. **Low-dose 5-ASA sufficient (R13):** The subgroup signal for moderate disease benefiting from 4.8 g/d, combined with very low evidence quality, makes this actively debated.
3. **Conditional surveillance recommendation (R47):** Given that surveillance colonoscopy is standard of care in every IBD center, a "very low quality" conditional recommendation feels incongruous -- but accurately reflects the absence of prospective mortality data.

**Historical significance:** This guideline marks the transition from the biologic era (anti-TNF only) to the advanced therapy era (vedolizumab, tofacitinib, and imminently ustekinumab). It was the last major UC guideline published before the JAK inhibitor safety controversy and before ustekinumab's UC approval. As such, it captures a specific moment in the therapeutic timeline -- one where optimism about small molecules was at its peak and before the safety concerns that would reshape treatment algorithms.
