# ECCO Guidelines on Therapeutics in Ulcerative Colitis: Medical Treatment (Raine et al., 2022)

The most current international guideline on medical treatment of UC, authored by a 33-expert panel spanning 20+ countries with six patient representatives as full voting members. Published in the Journal of Crohn's and Colitis in 2022 (online October 2021), this guideline replaces the prior ECCO UC guidelines and is the first major UC guideline to incorporate ustekinumab, the first to issue a head-to-head recommendation (vedolizumab over adalimumab from the VARSITY trial), and the first to address the emerging JAK inhibitor safety signals from the ORAL Surveillance trial in rheumatoid arthritis. The guideline uses GRADE methodology across 21 recommendations derived from PICO questions, with meta-analyses conducted de novo by the panel's methodologists. A companion guideline covers surgical treatment and hospitalized severe UC — this document focuses exclusively on outpatient medical management. Key European distinctives: vedolizumab positioned before anti-TNF based on VARSITY, stronger emphasis on topical 5-ASA, detailed tofacitinib safety discussion reflecting EMA regulatory actions, and explicit acknowledgment that therapeutic drug monitoring lacks prospective evidence for a formal recommendation.

## General Approach to UC Management

### Claim 1: Disease severity is a continuum; treatment is stratified by both severity and extent — but trial populations and real-world patients differ fundamentally

**Author's claim:** "Attempts to define disease severity are widely used in setting clinical trial inclusion criteria and can be measured according to several different definitions. Trial populations will inevitably vary, and we reflect the continuum of disease severity by having the moderate disease category span both broad categories."

**Evidence presented:** The guideline divides recommendations into two working groups: mildly-to-moderately active disease and moderately-to-severely active disease, with explicit acknowledgment that these divisions are "somewhat arbitrary, partially overlapping, and inconsistently defined." Disease extent is classified using the Montreal system: proctitis (rectum only), left-sided UC (distal to splenic flexure), extensive UC (proximal to splenic flexure). The panel notes that disease distribution can change over time and proximal extension is a negative prognostic marker.

**Confidence:** HIGH. The continuum model accurately reflects clinical reality and avoids the false precision of rigid severity scores. The acknowledgment that trial populations differ from clinic patients is honest and important.

**What's at stake:** Treatment decisions based on rigid severity definitions can undertreat high-risk patients with "mild" current symptoms or overtreat patients with a single severe episode but limited cumulative burden. The continuum model pushes clinicians toward individualized assessment.

**Who disagrees:** No formal disagreement — the continuum concept is widely accepted. However, payers and formularies often require strict severity definitions (e.g., Mayo score cutoffs) for biologic approval, creating tension between guideline philosophy and real-world access.

**Alternative reading:** The ACG 2019 guideline addresses this differently — by proposing a unified four-domain severity classification (PROs, inflammatory burden, disease course, disease impact) rather than simply acknowledging a continuum. The ACG approach is more prescriptive; the ECCO approach is more descriptive.

**My assessment:** The continuum model is intellectually honest but operationally vague. The ACG's four-domain framework gives clinicians more concrete guidance for identifying high-risk patients who need early escalation. ECCO's approach risks leaving the decision entirely to clinician judgment, which may disadvantage patients whose physicians are less aggressive about escalation.

### Claim 2: The ultimate treatment goal is health-related quality of life and avoidance of disability — endoscopic healing is the key intermediate target, with histologic healing emerging

**Author's claim:** "The ultimate goal of treatment in UC is to maintain health-related quality of life [QoL] and avoid disability. To achieve this, it is important to not only provide rapid relief of clinical symptoms, but also achieve endoscopic healing where possible, as this is associated with improved long-term outcomes."

**Evidence presented:** The endorsement of STRIDE-II (Turner et al., 2021) is notable — this is the 2021 update that elevated histologic healing from "not a target" to "an adjunctive measure of remission," a significant evolution from STRIDE-I (2015) which the ACG 2019 guideline cited. The panel selected endoscopic and clinical outcomes as being of "critical importance" in their GRADE process.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G2 (ACG 2019) set mucosal healing (Mayo 0 or 1) as the treatment target but explicitly rejected histologic healing as a validated endpoint. G5 (AGA 2020) aligned with ACG. ECCO is the only one of these three to reference STRIDE-II and its histologic healing upgrade, reflecting the guideline's later publication date.

**My assessment:** The reference to STRIDE-II is an important temporal advantage — this guideline could incorporate evidence that was unavailable when ACG and AGA were written. However, the guideline doesn't make histologic healing a formal recommendation; it simply notes the shift. The gap between "endoscopic healing is the target" and "histologic healing is emerging" will likely close in the next ECCO update.

## Mild-to-Moderately Active UC: Induction

### Claim 3: Oral 5-ASA ≥2 g/d induces remission with low-quality evidence but strong recommendation; topical 5-ASA ≥1 g/d is strongly recommended for distal colitis; combined oral + rectal is weakly recommended over oral monotherapy

**Recommendation 1:** "We recommend 5-aminosalicylates at a dose of ≥2 g/day [d] to induce remission in patients with mildly-to-moderately active UC [strong recommendation; quality of evidence low]"

**Recommendation 2:** "We recommend topical [rectal] 5-ASA at a dose of ≥1 g/d for the induction of remission in active distal colitis [strong recommendation, low-quality evidence]"

**Recommendation 3:** "We suggest the use of oral 5-ASA [≥2 g/d] combined with topical [rectal] 5-ASA over oral 5-ASA monotherapy for induction of remission in adult patients with active UC of at least rectosigmoid extent [weak recommendation; very low-quality evidence]"

**Evidence summary:** Meta-analysis of 11 RCTs (n=2,156): oral 5-ASA RR 1.56 (95% CI 1.24-1.97) for clinical remission, RR 1.58 (1.35-1.86) for clinical response. Endoscopic response in 4 RCTs (n=416): RR 1.73 (1.0-3.0). SAEs: 6.1% vs 9% placebo. For topical 5-ASA, meta-analysis of 8 studies (≥1 g/d for 2-8 weeks): clinical remission RR 3.56 (2.08-6.09), clinical response RR 2.46 (2.01-3.01), endoscopic response RR 2.75 (2.04-3.7). For combination therapy: 4 trials (n=322), clinical remission RR 1.45 (0.98-2.13) — not statistically significant but trend favored combination. Serious inconsistency (I²=71%) and risk of bias present. The guideline's own Cochrane reference confirms no apparent differences between 5-ASA formulations and no overall evidence for superior efficacy of higher total daily doses across dose-ranging trials.

**Key nuance on dosing:** The ASCEND trials' subgroup analysis suggested benefit of 4.8 g/day over 2.4 g/day in patients with more active disease or prior corticosteroid/5-ASA/rectal therapy use, and greater mucosal healing at 4.8 g/day. But this was not seen in MMX 5-ASA trials. The guideline does NOT make a formal low-vs-high dose recommendation — unlike ACG 2019 which conditionally recommends low-dose (2-2.4 g/d) as sufficient.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G2 (ACG 2019) makes similar recommendations but with an explicit 2-2.4 g/d "low dose is sufficient" conditional recommendation (R13) — a significant divergence. G5 (AGA 2020) uses ≥2 g/d for extensive and ≥1 g/d rectal, aligning more closely with ECCO. The European convention of ≥2 g/d as the standard dose (without a "low dose sufficient" qualifier) reflects higher baseline 5-ASA dosing in European practice. The combination therapy recommendation is consistently weak/conditional across all guidelines — the evidence simply isn't strong enough for a firm recommendation despite clinical logic.

**My assessment:** ECCO's refusal to make a "low dose is sufficient" recommendation is clinically significant. In European practice, most clinicians start at ≥3-4 g/d for active disease and the ≥2 g/d floor in R1 is genuinely a floor, not a target. The ACG's R13 (2-2.4 g/d sufficient) would be considered undertreatment by many European experts. The combination therapy recommendation, while weak, carries more weight in the ECCO document because of the stronger European tradition of topical therapy — enemas and suppositories are more culturally accepted and prescribed in Europe than in the US.

### Claim 4: Topical 5-ASA is superior to topical steroids for clinical remission; topical steroids remain an option with very low-quality evidence

**Recommendation 4:** "We recommend using topical [rectal] steroids for the induction of remission in patients with active distal colitis [strong recommendation, very low-quality evidence]"

**Recommendation 5:** "We suggest treatment with topical [rectal] 5-ASAs over topical [rectal] steroids for induction of remission in patients with active distal colitis [weak recommendation, very low quality of evidence]"

**Evidence summary:** Topical steroids vs placebo meta-analysis of 5 RCTs: clinical remission RR 2.12 (1.48-3.06), clinical response RR 2.18 (1.58-3.01), endoscopic response RR 1.44 (1.21-1.70). Topical 5-ASA vs topical steroids (13 studies, n=1,395): clinical remission RR 1.36 (1.19-1.56) favoring 5-ASA, but clinical response RR 1.09 (0.97-1.22) and endoscopic response RR 1.08 (0.82-1.44) were not significantly different. SAEs did not differ.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G2 (ACG 2019) also prefers rectal 5-ASA over rectal steroids (R7, strong, moderate quality). The ECCO quality rating of "very low" for these recommendations is notably lower than ACG's "moderate" — reflecting the ECCO methodologists' stricter assessment. Both guidelines agree on the clinical hierarchy: 5-ASA first, steroids as alternative.

**My assessment:** The simultaneous strong recommendation for topical steroids (R4) and weak recommendation for 5-ASA over steroids (R5) creates a tension: if 5-ASA is superior, why is the recommendation to prefer it only "weak"? The answer lies in GRADE mechanics — the very low quality of evidence for the comparative question and the panel's acknowledgment of patient acceptability issues (some patients cannot retain enemas, prefer foam, or have 5-ASA intolerance). The guideline appropriately preserves topical steroids as a legitimate option.

### Claim 5: Budesonide MMX 9 mg/d is appropriate for 5-ASA-refractory mild-moderate UC but has no maintenance role; is not recommended for extensive or severe disease

**Recommendation 6:** "We suggest the use of colonic-release corticosteroids for induction of remission in patients with active mild-to-moderate UC [weak recommendation, low quality of evidence]"

**Evidence summary:** Three RCTs (n=542), 8-week follow-up: budesonide MMX superior to placebo for clinical remission (RR 2.86; 1.62-5.04), clinical response (RR 1.46; 1.11-1.93), and endoscopic response (RR 1.43; 1.10-1.84). Pooled phase 3 analysis: combined clinical + endoscopic remission 17.7% vs 6.2% placebo (OR 3.3; 1.7-6.4). Subgroup analysis: benefit seen in left-sided colitis but not statistically significant in extensive disease. In 5-ASA-refractory patients: combined clinical + endoscopic remission 13% vs 7.5% (p=0.049), histologic healing 27% vs 17.5% (p=0.016). No maintenance data exist for budesonide MMX. The guideline restricts budesonide MMX recommendations to mild-moderate disease and reserves systemic steroids (prednisolone) for moderate-severe disease, reflecting the trial populations.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G2 (ACG 2019, R9, R14) makes STRONG recommendations for budesonide MMX with moderate quality evidence — notably stronger than ECCO's WEAK recommendation with low quality. This reflects different GRADE judgments about the same evidence base. Both guidelines position budesonide MMX after 5-ASA failure and before systemic steroids. Neither addresses beclomethasone dipropionate, which is available in Europe but not the US.

**My assessment:** The downgrade from ACG's "strong" to ECCO's "weak" is interesting and probably reflects the ECCO methodologists' stricter handling of the extensive disease subgroup (where budesonide MMX was not statistically superior to placebo). The absolute effect size (17.7% combined remission) is modest, and the weak recommendation feels more honest about the limitations. The guideline's silence on beclomethasone dipropionate — a topical steroid available in several European countries — is a notable omission given the European authorship.

### Claim 6: Thiopurines should NOT be used as monotherapy for induction — they are too slow; initiate alongside an effective induction agent if maintenance is planned

**Recommendation 7:** "We suggest against the use of thiopurines as monotherapy for the induction of remission in patients with active UC [weak recommendation, very low quality of evidence]"

**Evidence summary:** Two RCTs (n=130) comparing azathioprine to placebo for induction (1-4 months, with concomitant corticosteroids): clinical remission RR 1.22 (0.79-1.88) — not significant. No data on clinical response, endoscopic response, or SAEs. No RCTs identified for mercaptopurine or thioguanine. The slow onset of action (8-12 weeks) makes thiopurines inherently unsuitable as induction monotherapy.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G2 (ACG 2019, R21) makes a STRONG recommendation against thiopurine/MTX monotherapy for induction. G5 (AGA 2020) concurs. The ECCO recommendation is notably weaker ("suggest against" vs "recommend against"), which may reflect a European comfort with thiopurines that US guidelines don't share — but the clinical message is identical.

**My assessment:** This is one of few instances where ECCO is less forceful than ACG. The "weak" recommendation probably reflects the small sample size (only 130 patients across two old trials) and the GRADE requirement for evidence quality, not clinical equipoise. Practically, no one should use thiopurine monotherapy for active UC, and the guideline's qualifying statement — that thiopurines are appropriate to initiate alongside an effective induction agent when maintenance is planned — is the clinically important takeaway.

## Mild-to-Moderately Active UC: Maintenance

### Claim 7: Oral 5-ASA ≥2 g/d and topical 5-ASA for distal disease are strongly recommended for maintenance despite very low-quality evidence; thiopurines are strongly recommended for steroid-dependent or 5-ASA-intolerant patients

**Recommendation 8:** "We recommend the use of oral 5-ASA at a dose ≥2 g/day for maintenance of remission in UC patients [strong recommendation; very low quality of evidence]"

**Recommendation 9:** "We suggest the use of topical [rectal] 5-ASA for the maintenance of remission in patients with distal UC [weak recommendation, very low-quality evidence]"

**Recommendation 10:** "We recommend monotherapy with thiopurines for the maintenance of remission in patients with steroid-dependent UC or who are intolerant to 5-ASA [strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence]"

**Evidence summary:** Oral 5-ASA maintenance: 2 RCTs (n=306), 48-52 weeks. Clinical remission RR 1.54 (1.11-2.14). Endoscopic remission RR 1.20 (1.00-1.44) — trend but not significant. SAEs RR 0.41 (0.23-0.71) favoring 5-ASA. Topical 5-ASA maintenance: 4 RCTs, doses 1 g three times weekly to 1 g daily, 12-24 months. Clinical remission RR 2.22 (1.26-3.90). Endoscopic remission in one small study (n=25): RR 4.88 (1.31-18.18). Thiopurine maintenance: 4 RCTs (n=232), 1-year follow-up. Azathioprine superior to placebo for clinical remission (56% vs 35%; RR 1.59, 1.19-2.11). Significant safety concerns: thiopurines should be discouraged in patients >65 years due to lymphoma and infection risk. No evidence supports methotrexate for UC maintenance — the MERIT-UC trial was negative.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G2 (ACG 2019) makes similar strong recommendations for oral and rectal 5-ASA maintenance (R16, R17) with moderate quality evidence — ECCO's "very low" quality rating is notably harsher. The thiopurine maintenance recommendation (R10) is stronger in ECCO (strong, moderate quality) than ACG (R32: conditional, low quality), reflecting the larger European experience with thiopurine monotherapy and the UK IBD BioResource data (Stournaras et al., 2021, n=11,928) showing thiopurine effectiveness in UC.

**My assessment:** The "very low quality" ratings for 5-ASA maintenance are striking — they reflect GRADE's strict requirement for modern, well-reported RCTs, which don't exist for these older drugs. The strong recommendations despite very low evidence quality are justified by decades of clinical experience, low cost, and excellent safety. The ECCO's stronger thiopurine endorsement compared to ACG reflects genuine practice differences: European gastroenterologists are more comfortable with thiopurine monotherapy as a steroid-sparing strategy, while US practice has moved more rapidly toward biologics.

## Moderately-to-Severely Active UC: Induction

### Claim 8: Systemic corticosteroids (prednisolone) are strongly recommended for induction despite very low-quality evidence — a paradox explained by clinical experience, large effect size, and limited alternatives at the time of original trials

**Recommendation 11:** "We recommend oral prednisolone for induction of remission in non-hospitalised patients with moderately-to-severely active UC [strong recommendation; very low quality of evidence]"

**Evidence summary:** Only two placebo-controlled RCTs met the panel's PICO criteria (Truelove & Horler 1955, Lennard-Jones 1960), yielding RR 2.83 (1.79-4.46) for clinical remission. Quality downgraded for risk of bias, indirectness, and imprecision. No AE data from these trials. The guideline emphasizes: corticosteroids have no maintenance role, courses should be limited to 3 months maximum, and a corticosteroid-sparing agent should be initiated for any patient requiring >1 course per year or flaring during taper. Corticosteroid-free remission is the desired outcome given irreversible side effects of prolonged exposure.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G2 (ACG 2019, R11) also makes a strong recommendation with low quality evidence. G5 (AGA 2020) concurs. The 3-month maximum and "one course per year" trigger for steroid-sparing therapy are consistent across all guidelines.

**My assessment:** The strong/very-low paradox is more pronounced in ECCO than ACG because ECCO's methodologists were stricter about evidence quality. The real message is in the qualifying text, not the recommendation: steroids are a bridge to something else, never a destination. The explicit "3 months maximum" and ">1 course per year = escalate" guidance is more operationally useful than the GRADE rating.

### Claim 9: Anti-TNF agents (infliximab, adalimumab, golimumab) are strongly recommended for induction; network meta-analyses suggest infliximab superiority, and combination therapy with thiopurines improves outcomes with infliximab

**Recommendation 12:** "We recommend treatment with anti-tumour necrosis factor [TNF] agents [infliximab, adalimumab, and golimumab] to induce remission in patients with moderate-to-severe UC who have inadequate response or intolerance to conventional therapy [strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence]"

**Evidence summary:** Nine RCTs pooled: clinical remission RR 2.23 (1.81-2.76), clinical response RR 1.56 (1.38-1.76), mucosal healing RR 1.49 (1.32-1.68). Safety data from large cohort studies were reassuring. No head-to-head anti-TNF trials exist. Network meta-analyses (Bonovas 2018, Singh 2020): infliximab superior to adalimumab for clinical remission (OR 2.10; 1.21-3.64) and to both adalimumab and golimumab for clinical response and mucosal healing. In patients with prior biologic failure, adalimumab showed markedly lower efficacy (non-significant vs placebo in subgroup analysis). The combination of infliximab + azathioprine is more effective than infliximab alone (UC-SUCCESS trial, Panaccione 2014). For patients with loss of response and anti-drug antibodies, adding a thiopurine when switching to a second anti-TNF reduces immunogenicity (Roblin 2020).

**Key omission:** Unlike ACG 2019 (R24, strong recommendation for IFX + thiopurine combination), ECCO does NOT issue a formal PICO-based combination therapy recommendation. The evidence is discussed in the supporting text but not elevated to a standalone recommendation — a notable structural difference.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G2 (ACG 2019, R22) makes the same strong recommendation with HIGH quality evidence — ECCO's "moderate" is a downgrade from ACG's "high." The difference likely reflects ECCO's more conservative approach to indirectness in the evidence base. G5 (AGA 2020) also strongly recommends anti-TNF therapy. No guideline makes a formal recommendation for one anti-TNF over another, though the network meta-analysis data favoring infliximab is acknowledged by all.

**My assessment:** The absence of a formal combination therapy recommendation is the most interesting structural difference from ACG. It's not that ECCO disagrees with combination therapy — the text clearly states IFX + AZA is more effective — but the panel chose not to formulate a PICO question around it. This may reflect the European trend toward vedolizumab monotherapy as first-line biologic (avoiding the combination therapy question entirely) or the panel's focus on questions with direct RCT evidence. The network meta-analysis data favoring infliximab is well-presented and clinically useful, though the guideline appropriately stops short of making a formal ranking recommendation.

### Claim 10: Vedolizumab is strongly recommended for induction with low-quality evidence; the weak recommendation favoring vedolizumab over adalimumab (from the VARSITY trial) is the guideline's most distinctive feature

**Recommendation 13:** "We recommend treatment with vedolizumab for the induction of remission in patients with moderately-to-severely active UC who have inadequate response or intolerance to conventional therapy [strong recommendation, low quality of evidence]"

**Recommendation 19:** "We suggest the use of vedolizumab rather than adalimumab for the induction and maintenance of remission in patients with moderately-to-severely active ulcerative colitis [weak recommendation, low level of evidence]"

**Evidence summary:** Two placebo-controlled RCTs (n=620): clinical remission RR 2.14 (1.03-4.43), clinical response RR 1.51 (0.99-2.29) — not significant. GEMINI I: week 6 endoscopic remission 40.9% vs 24.8% (p=0.001). Heterogeneity between the two RCTs drove inconsistency ratings down. SAEs: RR 0.71 (0.39-1.30), favorable safety profile confirmed in large cohort studies (Kirchgesner 2020: lower serious infection rates vs anti-TNF).

**VARSITY trial (Sands 2019):** The only head-to-head biologic trial in UC. Vedolizumab vs adalimumab over 52 weeks: clinical remission RR 1.39 (1.10-1.76), endoscopic remission RR 1.43 (1.17-1.75), clinical response RR 1.46 (1.29-1.67) — all favoring vedolizumab. Corticosteroid-free clinical remission was numerically lower with vedolizumab (RR 0.58; 0.32-1.05), but evidence quality was low due to sparse data and wide confidence intervals. Dose escalation was not permitted in VARSITY, despite evidence of improved outcomes with dose escalation for both drugs.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** This is ECCO's unique contribution — no other major society guideline makes an explicit head-to-head recommendation between biologics. G2 (ACG 2019) predates VARSITY. G5 (AGA 2020) references VARSITY in its evidence review but does not make a vedolizumab-over-adalimumab recommendation, instead conditionally recommending infliximab or vedolizumab before adalimumab in biologic-naive patients using a broader evidence synthesis. The ECCO R19 is the most "European" feature of the guideline — it formalizes the European preference for gut-selective therapy first.

**My assessment:** The VARSITY-based R19 is the guideline's most important and most debated recommendation. It has several limitations acknowledged by the panel: (a) no dose escalation was permitted — real-world adalimumab optimization (weekly dosing, combination with immunomodulator) might close the gap; (b) the corticosteroid-free remission paradox (numerically favoring adalimumab) complicates the narrative; (c) VARSITY compared vedolizumab to adalimumab, not infliximab, and infliximab showed superiority to adalimumab in network meta-analyses — so the vedolizumab-over-adalimumab finding cannot be extrapolated to vedolizumab-over-infliximab. Nevertheless, for anti-TNF-naive patients where the choice is between vedolizumab and adalimumab, VARSITY provides the only head-to-head data in UC and the direction is clear. The weak strength of the recommendation appropriately reflects the limitations.

### Claim 11: Tofacitinib is strongly recommended for induction with moderate-quality evidence; the guideline explicitly addresses emerging safety concerns (herpes zoster, VTE, MACE, malignancy) that post-date the ACG 2019 guideline

**Recommendation 14:** "We recommend treatment with tofacitinib to induce remission in patients with moderate-to-severe UC who have inadequate response or intolerance to conventional therapy [strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence]"

**Evidence summary:** Two RCTs (n=1,220): OCTAVE 1 and 2. Clinical response RR 1.79 (1.49-2.14), clinical remission RR 3.26 (1.95-5.43), endoscopic response RR 5.18 (2.12-12.69). Half of patients had prior anti-TNF failure; efficacy was similar regardless of anti-TNF exposure status. Network meta-analyses (Singh 2020): no statistical difference between tofacitinib and anti-TNF agents or ustekinumab in biologic-naive patients, possible benefit over adalimumab or vedolizumab in anti-TNF-exposed patients.

**Safety — the most detailed safety discussion of any agent in this guideline:** Herpes zoster: RR 6.53 across JAK inhibitor trials (Olivera 2020), incidence rate 4.1/100 person-years in UC program, dose-dependent (higher with 10 mg BID), more common in older patients and those on concomitant corticosteroids. VTE: ORAL Surveillance in RA patients ≥50 years with cardiovascular risk factors — significantly increased VTE risk with tofacitinib 10 mg BID vs anti-TNF; not seen at 5 mg BID. EMA recommended using lowest efficacious dose and avoiding 10 mg BID maintenance in patients with VTE risk factors. The RIVETING trial (Vermeire 2021): patients in sustained remission ≥6 months on 10 mg BID randomized to continue or de-escalate to 5 mg BID — remission rates 90% vs 77% at 6 months, numerically fewer herpes zoster cases at 5 mg BID. Post-marketing surveillance also suggested increased cardiac events and malignancies (EMA 2021). The guideline concludes: "the efficacy data, including in patients with previous anti-TNF exposure, along with the benefits associated with oral dosing and lack of immunogenicity, support our recommendations for tofacitinib as a treatment option... with the risks and benefits to be considered for each patient."

**Comparison against other guidelines:** This is where the publication timeline matters most. G2 (ACG 2019) made strong recommendations for tofacitinib in both anti-TNF-naive and anti-TNF-exposed patients (R27, R28) with moderate quality evidence and minimal safety caveats — the OCTAVE data were fresh and the ORAL Surveillance results were not yet available. The ECCO guideline, published 3 years later, incorporates the full post-marketing safety picture. The recommendation remains strong, but the supporting text is notably more cautionary. G5 (AGA 2020) was intermediate: conditional recommendations, preference for infliximab or vedolizumab before tofacitinib in biologic-naive patients. ECCO's approach — strong recommendation but with detailed risk stratification — represents a practical middle ground: tofacitinib works well, but patient selection matters.

**My assessment:** The tofacitinib section is the guideline's most careful piece of writing. The panel walked a difficult line: maintaining a strong recommendation (because the efficacy data are robust) while honestly presenting safety concerns that have reshaped prescribing. The actionable guidance is in the details: use 5 mg BID for maintenance when possible, avoid 10 mg BID in patients with VTE risk factors, counsel about herpes zoster (consider recombinant zoster vaccine), and weigh the oral convenience against safety in older patients. The mention of the RIVETING de-escalation data (90% vs 77% remission) is particularly clinically useful — it suggests that many patients on 10 mg BID can safely step down.

### Claim 12: Ustekinumab is strongly recommended for induction — the first major UC guideline to incorporate it, with efficacy in both biologic-naive and biologic-exposed patients

**Recommendation 15:** "We recommend treatment with ustekinumab for the induction of remission in patients with moderately-to-severely active UC with inadequate response or intolerance to conventional therapy. [strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence]"

**Evidence summary:** Single phase 3 RCT (UNIFI, Sands 2019). Ustekinumab 6 mg/kg IV induction: clinical remission 15.5% vs 5.3% placebo (RR 2.91; 1.72-4.94), clinical response 61.8% vs 31.3% (RR 1.97; 1.64-2.37), endoscopic improvement 27.0% vs 13.8% (RR 1.96; 1.41-2.72). Of randomized patients, 51.1% had prior biologic failure (16.6% had failed both anti-TNF and vedolizumab). Benefit vs placebo was observed regardless of biologic exposure history. IBDQ score change from baseline: 35.0 vs 16.16 (p<0.001). Fecal calprotectin reduction: -1368.26 vs +17.92 (p<0.001). SAEs: 5.2% vs 7.9% (RR 0.67; 0.39-1.17). Network meta-analyses: no statistical difference vs anti-TNF or tofacitinib in biologic-naive patients, possible benefit over adalimumab or vedolizumab in anti-TNF-exposed patients.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G2 (ACG 2019) predates ustekinumab's UC approval (October 2019) by 8 months — this is a major temporal gap. G5 (AGA 2020) was published after UNIFI but before ustekinumab received its formal UC recommendation in most guidelines. The ECCO guideline is the first to give ustekinumab a full GRADE-based strong recommendation, making it the most current advanced therapy algorithm.

**My assessment:** The 15.5% clinical remission rate is modest in absolute terms but must be interpreted in context: this was a heavily biologic-refractory population. The drug's favorable safety profile (no increased infection signal, minimal immunogenicity) and convenient maintenance dosing (SC every 8-12 weeks) make it an attractive option, particularly for patients who have failed multiple mechanisms. The guideline appropriately doesn't position ustekinumab relative to other biologics — the evidence doesn't support a ranking — but its inclusion expands the treatment armamentarium at a time when sequencing decisions are increasingly complex.

## Moderately-to-Severely Active UC: Maintenance

### Claim 13: Continue the agent that induced remission; anti-TNF, vedolizumab, tofacitinib, and ustekinumab all receive strong maintenance recommendations; there is no role for 5-ASA or methotrexate in maintenance after advanced therapy

**Recommendation 16:** Anti-TNF agents for maintenance after anti-TNF induction [strong, high-quality evidence]

**Recommendation 18:** Vedolizumab for maintenance after vedolizumab induction [strong, moderate-quality evidence]

**Recommendation 20:** Tofacitinib for maintenance after tofacitinib induction [strong, moderate-quality evidence]

**Recommendation 21:** Ustekinumab for maintenance after ustekinumab induction [strong, moderate-quality evidence]

**Evidence summary:** Anti-TNF maintenance meta-analysis (10 RCTs): clinical remission RR 1.98 (1.60-2.45), steroid-free clinical remission RR 2.86 (1.67-4.90), sustained clinical remission RR 2.76 (1.78-4.28), QoL improvement RR 1.71 (1.27-2.32). This is the only "high-quality" evidence rating for an advanced therapy in the guideline. Vedolizumab maintenance (3 RCTs, n=441, 52-60 weeks): clinical remission RR 2.37 (1.74-3.23), sustained clinical remission 20.7% vs 9.4% (RR 2.16; 1.34-3.50). Subcutaneous vedolizumab maintenance showed similar efficacy to IV (Sandborn 2020). Tofacitinib maintenance (OCTAVE Sustain, n=593): clinical remission 5 mg BID or 10 mg BID superior to placebo (RR 3.37; 2.23-5.10), endoscopic remission RR 3.88 (1.90-7.95). Ustekinumab maintenance (UNIFI): 90 mg SC q8w — clinical remission RR 1.82 (1.33-2.49), steroid-free remission RR 1.79 (1.30-2.47), endoscopic remission RR 1.79 (1.36-2.36). 90 mg SC q12w also superior to placebo, but numerically lower than q8w, especially in biologic-refractory patients. No guideline addresses concomitant 5-ASA with advanced therapy — this question was not formulated as a PICO.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G2 (ACG 2019) makes equivalent strong maintenance recommendations for anti-TNF, vedolizumab, and tofacitinib (R34-36). ACG additionally recommends against concomitant 5-ASA with biologics (R30, conditional) and against methotrexate for maintenance (R33, conditional) — recommendations ECCO doesn't make, likely because these weren't prioritized as PICO questions. The ECCO maintenance recommendations benefit from including ustekinumab and the vedolizumab SC data.

**My assessment:** The universal "continue what induced remission" principle is consistent across guidelines and makes clinical sense. The more interesting question — what to do when the induction agent loses efficacy — is addressed only indirectly through the drug monitoring discussion. The guideline's silence on concomitant 5-ASA with biologics contrasts with ACG's explicit recommendation against it, but this reflects the PICO-driven structure rather than a difference in clinical opinion.

## Therapeutic Drug Monitoring

### Claim 14: There is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against therapeutic drug monitoring to improve clinical outcomes — ECCO takes the most conservative position of any major guideline

**Recommendation 17:** "In UC patients who have lost response to an anti-TNF agent, there is currently insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the use of therapeutic drug monitoring to improve clinical outcomes"

**Evidence summary:** Multiple retrospective studies demonstrate associations between trough drug levels and clinical outcomes for infliximab, adalimumab, vedolizumab, and ustekinumab. Retrospective analyses suggest TDM-informed decisions may be more successful than clinically guided decisions (Kelly 2017). However, all studies are retrospective and cannot establish causality. One abstract-only prospective study (SERENE-UC, Colombel 2020) randomized 371 adalimumab responders to standard dose, high dose, or TDM-guided dosing — the TDM arm was exploratory, not powered for superiority, and showed a non-significant trend (36.5% vs 29% clinical remission). No adequately powered prospective RCT exists. The panel also notes that similar challenges apply to proactive TDM in patients not experiencing loss of response.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** This is the most striking divergence from other guidelines. G2 (ACG 2019, R29) conditionally recommends measuring drug levels and antibodies in anti-TNF loss of response. G5 (AGA 2020) makes a stronger recommendation for reactive TDM in active disease. ECCO's refusal to make ANY recommendation — even a weak one — reflects the strictest interpretation of GRADE: association is not causation, and without a prospective trial randomizing patients to TDM-guided vs empiric management, you cannot recommend TDM.

**My assessment:** This is intellectually rigorous but clinically impractical. The GRADE purist position — "no prospective RCT, no recommendation" — is defensible methodologically, but it leaves clinicians without guidance on a question they face daily. The vast majority of IBD specialists use reactive TDM routinely; ECCO's silence won't change practice but may be used by payers to deny TDM coverage. The panel's honesty about the evidence gap is admirable, but a weak/conditional recommendation based on the consistent retrospective data (as ACG and AGA did) would have been more clinically useful. The guideline correctly identifies the critical research need: a prospective trial randomizing patients with loss of response to TDM-guided vs empiric management.

## What the Guideline Does NOT Cover

### Acute Severe UC and Surgery

The ECCO medical treatment guideline explicitly defers ASUC management and surgical treatment to a companion guideline: "ECCO Guidelines on Therapeutics in Ulcerative Colitis: Surgical Treatment." This is a fundamental structural difference from ACG 2019, which devotes an extensive section to ASUC (R37-R46, 10 graded recommendations). The medical guideline makes no recommendations on:

- IV corticosteroid dosing for hospitalized patients
- The 3-5 day steroid window and Oxford criteria
- Rescue therapy with infliximab vs cyclosporine
- VTE prophylaxis in hospitalized UC
- CMV evaluation in steroid-refractory disease
- Surgical timing and staged IPAA
- Pouchitis management

**Comparison:** The ACG 2019 guideline's ASUC section is among its strongest — the detailed protocol for the hospitalized patient (IV methylprednisolone 60 mg/d, Oxford index at day 3, surgical consultation for nonresponders, CYSIF/CONSTRUCT-level evidence for infliximab vs cyclosporine) is a model of actionable guidance. ECCO's separation into two documents means clinicians treating hospitalized patients must consult a separate guideline. For a comprehensive UC reference, this fragmentation is a disadvantage. However, the surgical companion guideline may provide more detailed operative guidance than ACG's brief surgical considerations section.

**Cyclosporine note:** ACG 2019 devotes substantial evidence review to cyclosporine (CYSIF trial, CONSTRUCT trial, ENEIDA registry), reflecting its continued use in expert centers. The ECCO medical guideline's silence on cyclosporine reflects the PICO-driven structure — if the panel didn't formulate a cyclosporine PICO question for outpatient management, it doesn't appear. This is a limitation for centers that use cyclosporine or tacrolimus as bridge therapy.

**Overall GRADE assessment:** Of 21 recommendations, 15 are strong (71%) and 6 are weak (29%) — notably, ECCO uses "weak" where ACG uses "conditional." Only ONE recommendation has "high" quality evidence (R16: anti-TNF maintenance). Five have "moderate" quality, five "low," seven "very low," and one "low" for R19. The preponderance of strong recommendations with low/very-low evidence reflects GRADE's allowance for upgrading recommendation strength based on clinical experience, safety, cost, and patient values — a pattern also seen in ACG 2019 but more pronounced here due to stricter evidence quality ratings.

**Strongest sections:**
1. **Advanced therapy evidence syntheses (R12-R15, R18-R21):** The de novo meta-analyses for each agent are the guideline's core contribution. The systematic approach to anti-TNF (9 RCTs pooled), vedolizumab (2 RCTs + VARSITY), tofacitinib (2 RCTs + detailed safety), and ustekinumab (single RCT, well-characterized) provides clinicians with a comprehensive, up-to-date evidence map.
2. **Tofacitinib safety discussion:** The most thorough treatment of JAK inhibitor safety in any UC guideline, incorporating ORAL Surveillance, EMA regulatory actions, RIVETING de-escalation data, and practical risk stratification.
3. **VARSITY-based vedolizumab-over-adalimumab recommendation (R19):** Methodologically honest (weak, low evidence), clinically provocative, and uniquely European.

**Weakest sections:**
1. **Absence of ASUC management:** The decision to publish a separate surgical/hospitalized guideline fragments UC care. A clinician managing a hospitalized patient needs both documents.
2. **No therapeutic drug monitoring recommendation (R17):** GRADE purity at the expense of clinical utility. Most specialists will continue using reactive TDM regardless.
3. **No sequencing algorithm:** Like ACG 2019, the guideline makes strong recommendations for individual agents but offers no framework for choosing among them. The VARSITY-based R19 is the closest to sequencing guidance, but it only addresses one pairwise comparison.
4. **Limited discussion of treatment withdrawal:** Acknowledged as "beyond the scope of this current guideline" and deferred to a separate ECCO Topical Review (Doherty 2018). For a document on therapeutics, the absence of de-escalation guidance is a significant gap.
5. **No chemotherapy or CRC surveillance guidance:** Unlike ACG 2019 which covers chemoprevention and surveillance colonoscopy, ECCO defers these to separate guidelines.

**Cross-reference priorities:**
- **G2 (ACG UC 2019 - Rubin):** The most important comparator. Key differences: (a) ECCO includes ustekinumab (ACG predates it); (b) ECCO makes no TDM recommendation (ACG conditionally recommends reactive TDM); (c) ECCO gives a weak recommendation for vedolizumab over adalimumab (ACG makes no head-to-head recommendation); (d) ECCO covers ASUC and surgery in a separate guideline (ACG integrates them); (e) ECCO has stricter evidence quality ratings — ACG rates 5-ASA induction and anti-TNF induction as "high" while ECCO rates both lower; (f) ECCO uses higher 5-ASA dosing (≥2 g/d as minimum vs ACG's 2-2.4 g/d "sufficient"); (g) ECCO's tofacitinib safety discussion is far more detailed, reflecting the later publication date.
- **G5 (AGA UC 2020 - Feuerstein):** AGA's conditional recommendations and explicit sequencing (infliximab/vedolizumab before tofacitinib) contrast with ECCO's strong recommendations and more nuanced safety-based approach. AGA covers 5-ASA dosing, rectal therapy, and monitoring in more granular detail for mild-moderate UC. ECCO's VARSITY-based recommendation and ustekinumab inclusion make it the most current guideline.
- **ECCO Surgical Treatment companion guideline:** Essential reading for the ASUC and surgical content that this medical guideline omits. Together they form the complete ECCO UC treatment framework.

**Most controversial claims:**
1. **Vedolizumab over adalimumab (R19):** Based on a single trial without dose escalation. The corticosteroid-free remission paradox weakens the case. Does NOT mean vedolizumab over infliximab.
2. **No TDM recommendation (R17):** Will be ignored by most specialists but could be weaponized by payers. The intellectually honest position may be clinically counterproductive.
3. **Strong recommendation for tofacitinib (R14) despite detailed safety concerns:** The panel's balancing act — strong efficacy + real safety risks = strong recommendation with caveats — is logically coherent but creates cognitive dissonance for clinicians weighing the risks.

**Historical significance:** This guideline captures a pivotal moment in UC therapeutics: the post-VARSITY, post-UNIFI, post-ORAL Surveillance landscape. It is simultaneously the most current UC guideline (incorporating ustekinumab and the 2021 safety data), the most methodologically rigorous (strict GRADE application, including the refusal to recommend TDM without prospective data), and the most European in its clinical philosophy (vedolizumab-first, higher 5-ASA dosing, comfort with thiopurine monotherapy). The companion surgical guideline structure acknowledges the multidisciplinary nature of UC care but fragments the reference. The next ECCO update will likely need to address the expanding small molecule pipeline (ozanimod, upadacitinib) and the growing evidence for histologic healing as a formal treatment target.
