# ECCO Guidelines on Therapeutics in Crohn's Disease: Medical Treatment (Torres et al., 2020)

The first ECCO guideline on medical treatment of Crohn's disease to formally adopt the GRADE methodology. Developed by a panel of 48 European IBD experts with methodologists, librarians, and four patient representatives from EFCCA. The guideline contains **32 numbered GRADE recommendations** across three domains: induction therapy, maintenance therapy, and perianal fistulising disease. Notably, postoperative management was **excluded** — deferred to the separate ECCO surgical guideline. This is the definitive European counterpart to the ACG 2018 guideline, with important divergences in treatment philosophy, thiopurine positioning, TDM guidance, and biologic sequencing.

## Scope and Methodology

The guideline used PICO-formatted questions developed through a Delphi consensus process. Comprehensive literature searches were run on EMBASE, PubMed/Medline, and Cochrane Central. GRADE methodology was applied: quality of evidence rated high, moderate, low, or very low; strength of recommendation graded strong or weak. Final recommendations required ≥80% panel agreement. Of the 32 recommendations: **16 are strong, 16 are weak**. Highest-quality evidence (high) supports **2 recommendations** (both for ustekinumab induction — R1.8 and R1.10 indirect comparison). Moderate quality supports the majority; very low quality supports several fistula-related recommendations.

**Key methodological note:** Unlike the ACG 2018 guideline (which deferred TDM entirely), ECCO 2020 includes formal GRADE recommendations on proactive and reactive TDM — making it the first major CD guideline to directly address TDM with GRADE methodology. However, the guideline explicitly excluded postoperative management, referring to a separate ECCO surgical guideline. Biosimilar use is addressed in the narrative text but not as formal GRADE recommendations.

**Comparison to ACG 2018:** ECCO's panel was larger (48 vs. 6) and included patient representatives — a notable methodological strength. The ACG guideline has more recommendations (60 vs. 32) but covers a broader scope (diagnosis, surveillance, surgery, postoperative). ECCO's narrower scope on medical treatment allows more granular guidance within those domains.

## General Approach to Crohn's Disease Management

### Claim 1: CD is a progressive disease requiring early intensive monitoring and individualized risk-stratified therapy

**Evidence summary:** The guideline opens with an explicit statement of the modern CD treatment paradigm: chronic untreated inflammation results in progressive bowel damage, even when asymptomatic. This is the foundational premise driving early intervention and tight control. The guideline emphasizes stratifying patients by prognostic risk factors (age, disease extent, perianal involvement, deep ulcerations, complicated phenotype) and individualizing therapy. Because of the well-documented disconnect between symptoms and inflammation, objective monitoring (endoscopy, CRP, fecal calprotectin, imaging) is mandatory. The treat-to-target concept is endorsed though the STRIDE-II update had not yet been published.

**Key patient-centered additions:** Four EFCCA patient representatives participated in all face-to-face meetings — a first for ECCO guideline development. The guideline explicitly incorporates patient values and preferences into recommendation strength, consistent with GRADE methodology.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G1 (ACG 2018) shares the progressive-disease premise but phrases it less forcefully. G8 is more assertive about early biologic use in high-risk patients. G6 (AGA 2021) likewise endorses treat-to-target but with STRIDE-II targets. The ECCO emphasis on individualized risk stratification is more developed than ACG's simpler low/moderate/high-risk framework.

**My assessment:** The paradigm statement is the most important section of the guideline — it justifies the entire treatment algorithm. The inclusion of patient representatives is methodologically admirable but its practical impact on the final recommendations is unclear. The guideline's emphasis on cost considerations alongside efficacy reflects the European multi-payer reality and is a genuine differentiator from US guidelines.

## Section 1: Induction of Remission

### Claim 2: 5-ASA and sulfasalazine are ineffective for inducing remission in CD; budesonide is effective for mild-to-moderate ileocecal disease (R1.1-R1.2)

**Recommendation 1.1:** "We suggest against the use of 5-ASA for induction of remission of Crohn's disease." **Weak recommendation, moderate-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 1.2:** "We recommend using budesonide for the induction of clinical remission in patients with active mild-to-moderate Crohn's disease limited to the ileum and/or ascending colon." **Strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** A meta-analysis of 7 RCTs (5-ASA or sulfasalazine vs. placebo) found no significant effect for induction of clinical remission (RR 1.28; 95% CI 0.97-1.69). A Cochrane review confirmed this. Sulfasalazine showed a marginal effect of borderline significance (RR 1.38; 95% CI 1.00-1.89), limited to colonic disease. Budesonide 9 mg/day was superior to placebo for clinical response (RR 1.46; 95% CI 1.03-2.07) and clinical remission (RR 1.93; 95% CI 1.37-2.73). Budesonide was NOT superior to mesalazine for clinical remission (RR 1.30; 95% CI 0.98-1.72) but was superior for clinical response (RR 1.22; 95% CI 1.03-1.45).

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G1 (ACG 2018, R11) gives a **strong** recommendation against mesalamine with moderate-quality evidence — stronger wording than ECCO's "weak." G1 (R12) is also strong for budesonide but with low-quality evidence vs. ECCO's moderate. G8 is more cautious due to the borderline significance of sulfasalazine's effect. Both guidelines agree on the fundamental message: 5-ASA doesn't work for CD.

**My assessment:** ECCO's "weak" recommendation against 5-ASA (vs. ACG's "strong") is notable. The difference may reflect the European panel's desire to leave room for sulfasalazine in colonic CD — a legacy European practice. The budesonide recommendation is one of the few "strong, moderate" recommendations — appropriately so, given consistent RCT data. The anatomical limitation (ileum/ascending colon only) is practically crucial: budesonide is useless for jejunal, proximal ileal, or left-sided colonic disease. The guideline acknowledges this treatment gap explicitly.

### Claim 3: Systemic corticosteroids are effective for moderate-to-severe CD but carry substantial toxicity; they are a bridge, not a destination (R1.3)

**Recommendation 1.3:** "In patients with active, moderate-to-severe Crohn's disease, we suggest the use of systemic corticosteroids for the induction of clinical response and remission." **Weak recommendation, moderate-quality evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Pooled data from two RCTs (n=267): corticosteroids were twice as effective as placebo for clinical remission (RR 1.99; 95% CI 1.51-2.64). Clinical response with methylprednisolone was 93.6% vs. 53.4% for placebo (RR 1.75). However, adverse events were 5-fold higher (31.8% vs. 6.5%; RR 4.89). Steroid-related AEs include Cushing syndrome, acne, infection (increased abscess risk), ecchymoses, hypertension, diabetes, osteoporosis, cataracts, glaucoma. The guideline defines **corticosteroid dependency/excess** as: inability to wean below prednisolone 10 mg/day (or budesonide 3 mg/day) within 3 months, relapse within 3 months of stopping, or need for >1 course in 12 months — any of these should trigger a steroid-sparing strategy. Conventional steroids do not prevent disease relapse and are ineffective for maintenance.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G1 (ACG 2018, R17-R18) gives a **strong** recommendation for short-term steroid use (vs. ECCO's "weak") — this is a notable divergence. ACG's separate R18 (conditional) explicitly states steroids don't achieve mucosal healing. ECCO folds both messages into one weaker recommendation. The steroid-dependency definition is more precisely defined in G8 than G1.

**My assessment:** The "weak" recommendation for systemic steroids (vs. ACG's "strong") is puzzling — both agree steroids work, but ECCO weights the toxicity more heavily. This may reflect European reluctance to endorse a toxic therapy strongly, even when effective. The steroid-dependency definition is clinically invaluable: it provides a concrete trigger for treatment escalation. The 3-month wean benchmark should be tracked in every CD patient on steroids.

### Claim 4: Thiopurines should NOT be used as monotherapy for induction; their role is in steroid-sparing maintenance (R1.4)

**Recommendation 1.4:** "We suggest against the use of thiopurines as monotherapy for the induction of remission of moderate-to-severe luminal Crohn's disease." **Weak recommendation, very low-quality evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** Pooled analysis of 5 trials (n=380): no difference for induction of remission between thiopurines and placebo (48% vs. 37%; RR 1.23; 95% CI 0.97-1.55). Clinical response data from 3 trials: 42.8% vs. 26.9% (RR 1.87; 95% CI 0.44-7.96) with serious heterogeneity (I²=69%) and very serious imprecision. Serious AEs: 13.5% for AZA vs. 3.8% for placebo (RR 2.57; 95% CI 0.92-7.13). Thiopurines have a slow onset of action (8-12 weeks) making them unsuitable for acute induction. No consistent improvement in biomarkers (CRP, ESR, calprotectin) was observed during induction.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G1 (ACG 2018, R19) gives a **strong** recommendation against thiopurines for induction — stronger than ECCO's "weak." Both agree on the core message: don't use thiopurines to induce remission. The difference in strength reflects ECCO's acknowledgment that thiopurines ARE commonly started alongside steroids (as a bridge to maintenance), blurring the "induction" vs. "steroid-sparing" distinction.

**My assessment:** The clinical reality is that thiopurines ARE often started at the same time as steroids for induction in European practice — the slow onset means the steroid does the induction work while the thiopurine gradually takes over. ECCO's "weak" recommendation acknowledges this pragmatic reality better than ACG's more categorical "strong." However, the very low-quality evidence for the recommendation is honest: the trials are old, small, and heterogeneous.

### Claim 5: No consensus was reached on methotrexate for induction; anti-TNF agents are the standard for moderate-to-severe disease (R1.5-R1.7)

**Recommendation 1.5:** "We recommend the use of TNF inhibitors (infliximab, adalimumab, and certolizumab pegol) to induce remission in patients with moderate-to-severe Crohn's disease who have not responded to conventional therapy." **Strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 1.6:** "We suggest against the combination of adalimumab and thiopurines over adalimumab alone to achieve clinical remission and response." **Weak recommendation, moderate-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 1.7:** "We recommend combination therapy with a thiopurine when starting infliximab to induce remission." **Strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** This is the most clinically important set of recommendations in the guideline, centered on the divergent evidence for infliximab vs. adalimumab combination therapy.

**Anti-TNF vs. placebo:** Meta-analysis of RCTs supports efficacy for clinical remission (RR 1.6; 95% CI 1.17-2.36) and clinical response (RR 1.43; 95% CI 1.17-1.73). No significant differences in AEs (RR 0.99).

**Infliximab combination (SONIC trial):** Combination therapy with infliximab + AZA was superior to infliximab monotherapy for clinical remission at Week 26 (RR 1.64; 95% CI 1.07-2.53) and mucosal healing (RR 1.82; 95% CI 1.01-3.26). Lower rates of serious AEs were observed in the combination arm (RR 0.56; 95% CI 0.32-0.97).

**Adalimumab combination (DIAMOND trial):** Combination therapy with adalimumab + AZA was NOT superior to adalimumab monotherapy for clinical remission at Week 26 (RR 0.95; 95% CI 0.78-1.15). Endoscopic improvement favored combination at Week 26 (RR 1.32; 95% CI 1.06-1.65) but was lost at 1 year. The DIAMOND trial used lower AZA doses (25-100 mg/day vs. standard 2-2.5 mg/kg/day), which may explain the negative result.

**Certolizumab pegol:** Not approved in the EU for CD (commercially available in Switzerland and Russia). A 2015 network meta-analysis suggested infliximab + AZA (OR 3.1) and adalimumab monotherapy (OR 2.1) were superior to certolizumab pegol for induction.

**Methotrexate:** Only one RCT (Feagan 1995, n=141): MTX 25 mg/week IM was superior to placebo for remission in steroid-dependent patients at 16 weeks (RR 2.06). However, no studies compared MTX monotherapy vs. placebo for induction, and no consensus could be reached — the guideline makes **no recommendation on MTX for induction**. MTX may be considered when alternatives (including surgery) cannot be used.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** This is the most important area of divergence between G8 and G1. G1 (ACG 2018, R26) gives a single strong recommendation for combination therapy regardless of anti-TNF agent — it does NOT differentiate between infliximab and adalimumab. G8's differentiation (R1.7 strong for infliximab combination vs. R1.6 weak against adalimumab combination) is a more nuanced, evidence-based position that directly reflects the divergent SONIC and DIAMOND trial results. G6 (AGA 2021) is likely to align more with individualized approaches based on TDM.

**My assessment:** This is the single most important section of the guideline and the clearest contribution of ECCO 2020 to CD therapeutics. The differential recommendation — combination strongly recommended for infliximab but not for adalimumab — is evidence-based and clinically actionable. It reflects the reality that different anti-TNF agents have different immunogenicity profiles. The DIAMOND trial's low-dose AZA is a significant confounder that the guideline acknowledges but doesn't fully resolve. The lack of consensus on MTX for induction leaves a genuine treatment gap for patients who cannot use thiopurines or biologics.

### Claim 6: Ustekinumab and vedolizumab are effective induction agents for both biologic-naive and anti-TNF-experienced patients; no head-to-head data differentiate them (R1.8-R1.10)

**Recommendation 1.8:** "We recommend ustekinumab for induction of remission in patients with moderate-to-severe Crohn's disease with inadequate response to conventional therapy and/or to anti-TNF therapy." **Strong recommendation, high-quality evidence.** (One of only two "high" quality recommendations in the guideline.)

**Recommendation 1.9:** "We recommend vedolizumab for induction of response and remission in patients with moderate-to-severe Crohn's disease with inadequate response to conventional therapy and/or to anti-TNF therapy." **Strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 1.10:** "We equally suggest the use of either ustekinumab or vedolizumab for the treatment of moderate-to-severe active luminal Crohn's disease in patients who have previously failed anti-TNF therapy." **Weak recommendation, very low-quality evidence.**

**Evidence summary:**

**Ustekinumab:** Meta-analysis of 4 RCTs (n=1,947): RR for clinical response 1.56 (95% CI 1.38-1.77); RR for clinical remission 1.76 (95% CI 1.40-2.22). Endoscopic improvement at 8 weeks: 47.7% vs. 29.9% (RR 1.60; 95% CI 1.13-2.26). No increase in AEs (RR 0.96) or serious AEs (RR 0.79). Anti-drug antibody formation <5%.

**Vedolizumab:** Meta-analysis of 3 RCTs (n=969): RR for clinical remission 2.01 (95% CI 1.50-2.71); RR for clinical response 1.55 (95% CI 1.14-2.11). Serious AE rate not different from placebo (RR 0.94).

**Indirect comparison (ustekinumab vs. vedolizumab):** One systematic review (n=1,249) found no significant difference for clinical response (RR 1.14; 95% CI 0.65-1.99) or clinical remission (RR 1.16; 95% CI 0.54-2.48) in anti-TNF-failure patients. Quality of evidence was **very low** for both outcomes.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G1 (ACG 2018, R27, R30) gives strong, high-quality evidence for both vedolizumab and ustekinumab induction. G8's "high" rating only for ustekinumab (not vedolizumab) reflects the larger evidence base for ustekinumab. G1 did not attempt an indirect comparison between the two agents. G8's R1.10 ("equally suggest either") is an honest acknowledgment of the evidence gap — clinicians must choose without comparative data.

**My assessment:** R1.10 is both the most honest and most frustrating recommendation in the guideline. "Equally suggest either" is methodologically correct (very low-quality evidence for the comparison) but clinically unsatisfying. The choice between ustekinumab and vedolizumab drives real-world treatment decisions daily, and the guideline cannot help. The higher quality evidence for ustekinumab (high vs. moderate for vedolizumab) is an implicit signal, but the guideline stops short of recommending it preferentially. Surgery is explicitly mentioned as an alternative option in refractory patients — a characteristically European perspective.

## Section 2: Maintenance of Remission

### Claim 7: 5-ASA is ineffective for maintenance; thiopurines are effective for steroid-dependent patients but not for early disease (R2.1-R2.3)

**Recommendation 2.1:** "We recommend against the use of oral 5-aminosalicylic acid for maintenance of medically induced remission in patients with Crohn's disease." **Strong recommendation, low-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 2.2:** "Thiopurines are recommended for the maintenance of remission in patients with steroid-dependent Crohn's disease." **Strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 2.3:** "We recommend against the early introduction of thiopurine therapy in patients with newly diagnosed Crohn's disease for maintaining remission." **Weak recommendation, low-quality evidence.**

**Evidence summary:**

**5-ASA maintenance:** 11 placebo-controlled trials (1-4 g/day, 4-36 months): no statistically significant benefit (RR 1.03; 95% CI 0.92-1.16). Safety data were very sparse.

**Thiopurines for steroid-dependent patients:** Meta-analysis of 6 trials (n=489): AZA superior to placebo for maintenance of remission (RR 1.19; 95% CI 1.05-1.34). However, serious AEs were significantly higher (9.0% vs. 2.9%; RR 2.45; 95% CI 1.22-4.90): pancreatitis, leukopenia, nausea, allergic reactions, infections.

**Early thiopurine introduction:** The AZTEC trial (n=131, recent diagnosis <8 weeks): no significant benefit for clinical remission at 76 weeks (RR 1.27; 95% CI 0.94-1.72) or sustained corticosteroid-free remission (RR 1.21; 95% CI 0.79-1.84). Serious AEs trended higher (20.6% vs. 11.1%; RR 1.85).

**Long-term thiopurine continuation (R2.10):** Meta-analysis of 4 trials (n=215): discontinuation of AZA increased clinical relapse risk (RR 2.39; 95% CI 1.38-4.13). Trend towards fewer serious AEs with discontinuation but not significant (RR 0.32; 95% CI 0.04-2.92). Data beyond 18 months are lacking.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** This is the most dramatic area of divergence between ECCO and ACG. G1 (ACG 2018, R46) also recommends against 5-ASA maintenance (strong, moderate). However, G1 does NOT have a recommendation against early thiopurine introduction (R2.3 is unique to G8). G1's thiopurine recommendations (R20-R21) are broadly pro-thiopurine for steroid-sparing and maintenance without the early-disease caveat. G8's R2.3 reflects the negative AZTEC trial and represents a more restrained European position — ironic given Europe's historically greater thiopurine use. G6 (AGA 2021) is likely to further de-emphasize thiopurine monotherapy in favor of early biologic use.

**My assessment:** R2.3 (against early thiopurine introduction) is the most surprising recommendation in the guideline. It directly contradicts traditional European practice where thiopurines were the default first-line immunomodulator. The AZTEC trial, while negative, was underpowered and studied a low-risk population. This recommendation may represent an overcorrection. However, combined with R2.10 (continue thiopurines once on them), the message is nuanced: don't start thiopurines early in low-risk patients, but once patients are on them and in remission, continuing is better than stopping.

### Claim 8: Methotrexate parenterally is effective for maintenance; biologics should be continued to maintain their induced remissions (R2.4-R2.7)

**Recommendation 2.4:** "We recommend methotrexate administered parenterally for the maintenance of remission in patients with steroid-dependent Crohn's disease." **Weak recommendation, moderate-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 2.5:** "In patients with Crohn's disease who achieved remission with anti-TNF agents, maintenance treatment using the same treatment is recommended." **Strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 2.6:** "We recommend vedolizumab for maintaining clinical remission in patients with moderate-to-severe Crohn's disease who achieved remission with vedolizumab." **Strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 2.7:** "We recommend the use of ustekinumab to maintain clinical remission in patients with Crohn's disease who achieved remission with ustekinumab." **Strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence.**

**Evidence summary:**

**MTX maintenance:** One RCT (Feagan 2000): after 16-24 weeks of 25 mg/week IM induction, patients randomized to 15 mg/week IM MTX vs. placebo for 40 weeks. Remission maintained: 65% vs. 39% (RR 1.67; 95% CI 1.05-2.67). Nausea/vomiting more common with MTX (40% vs. 25%); no severe leukopenia.

**Anti-TNF maintenance:** Meta-analysis of 5 landmark trials (n=1,771, follow-up 24-30 weeks): overall RR for maintaining remission 1.78 (95% CI 1.51-2.09). Individual agents: infliximab RR 1.86, adalimumab RR 2.06, certolizumab pegol RR 1.62. Network meta-analysis found no significant differences between agents. Monotherapy with anti-TNFs considered safe vs. placebo in RCTs, though sample size and short follow-up limit detection of rare AEs.

**Vedolizumab maintenance:** GEMINI 2 data: vedolizumab q8w superior to placebo for clinical remission at Week 52 (39% vs. 21.6%; RR 1.81; 95% CI 1.26-2.59) and steroid-free remission (RR 2.00).

**Ustekinumab maintenance:** UNITI-IM data: at 44 weeks, clinical remission 51% vs. 35.9% (RR 1.42; 95% CI 1.10-1.84). Steroid-free remission 44.7% vs. 29.8% (RR 1.50). No significant difference between q8w and q12w dosing. Endoscopic remission data limited (subgroup of 70 patients, not significant).

**No head-to-head maintenance trials** exist comparing vedolizumab or ustekinumab with anti-TNF agents.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G1 (ACG 2018) gives similar recommendations but with quality distinctions: anti-TNF maintenance (R49) is "high" quality in G1 vs. "moderate" in G8. Vedolizumab (R51) and ustekinumab (R53) maintenance are "conditional" in G1 vs. "strong" in G8. This is a notable upgrade — ECCO is more confident about non-anti-TNF biologics for maintenance than ACG was in 2018.

**My assessment:** The upgrade from "conditional" (G1) to "strong" (G8) for vedolizumab and ustekinumab maintenance reflects maturing evidence and greater European experience with these agents. The absence of head-to-head maintenance data remains the key evidence gap. The guideline's recommendation to continue the same agent that induced remission ("use the same treatment") is logically sound but avoids the harder question: should patients who remitted on one agent ever be switched?

### Claim 9: Insufficient evidence supports proactive or reactive TDM; immunomodulator withdrawal after achieving remission on combination therapy is suggested (R2.8-R2.9, R2.11-R2.13)

**Recommendation 2.8:** "In Crohn's disease patients in clinical remission under anti-TNF treatment, there is currently insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the use of proactive therapeutic drug monitoring to improve clinical outcomes as compared to routine care." **Weak recommendation, moderate-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 2.9:** "In Crohn's disease patients who have lost response to an anti-TNF agent, there is currently insufficient evidence to recommend for or against the use of reactive therapeutic drug monitoring to improve clinical outcomes." **Weak recommendation, low-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 2.11:** "In patients with Crohn's disease who have achieved long-term remission with the combination of infliximab and immunosuppressants, we suggest monotherapy with infliximab." **Weak recommendation, very low-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 2.12:** Same as R2.11 but for adalimumab. **Weak recommendation, low-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 2.13:** "There is insufficient evidence to recommend either continuation or withdrawal of anti-TNF therapy in Crohn's disease patients after achieving long-term remission. Therefore, the decision to continue anti-TNF therapy should be individualised."

**Evidence summary:**

**Proactive TDM (TAXIT, TAILORIX):** Two RCTs found no advantage of proactive TDM over clinically-based dosing. TAXIT (n=273): clinical remission at 1 year — 66% clinically-based vs. 69% TDM-based (no significant difference), though TDM group had fewer relapses (7% vs. 17%; p=0.018). TAILORIX (n=122): no difference in sustained steroid-free clinical remission with mucosal healing across three arms (33% TDM1, 27% TDM2, 40% control; p=0.50).

**Reactive TDM:** Only one RCT (n=69) compared TDM-guided vs. symptom-based dose intensification in secondary IFX failure — no difference in regaining clinical response (57.6% vs. 52.8%; RR 1.09). However, observational data consistently show TDM-guided approaches are more cost-effective (>10% cost savings) and may improve endoscopic outcomes.

**Immunomodulator withdrawal:** Cochrane review of 2 RCTs — same relapse rate with infliximab monotherapy vs. combination therapy (48% vs. 49%; RR 1.02). For adalimumab, meta-analysis of 9 studies: no difference in maintenance of remission (RR 1.01; 95% CI 0.91-1.13) or serious AEs (RR 0.88).

**Anti-TNF withdrawal:** Systematic review of 23 observational studies (n=920): overall relapse rate 44% (95% CI 36-51%). Relapse rates: 38% at 6 months, 40% at 12 months, 49% at >25 months after discontinuation. No controlled studies exist.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** This is the area of greatest methodological divergence from G1. G1 (ACG 2018) explicitly deferred TDM entirely to a separate guideline (AGA 2017). G8 includes formal GRADE recommendations — even though both are "insufficient evidence" statements. The proactive TDM recommendation (R2.8) has **moderate-quality evidence** (higher than expected) because two large RCTs exist — they're just negative. G8's immunomodulator withdrawal recommendations (R2.11-R2.12) have no direct counterpart in G1. G6 (AGA 2021) will likely have stronger TDM guidance.

**My assessment:** The TDM recommendations are methodologically honest but clinically unsatisfying. "Insufficient evidence" is the right GRADE conclusion from the TAXIT/TAILORIX data, but it leaves clinicians without guidance on one of the most common management questions. The caveat that reactive TDM may offer cost savings is a pragmatic acknowledgment that, even without clinical outcome superiority, knowing drug levels helps avoid futile dose escalation. R2.11-R2.12 (suggesting monotherapy after achieving remission on combination) directly contradicts the G1 approach of continuing combination therapy — this is a major philosophical divergence with practical implications for lymphoma risk.

## Section 3: Perianal Fistulising Disease

### Claim 10: Infliximab is the only biologic with RCT-level evidence for fistula healing; adalimumab has weaker evidence; ustekinumab and vedolizumab lack sufficient evidence (R3.1-R3.5)

**Recommendation 3.1:** "We recommend infliximab for the induction and maintenance of remission in complex perianal fistulae in Crohn's disease." **Strong recommendation, low quality of evidence.**

**Recommendation 3.2:** "We suggest adalimumab may be used for induction and maintenance of remission in complex perianal fistulae in Crohn's disease." **Weak recommendation, very low-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 3.4:** "In patients with Crohn's disease and complex perianal fistula there is insufficient evidence to recommend the use of ustekinumab for fistula healing." **Weak recommendation, moderate-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 3.5:** "In patients with Crohn's disease and complex perianal fistula there is insufficient evidence to recommend the use of vedolizumab for fistula healing." **Weak recommendation, low-quality evidence.**

**Evidence summary:** This is the clearest evidence hierarchy in the entire guideline:

**Infliximab (Gold standard):** Two dedicated RCTs. Induction: 46% vs. 12.9% complete fistula closure (RR 3.57; 95% CI 1.38-9.25). Maintenance (ACCENT II): 34.4% vs. 19.2% at Week 54 (RR 1.79; 95% CI 1.10-2.92). Higher infliximab trough levels associated with better fistula healing in observational data.

**Adalimumab (Secondary endpoint):** CHARM post-hoc analysis (n=117 with fistulae at baseline): superiority over placebo for fistula healing at 56 weeks (RR 2.57; 95% CI 1.13-5.84). CHOICE open-label trial: 39% complete fistula healing after infliximab failure. Evidence was downgraded because fistula healing was a secondary endpoint in a post-hoc analysis.

**Ustekinumab (Post-hoc only):** CERTIFI/UNITI post-hoc (n=238): trend favoring ustekinumab for fistula response but NOT statistically significant for induction (RR 1.77; 95% CI 0.93-3.37) or maintenance.

**Vedolizumab (Post-hoc only):** GEMINI 2 post-hoc (n=45 with complex perianal fistulae): trend favoring vedolizumab (RR 2.23; 95% CI 0.57-8.72), not statistically significant. Dedicated RCT (NCT02630966) prematurely stopped for slow recruitment.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G1 (ACG 2018, R35-R37) gives strong recommendations for infliximab (moderate evidence), adalimumab (low evidence), and certolizumab pegol (low evidence) for perianal fistulas. G8 is MORE conservative on adalimumab (weak, very low evidence) and does not endorse certolizumab for fistulas at all (not approved in EU). This is a significant divergence: ECCO's evidence standards for fistula healing are stricter, reflecting the European regulatory environment where adalimumab is not formally indicated for fistulizing CD.

**My assessment:** G8's fistula recommendations are more conservative than G1's — and likely more honest. The evidence hierarchy (infliximab >>> adalimumab >>> ustekinumab/vedolizumab) is well-supported. Clinicians using adalimumab for fistulas need to understand they're operating on post-hoc secondary endpoint data. The "insufficient evidence" for ustekinumab and vedolizumab in fistulas is an important restraint on off-label use, though the guideline acknowledges these agents may be considered when anti-TNFs are ineffective or contraindicated (narrative text, not a formal recommendation).

### Claim 11: Antibiotics and thiopurines should NOT be used as monotherapy for fistula closure; combination surgical-medical care is standard (R3.3, R3.6-R3.7)

**Recommendation 3.3:** "In patients with Crohn's disease and complex perianal fistula there is insufficient evidence regarding the effect of adding immunomodulators to anti-TNF on fistula healing." **Weak recommendation, very low-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 3.6:** "We suggest against using antibiotics alone for fistula closure in patients with Crohn's disease and complex perianal fistulae." **Weak recommendation, low-quality evidence.**

**Recommendation 3.7:** "We suggest against using thiopurine monotherapy for fistula closure in patients with Crohn's disease and complex perianal fistulae." **Weak recommendation, very low-quality evidence.**

**Evidence summary:**

**Antibiotics:** Only one RCT (n=25) compared antibiotics with placebo: remission at Week 10 in 17.6% vs. 12.5% (RR 1.41; 95% CI 0.17-11.54) — no significant benefit as monotherapy. Ciprofloxacin may improve short-term anti-TNF efficacy but doesn't impact long-term healing. Antibiotics remain indicated for **control of perianal sepsis**, not fistula closure.

**Thiopurines:** Meta-analysis of only 18 patients from RCTs: AZA not superior to placebo for fistula healing (RR 2.00; 95% CI 0.67-5.93). One additional study (Present 1980) reported fistula closure in 31% on MP vs. 6% on placebo but data could not be pooled (reported per fistula, not per patient).

**Combination therapy for fistulas:** A pooled analysis of anti-TNF trials found no significant benefit of adding immunomodulators for fistula closure (OR 1.10; 95% CI 0.68-1.78). However, one retrospective study showed HR 2.58 (95% CI 1.16-5.6) for fistula healing with combination therapy in immunosuppressive-naive patients.

**Surgical co-management:** The guideline states that "joint management and approach by IBD clinicians and surgeons is considered the standard of care." Control of sepsis and prevention of perianal infections before starting immunosuppression is mandatory. Any immunosuppressive treatment must be stopped if septic complications develop.

**Comparison against other guidelines:** G1 (ACG 2018, R38-R43) provides much more detailed surgical guidance for fistulizing disease — specific recommendations for abscess drainage (R42), seton placement (R43), and antibiotic combination with infliximab (R41). G8 is more restrained: it emphasizes surgical co-management in the narrative text but does not issue formal GRADE recommendations on surgical techniques. This reflects the guideline's scope limitation to medical treatment — surgical management is in the separate ECCO surgical guideline.

**My assessment:** The negative recommendations against antibiotic and thiopurine monotherapy for fistulas are valuable practice-changing statements. Too many patients with perianal CD are treated with ciprofloxacin/metronidazole or AZA alone while awaiting specialist referral. The emphasis on surgical co-management is the most important clinical message in this section — and the guideline appropriately reinforces it despite not making formal surgical recommendations. The absence of formal recommendations on abscess drainage and seton placement (unlike G1) is a limitation but understandable given the scope.

## Key Points for Clinical Practice (Guideline Summary)

The guideline provides narrative "Key Points" after each section. The consolidated messages are:

**Induction:**
- Budesonide is first-line for mild-to-moderate ileocecal CD (CDAI 150-220, SES-CD ≤6, CDEIS ≤8)
- 5-ASA/sulfasalazine have no therapeutic effect
- Systemic steroids are effective but limited by toxicity; steroid dependency/excess should trigger a steroid-sparing strategy
- Thiopurines alone are NOT effective for induction but are frequently combined with steroids as a bridge to maintenance
- Anti-TNF agents, ustekinumab, and vedolizumab are all effective in biologic-naive and -experienced populations
- Infliximab + thiopurine combination is more effective than infliximab alone; for adalimumab, combination showed no benefit
- The choice between biologics depends on patient characteristics, preferences, costs, and local availability

**Maintenance:**
- Immunosuppressants (thiopurines, MTX) and biologics are the most effective maintenance therapies
- Aminosalicylates and steroids are not recommended for maintenance
- For mild disease, no therapy with tight monitoring may be considered
- Continue the same biologic that induced remission — no evidence to support switching from a successful agent
- Thiopurine monotherapy withdrawal increases relapse risk; long-term risks (lymphoma, skin cancer) require regular monitoring
- Decision to continue or withdraw anti-TNF in long-term remission is individualized

**Fistulizing disease:**
- Infliximab is first-choice biologic for complex perianal fistulae
- Adalimumab may be considered, especially after infliximab failure due to immunogenicity
- Evidence is insufficient for ustekinumab and vedolizumab in fistula healing
- Combined surgical-medical management is standard of care

**GRADE distribution:** Strong: 16 (50%). Weak: 16 (50%). High-quality evidence: 2 (6%) — R1.8 (ustekinumab induction) and the indirect comparison for R1.10. Moderate: 17 (53%). Low: 9 (28%). Very low: 6 (19%).

### Overall Guideline Strengths

1. **Methodological rigor:** GRADE adoption, PICO framework, Delphi consensus, patient representatives — most methodologically transparent ECCO guideline to date
2. **Differential infliximab vs. adalimumab combination recommendations (R1.6-R1.7):** The single most important clinical contribution — evidence-based differentiation where ACG lumped them together
3. **Honest TDM assessment:** Formal GRADE recommendations concluding evidence is insufficient, despite strong observational signals — methodological integrity over clinical expedience
4. **Structured evidence hierarchy for fistulas:** Clear and honest: IFX (RCT-level) > ADA (secondary endpoint) > UST/VDZ (post-hoc only)
5. **Early thiopurine restraint (R2.3):** Evidence-based negative recommendation against a deeply entrenched European practice pattern
6. **Immunomodulator withdrawal guidance (R2.11-R2.12):** Addresses a common clinical scenario that ACG did not — with appropriate acknowledgment of very low-quality evidence
7. **Patient-centered methodology:** Four patient representatives voting on recommendations — rare in GI guidelines

### Overall Guideline Weaknesses

1. **No postoperative guidance:** Excluded by scope but creates fragmentation — clinicians need both the medical and surgical guidelines to manage a single patient
2. **No treatment algorithm diagram:** Unlike the narrative text's rich clinical guidance, no visual decision pathway exists
3. **No head-to-head biologic comparison guidance:** R1.10 ("equally suggest either") is honest but clinically unhelpful; the guideline could provide a decision framework based on patient characteristics even without comparative trial data
4. **No consensus on methotrexate for induction:** Leaving this as "no recommendation" creates a genuine treatment gap — the Feagan 1995 data, while imperfect, is the best available
5. **No special populations:** Pregnancy, elderly, pediatric transition, and perianal CD specifics are not covered (deferred to separate ECCO topical reviews)
6. **No diet or lifestyle recommendations:** Exclusive enteral nutrition — a distinctively European induction strategy — is not discussed despite its use in pediatric and some adult European centers
7. **Limited biosimilar guidance:** Addressed in narrative but not as formal recommendations, despite biosimilars being more established in European markets than US markets at the time
8. **Reactive TDM recommendation (R2.9) ignores cost-effectiveness data:** The guideline acknowledges reactive TDM may be cost-saving but doesn't let this influence the recommendation

### Strongest Section

**Induction recommendations R1.5-R1.10 (biologic therapy for moderate-to-severe disease).** The differential combination therapy guidance (R1.6-R1.7) is the most clinically valuable contribution of this guideline. The sequencing of evidence from anti-TNF through ustekinumab to vedolizumab is logical and well-supported. The indirect comparison (R1.10) is honest about its limitations.

### Weakest Section

**Maintenance TDM (R2.8-R2.9).** Both recommendations conclude "insufficient evidence" despite two large RCTs and strong observational signals. While methodologically defensible, this leaves a practical vacuum. The guideline would have been stronger with a pragmatic statement: "While RCT evidence is insufficient to mandate TDM, we suggest using reactive TDM when available because it is cost-effective and may prevent futile dose escalation."

### Most Controversial Claim

**R2.3 (against early thiopurine introduction in newly diagnosed CD).** This contradicts decades of European practice based on a single negative trial (AZTEC) in a low-risk population. It represents the most dramatic shift from the traditional European step-up approach toward a more selective, risk-stratified strategy. Whether this recommendation holds up as higher-quality evidence emerges will be a critical test of the guideline's foresight.

### Key Research Gaps Identified

The guideline explicitly identifies:
1. How to treat mild-to-moderate CD localized outside the ileum/right colon
2. Studies on early biologic use vs. stepwise approach focusing on complication prevention
3. Validation of prognostic risk factors for disease progression
4. Large, well-powered RCTs of proactive TDM with adequate patient stratification
5. Head-to-head biologic comparison trials
6. Long-term (>52 week) safety data for monoclonal antibodies
7. Optimal duration of thiopurine maintenance (>18 months)
8. Controlled studies on anti-TNF withdrawal in long-term remission
9. Efficacy of ustekinumab and vedolizumab for fistula healing
10. Role of immunomodulator addition to anti-TNF for fistula outcomes

## Key Take-Home Messages

1. **CD is progressive — treat to prevent complications, not just symptoms.** Objective monitoring (endoscopy, CRP, calprotectin, imaging) is mandatory because symptoms don't correlate with inflammation.
2. **5-ASA doesn't work for CD — stop using it.** Sulfasalazine may have marginal benefit in colonic disease only. Budesonide is effective but limited to ileocecal disease.
3. **Steroids are a bridge, never a destination.** Steroid dependency (can't wean below prednisolone 10 mg/day within 3 months) demands a steroid-sparing strategy.
4. **Thiopurines are for maintenance, not induction.** Don't start them early in newly diagnosed, low-risk disease (R2.3). Once on them and in remission, continuation prevents relapse but carries long-term risks.
5. **Infliximab should be combined with a thiopurine; adalimumab may not need to be (R1.6-R1.7).** This evidence-based differentiation is the guideline's signature contribution.
6. **Ustekinumab has the highest-quality evidence for induction (high).** Vedolizumab is also effective (moderate evidence). No head-to-head data differentiate them in anti-TNF failures — choose based on patient factors.
7. **Infliximab is the only biologic with RCT-level evidence for fistula healing.** Adalimumab has weaker (post-hoc) evidence. Ustekinumab and vedolizumab lack sufficient evidence — use only when anti-TNFs are contraindicated and concomitant luminal disease is present.
8. **Proactive TDM is not yet supported by RCT evidence.** Reactive TDM may be cost-effective even without proven clinical superiority. The field needs better trials.
9. **Combined medical-surgical care is standard for complex perianal disease.** Control sepsis before immunosuppression. Surgery is always an alternative in refractory luminal disease.
10. **Patient preferences matter.** The inclusion of EFCCA patient representatives in the voting process acknowledges that treatment decisions involve values and trade-offs — not just efficacy data.
