# pimentel-ch4-1: Food poisoning causes approximately 10% of cases to develop post-infectious IBS

**Source:** [[Pimentel 2022 - Ch4 Food Poisoning Autoimmunity]] — Pimentel & Rezaie — The Microbiome Connection (2022), Ch 4

## The Claim

We now know that one out of nine people who experience food poisoning go on to develop IBS.

## Evidence

Mayo Clinic meta-analysis of 45 published food poisoning outbreaks (2017). Pimentel's CDC data-modeling study. European studies of Salmonella outbreaks showing persistent IBS-like bowel dysfunction. The historical context: Sir Arthur Hearst first described post-infectious IBS in 1918 in Medical Diseases of War, documenting soldiers with dysentery who developed alternating constipation and diarrhea after the bacteria had "died out."

## Stakes

This establishes that IBS is not idiopathic -- it has a known trigger in a substantial proportion of cases. If food poisoning causes IBS, then (a) preventing food poisoning prevents IBS, (b) treating food poisoning aggressively may reduce post-infectious IBS risk, and (c) post-infectious IBS should have a specific diagnostic test and treatment protocol.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** No serious disagreement about the 10% figure. The debate is whether post-infectious IBS represents a distinct subset or whether food poisoning is simply one of several triggers for a common final pathway.

## Assessment

The post-infectious IBS prevalence data is solid. Pimentel's contribution was not discovering this connection (European researchers identified Salmonella-IBS links in the 1990s) but rather identifying the molecular mechanism (CdtB-vinculin autoimmunity) and developing a diagnostic test.
