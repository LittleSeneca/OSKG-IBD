# pimentel-ch5-3: The three-gas model explains SIBO symptomatology: hydrogen (bloating), methane/IMO (constipation), hydrogen sulfide (diarrhea)

**Source:** [[Pimentel 2022 - Ch5 SIBO Definition and Diagnosis]] — Pimentel & Rezaie — The Microbiome Connection (2022), Ch 5

## The Claim

There are several subpopulations of microorganisms in SIBO, depending on which gas they produce after metabolizing food. One group of bacteria produces hydrogen, which can lead to bloating. Another group produces hydrogen sulfide, which usually leads to diarrhea, pain, and an urgent need to run to the bathroom. A third group of microorganisms is not bacteria, but rather archaea: single-celled organisms that produce methane. Methane affects normal movement in the gastrointestinal tract, and leads to spastic contractions that slow down propagation of contents through the bowel. That's why constipation is very common in this group of patients.

## Evidence

Breath test data showing distinct hydrogen, methane, and hydrogen sulfide patterns correlated with symptom profiles. The mechanism: hydrogen is produced first (by E. coli, Klebsiella), then scavenged by either methanogens (producing methane → constipation) or sulfate-reducing bacteria (producing hydrogen sulfide → diarrhea). The "winner" of the hydrogen competition determines symptoms.

## Stakes

The three-gas model transforms SIBO from a single disease to a family of related conditions distinguished by dominant gas type. Treatment should be gas-type-specific: rifaximin for hydrogen SIBO, different approaches for IMO (methane), and still-developing approaches for hydrogen sulfide SIBO. The model also explains why some SIBO patients have diarrhea while others have constipation -- it's not random variation but reflects different microbial populations.

## Assessment

The hydrogen/methane distinction is well-established and clinically actionable. The hydrogen sulfide component is newer and needs more validation -- the hydrogen sulfide breath test is not yet commercially available. The "foxes and wolves" competition model is a useful heuristic but likely oversimplifies a more complex microbial ecosystem. Nonetheless, this is the most clinically useful conceptual framework for SIBO subtyping.
