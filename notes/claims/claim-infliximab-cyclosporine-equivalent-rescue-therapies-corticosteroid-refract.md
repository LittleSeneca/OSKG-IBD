# aga-uc2020-10: Infliximab and cyclosporine are equivalent rescue therapies for corticosteroid-refractory ASUC

**Source:** [[AGA UC 2020 - Feuerstein]] — Feuerstein et al. — AGA Clinical Practice Guidelines: Ulcerative Colitis (2020)

## The Claim

Infliximab and cyclosporine are equivalent rescue therapies for corticosteroid-refractory ASUC

## Evidence

Infliximab vs. placebo: single RCT (n=45), single 5 mg/kg dose, RR for 90-day colectomy 0.44 (0.22-0.87). Cyclosporine vs. placebo: single small RCT, RR 0.61 (0.18-2.01) — trend favoring cyclosporine but not statistically significant. Cyclosporine 2 mg/kg/day comparable to 4 mg/kg/day with better safety. Two head-to-head trials (CYSIF, CONSTRUCT): no difference in short-term colectomy between infliximab and cyclosporine (RR 1.00; 0.72-1.40). Long-term CYSIF follow-up (median 4.5 years): colectomy-free survival 61.5% (cyclosporine) vs. 65.1% (infliximab), p=0.97. Notable: after initial randomization period, 45.7% of cyclosporine patients later used infliximab (at 1 year), rising to 57.1% (at 5 years); only 4 infliximab patients switched to cyclosporine. Drug survival favors infliximab in practice.

## Assessment

The long-term CYSIF data are the most clinically informative: cyclosporine is a bridge, not a destination. Most cyclosporine-treated patients eventually transition to infliximab. This reflects the practical reality that cyclosporine requires therapeutic drug monitoring, has significant toxicity (nephrotoxicity, hypertension, seizures), and is not a maintenance therapy. Infliximab can be continued as maintenance if effective. The AGA doesn't explicitly say "prefer infliximab over cyclosporine" — but the practical implication of the long-term data is that infliximab is the preferred rescue agent in most scenarios, with cyclosporine reserved for infliximab-refractory patients or those with contraindications to anti-TNF therapy. Both guidelines' equipoise (RR 1.00) reflects short-term outcomes; long-term treatment patterns tell a different story.
