# yamada-cddx64-1: The clinical presentation of CD is heterogeneous -- symptoms depend on anatomic location, inflammatory phenotype, and presence of complications

**Source:** [[Yamada 2022 - Ch64 Crohns Disease Diagnosis]] — Yamada's Textbook of Gastroenterology, 7E (2022), Ch 64

## The Claim

The clinical presentation of patients with CD is diverse and heterogeneous, with symptoms that can range from silent, insidious progression of mid-small bowel inflammation that might be misdiagnosed for years as 'irritable bowel syndrome' to a more acute presentation of diarrhea and/or weight loss, GI hemorrhage, perianal abscess/fistula, or intestinal obstruction.

## Evidence

Disease distribution: terminal ileum most common (60% have at least some ileal involvement); about half of ileal patients also have colonic involvement (usually right colon); ~20% isolated colonic involvement; ~30% confined to small bowel; ~5% upper tract (esophagus, stomach, duodenum, jejunum). Perianal disease in ~25% of patients, usually associated with distal colonic or small bowel inflammation. Symptoms correlate with location: dysphagia/odynophagia/nausea with upper tract; right lower quadrant pain and diarrhea with ileal disease; rectal bleeding and urgency with colonic involvement; perianal pain and drainage with fistulizing perianal disease. Up to 20% of CD patients are obese (not all have cachexia). In 5-15% of isolated colonic cases, UC vs CD cannot be initially distinguished (IBD-U/indeterminate colitis). Key differentiating features from UC at presentation: nonbloody diarrhea and weight loss favor CD; endoscopic/histological patchiness or rectal sparing favors CD; family history of CD favors CD.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high
**Rationale:** HIGH. This is the standard clinical teaching and is consistent with population-based cohort data.

## Stakes

The insidious onset means diagnostic delay is common -- patients may have symptoms for months or years before diagnosis. Misdiagnosis as IBS is especially common in patients with non-stricturing, non-penetrating small bowel CD without rectal bleeding or weight loss. The chapter emphasizes: "there are no hard and fast 'rules'" -- CD can present at any age, in any ethnicity, and at any BMI.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** No meaningful disagreement about clinical heterogeneity. Debate exists about whether IBD-U is a distinct entity or simply an early stage of either CD or UC.

**Alternative reading:** Not documented.

## Assessment

The clinical heterogeneity of CD is both its defining feature and its diagnostic challenge. The chapter's emphasis on obtaining a detailed chronological history is well-placed -- many CD diagnoses are missed because clinicians stop at "IBS" without asking whether symptoms predated the IBS label.
