# gottschall-ch35-1: Bacterial overgrowth in the small intestine occurs through multiple mechanisms and produces disease

**Source:** [[Gottschall 1994 - Ch3-5 The Vicious Cycle Mechanism]] — Gottschall — Breaking the Vicious Cycle (1994), Ch 3-5

## The Claim

Bacterial overgrowth in the stomach and small intestine can and does occur for various reasons among which are: (1) Interference with the high acidity of the stomach through the continual use of antacids; (2) A decrease in the acidity of the stomach such as occurs in the aging process; (3) Malnutrition or a diet of poor quality, and the resulting weakening of the body's immune system; (4) Antibiotic therapy which can cause a wide range of microbial changes.

## Evidence

References to published literature on gastric acid as a microbial barrier, peristalsis as a clearance mechanism, B12 malabsorption as a marker of small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, and historical stool studies (1904) showing abnormally large numbers of fermentative and putrefactive bacteria in children with celiac-type disease.

## Stakes

If bacterial overgrowth is the initiating event in the vicious cycle, then SCD works downstream (starving the overgrowth) rather than addressing the root cause (what allowed the overgrowth). This distinction is critical: SCD may control symptoms without curing the underlying motility/acid/immune defect.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Pimentel would argue that the root cause is often autoimmune damage to the migrating motor complex (post-infectious IBS/SIBO via anti-vinculin antibodies), which dietary change cannot fix. SCD is therefore a management strategy, not a cure, for SIBO with an autoimmune etiology.

**Alternative reading:** Not documented.

## Assessment

Gottschall was remarkably prescient about bacterial overgrowth mechanisms in 1994, predating Pimentel's formal SIBO-IBS model by 12 years. She correctly identifies the key protective mechanisms (acid, peristalsis, immune function) and their failure modes. The limitation is that she frames diet as sufficient to correct the overgrowth, when modern evidence suggests prokinetics or antibiotics may be needed for the underlying motility defect.
