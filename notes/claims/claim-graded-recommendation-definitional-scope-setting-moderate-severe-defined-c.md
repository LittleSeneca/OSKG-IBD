# aga-uc2020-1: The guideline explicitly defines moderate-severe UC by objective criteria — Truelove & Witts and Mayo Clinic score — and targets a high-risk population

**Source:** [[AGA UC 2020 - Feuerstein]] — Feuerstein et al. — AGA Clinical Practice Guidelines: Ulcerative Colitis (2020)

## The Claim

Not a graded recommendation (definitional, scope-setting). Moderate-severe UC is defined by: (a) corticosteroid dependence or refractoriness, (b) severe endoscopic disease (presence of ulcers), or (c) high risk of colectomy. Mayo Clinic score 6-12 with endoscopic subscore ≥2. ASUC is defined as hospitalized patients with ≥6 bloody bowel movements/day AND at least one marker of systemic toxicity (HR >90, temperature >37.8°C, Hb <10.5 g/dL, and/or ESR >30 mm/h). Predictors of aggressive disease: young age at diagnosis (<40), extensive disease, deep ulcers, extra-intestinal manifestations, early corticosteroid need, elevated inflammatory markers.

## Evidence

**GRADE rating:** Not graded (scope-setting).

Population-based cohort data cited: 15% of UC patients have an aggressive course; 20% of these require hospitalization. 5- and 10-year cumulative colectomy risk: 10-15%, concentrated in moderate-severe patients. Hospitalized ASUC patients have short-term colectomy rates of 25-30%. The guideline uses a dual definition (Truelove & Witts severity + Mayo endoscopic severity) that is more specific than the ACG guideline's broader severity categories.

## Assessment

The dual-criteria definition (Mayo + Truelove & Witts) creates more specific entry criteria for clinical trials but may not perfectly map to clinical practice where "moderate-severe" is often a gestalt. The separate guideline for mild-moderate UC (Ko et al. 2018) means a clinician managing a patient who escalates from mild to moderate disease must consult two different AGA documents. This is less convenient than the ACG's single-document approach.
