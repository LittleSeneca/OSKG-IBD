# steinhart-drug-1: Mucosal healing -- not just symptom control -- should be the goal of IBD therapy

**Source:** [[Steinhart 2018 - Ch7 Drug Therapy]] — Steinhart — Crohn's and Colitis: Understanding and Managing IBD, 3E (2018), Ch 7

## The Claim

There has been a move toward making elimination of active inflammation and healing of ulcers -- called mucosal healing -- a goal of therapy in IBD.

## Evidence

Clinical observation and the natural history of Crohn's complications. Steinhart acknowledges that mucosal healing "is a higher hurdle to achieve than remission or elimination of symptoms" and that complete healing "may be an unrealistic goal" in long-standing Crohn's. He notes that colonoscopy or imaging -- not just blood/stool tests -- is required to confirm mucosal healing, adding patient burden.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high
**Rationale:** HIGH. Mucosal healing as a treatment target is now the standard in IBD care, validated by the CALM trial (tight control vs symptom-based management) and endorsed by STRIDE-II guidelines. Steinhart's f

## Stakes

If mucosal healing is the real goal, then medications must be assessed not just by symptom relief but by endoscopic remission. This disqualifies some therapies (e.g., steroids do not produce sustained mucosal healing) and elevates others (biologics, thiopurines). It also means regular objective monitoring -- calprotectin, colonoscopy, imaging -- is medically necessary, not optional.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** No major disagreement on the concept. The debate is practical: how much healing is enough, whether complete healing is achievable in all patients, and whether the cost/inconvenience of repeated scopes is justified for all patients.

**Alternative reading:** Some clinicians argue that symptom-based management with escalation on flare is sufficient for mild disease and that mucosal healing as a universal goal overtreats patients who would do well with less aggressive approaches.

## Assessment

Steinhart presents this as an emerging consensus, which it was in 2018. The CALM trial (2017) had just demonstrated that tight control with objective targets produces better outcomes than symptom-driven care. This claim has aged well -- it has only been reinforced since 2018.
