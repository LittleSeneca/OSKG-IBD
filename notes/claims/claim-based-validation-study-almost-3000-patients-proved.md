# pimentel-ch4-3: The anti-CdtB/anti-vinculin blood test diagnoses IBS-D and IBS-M with >90% certainty, 98% if both markers are positive

**Source:** [[Pimentel 2022 - Ch4 Food Poisoning Autoimmunity]] — Pimentel & Rezaie — The Microbiome Connection (2022), Ch 4

## The Claim

Based on a validation study of almost 3000 patients, we proved that we can diagnose IBS with greater than 90 percent certainty over other diarrheal diseases... an improved test that can diagnose IBS with 98 percent post-test probability, if both markers are positive.

## Evidence

The 3000-patient validation study. The antibody kinetics: anti-CdtB appears first (marker of recent CdtB toxin exposure), anti-vinculin develops later (marker of established autoimmunity). The test's limitations: "It's not useful in identifying patients who have IBS with constipation. In addition, a negative test doesn't mean that you don't have IBS, as IBS may still be caused by other mechanisms." Positivity rate: "up to 60 percent of those with IBS-D and IBS-M test positive."

**Rating:** high

## Stakes

A positive blood test for IBS would transform clinical practice: eliminating the diagnosis-by-exclusion paradigm, reducing the 4-6 year diagnostic delay, avoiding $20,000-30,000 in unnecessary testing, and providing patients with the validation that their disease is organic. The test also stratifies patients: antibody-positive IBS is post-infectious/autoimmune and should receive SIBO-directed treatment; antibody-negative IBS may have different mechanisms.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The Rome Foundation and some academic gastroenterologists have been cautious about adopting the blood test, arguing that it identifies only the post-infectious subset and that broader validation is needed. Insurers may resist covering it without independent validation.

**Alternative reading:** The blood test may be identifying previous food poisoning exposure rather than active IBS -- many people have had food poisoning without developing IBS. The 60% positivity rate in IBS-D/M means 40% are negative, and the test does not address IBS-C at all.

## Assessment

The blood test is a genuinely significant advance, but its clinical utility is limited by its restriction to IBS-D/M and its 60% sensitivity. As a "rule-in" test (positive = IBS confirmed), it has high specificity. As a screening test (negative does not rule out IBS), it has limited sensitivity. This is not unusual for diagnostic tests -- the ANA test for lupus has similar characteristics (high specificity, limited sensitivity). The test's greatest value may be in confirming the organic nature of IBS for patients who have been dismissed, not in replacing the Rome Criteria entirely.
