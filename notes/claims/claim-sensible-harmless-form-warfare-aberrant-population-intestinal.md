# gottschall-ch35-3: Dietary manipulation of microbial energy supply is a "sensible and harmless form of warfare" on aberrant microbes

**Source:** [[Gottschall 1994 - Ch3-5 The Vicious Cycle Mechanism]] — Gottschall — Breaking the Vicious Cycle (1994), Ch 3-5

## The Claim

A sensible and harmless form of warfare on the aberrant population of intestinal microbes is to manipulate their energy (food) supply through diet. Most intestinal microbes require carbohydrates for energy, and the Specific Carbohydrate Diet severely limits the availability of carbohydrates. By depriving intestinal microbes of their energy source, their numbers gradually decrease along with the products they produce.

## Evidence

General microbial physiology -- most gut bacteria are saccharolytic (carbohydrate-fermenting). The principle is logical: starve the microbes of their preferred substrate and their population declines.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high
**Rationale:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the principle; LOW for the claim that dietary restriction alone can eliminate an established overgrowth in all patients. The principle is sound: reducing fermentable substrate reaching

## Stakes

This is the therapeutic rationale for ALL fermentable-carbohydrate-restricted diets -- SCD, low-FODMAP, LFE, SIBO Specific Food Guide. If the principle is correct, these diets all work through the same mechanism despite different food lists.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Microbiome ecologists would note that carbohydrate restriction also starves beneficial bacteria (Bifidobacteria, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii), potentially reducing SCFA production and compromising gut barrier function long-term. The goal is selective reduction of fermentable substrate, not total carbohydrate starvation.

**Alternative reading:** The diet may work not by starving bacteria but by reducing osmotic diarrhea (undigested carbs draw water into the lumen) and gas production (reducing bloating and pain), without substantially changing bacterial counts.

## Assessment

The "starve the microbes" framing is rhetorically effective but mechanistically oversimplified. Modern evidence suggests dietary carbohydrate restriction in SIBO/IBD primarily reduces gas, bloating, and osmotic diarrhea rather than substantially altering microbial populations. The bacterial count reduction may be modest; the symptom improvement may come from reduced fermentation products with the same bacterial load.
