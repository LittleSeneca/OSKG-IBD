# yamada-cddx64-2: Cross-sectional enterography (CTE/MRE) has replaced barium follow-through as first-line imaging for CD; MRE is preferred for perianal disease

**Source:** [[Yamada 2022 - Ch64 Crohns Disease Diagnosis]] — Yamada's Textbook of Gastroenterology, 7E (2022), Ch 64

## The Claim

Cross-sectional imaging modalities (CT and magnetic resonance imaging) with enterography protocols have largely replaced small bowel follow-through for the routine evaluation of known and suspected CD. These modalities are first-line imaging techniques that can rapidly and accurately identify areas of mucosal inflammation, fibrostenotic and penetrating complications, and many EIMs of CD.

## Evidence

CTE advantages: rapid image acquisition, lower cost, high accuracy for small bowel inflammation. MRE advantages: no ionizing radiation, superior soft tissue contrast, better for fistula and perianal disease characterization, validated scoring instrument (MRE global score) for serial quantitative assessment. Both require adequate small bowel distension with oral contrast and use IV contrast to identify mucosal hyperenhancement. Classic findings: "comb sign" (engorged vasa recta, highly specific for active inflammation), "target sign" (trilaminar enhancement: bright mucosa, dark submucosal edema, bright serosa). Small bowel ultrasound (SBUS) with IV contrast is an emerging alternative -- advantages of low cost, no radiation, speed -- but is operator-dependent and may be less sensitive than MRE for detecting extent and activity of small bowel CD. Capsule endoscopy is highly sensitive for mucosal lesions missed on cross-sectional imaging but limited by retention risk (~2-5% in CD), requiring patency assessment first. Balloon enteroscopy allows biopsies and therapeutic interventions deep in the small bowel but is reserved for targeted evaluation after capsule endoscopy.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high
**Rationale:** HIGH. The superiority of cross-sectional enterography over barium studies is well-established. The CTE vs MRE trade-off is the standard clinical teaching.

## Stakes

Choice of imaging modality has significant consequences: CTE exposes young patients (CD typically diagnosed in 20s-30s) to cumulative radiation with repeated studies over a lifetime. MRE avoids radiation but is more expensive and less accessible. For perianal CD, MRE is unequivocally superior -- Figure 64.14 demonstrates the dramatic difference in fistula visualization. The chapter's recommendation of MRE for perianal disease and CTE as an acceptable alternative for small bowel inflammation alone reflects the standard of care.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Some centers advocate small bowel ultrasound as a first-line imaging modality (particularly in Europe), reserving MRE/CTE for cases where ultrasound is inconclusive or where therapeutic decisions require precise anatomic mapping. The chapter notes this debate: SBUS "may be less sensitive than MRE for detecting the presence, extent, and activity of small bowel CD."

**Alternative reading:** Not documented.

## Assessment

The imaging section is one of the best in this chapter. The descriptions of comb sign and target sign are clear. The MRE preference for perianal disease is unequivocally correct -- CT simply cannot resolve fistula anatomy relative to sphincter muscles the way MRE can. For clinical practice: MRE for perianal CD and serial monitoring in young patients; CTE acceptable for acute presentations where speed matters or MRE is unavailable.
