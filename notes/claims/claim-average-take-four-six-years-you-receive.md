# pimentel-ch1-4: The IBS diagnosis-by-exclusion paradigm causes systematic harm through delay, cost, and unnecessary procedures

**Source:** [[Pimentel 2022 - Intro Ch1 IBS and SIBO Overlap]] — Pimentel & Rezaie — The Microbiome Connection (2022), Intro + Ch1

## The Claim

On average, it might take four to six years for you to receive a diagnosis of IBS, usually after undergoing a variety of costly and invasive diagnostic procedures... We've had patients who spent $20,000 in out-of-pocket co-pays for a variety of investigations before the diagnosis of IBS was finally settled on.

## Evidence

A study finding that "79 percent of community clinicians diagnosed IBS via exclusion, which resulted in more tests for their patients and significantly raised the direct costs of patient care." The list of unnecessary procedures: "stool tests, colonoscopies, upper endoscopies, computed tomography scans, ultrasounds, and x-rays," plus "appendectomies, hysterectomies." The NIH funding figure: $5 million/year for IBS research, amounting to "8 cents per IBS patient."

## Stakes

Pimentel is building the case for his alternative: a positive diagnostic test (breath testing for SIBO) that can short-circuit the exclusion paradigm. If SIBO breath testing can positively identify the IBS subgroup with bacterial overgrowth, the diagnostic odyssey becomes unnecessary for those patients.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Some gastroenterologists argue that the exclusion approach is appropriate because IBS symptoms overlap with IBD, celiac disease, and colorectal cancer -- conditions where delayed diagnosis has serious consequences. The cost of missed Crohn's disease or colon cancer exceeds the cost of unnecessary colonoscopies.

**Alternative reading:** The diagnosis-by-exclusion problem may be more attributable to the Rome Criteria's limitations than to physician behavior. If the criteria themselves require exclusion of other diseases first, clinicians following guidelines appropriately will produce diagnostic delays.

## Assessment

Pimentel's critique of the exclusion paradigm is compelling and well-supported. The NIH funding disparity ($5 million/year for a condition affecting 45 million Americans vs. the $21 billion economic burden) is genuinely scandalous. The question is whether breath testing for SIBO is reliable enough to replace the exclusion paradigm. The ACG 2020 guideline acknowledges breath testing's utility but stops short of recommending it as a standalone diagnostic tool.
