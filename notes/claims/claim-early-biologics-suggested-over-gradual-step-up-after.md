# aga-uc2020-7: Early biologics are suggested over gradual step-up after 5-ASA failure in high-risk patients — but the evidence is entirely indirect

**Source:** [[AGA UC 2020 - Feuerstein]] — Feuerstein et al. — AGA Clinical Practice Guidelines: Ulcerative Colitis (2020)

## The Claim

Early biologics are suggested over gradual step-up after 5-ASA failure in high-risk patients — but the evidence is entirely indirect

## Evidence

No studies directly compared early biologics vs. step-up therapy. Three studies compared thiopurines vs. 5-ASA in corticosteroid-exposed patients; thiopurines achieved higher corticosteroid-free remission rates. Extrapolating from PICOs 3 and 4: biologics > thiopurines > 5-ASA. The guideline reasons that 5-ASAs have not been studied in moderate-severe disease (their evidence base is in mild-moderate), and that "delaying effective treatment... may be harmful due to ongoing untreated active disease, increasing risk of UC-related complications, hospitalization, colectomy and overall inferior quality of life."

**Recommendation 7:** "Against continuing 5-ASA in patients who have achieved remission with biologics and/or immunomodulators or tofacitinib, after prior 5-ASA failure." **Conditional, very low quality.**

**Evidence summary:** One RCT: azathioprine + olsalazine vs. azathioprine alone — no difference in relapse at 2 years (RR 1.02; 0.77-1.34). Subgroup analysis of biologic/tofacitinib RCTs: no difference in induction or maintenance of remission between patients on vs. not on concomitant 5-ASA at trial entry (induction RR 0.94; 0.74-1.18; maintenance RR 0.92; 0.78-1.09). The guideline explicitly states it did not factor in chemoprevention (5-ASA and colorectal cancer risk), citing recent data that "sustained remission is protective regardless of the type of therapy used."

## Assessment

Recommendation 7 (stop 5-ASA) is the most practice-changing take-home message for many community gastroenterologists. The evidence is thin (very low quality) but the logic is sound: if 5-ASA failed to control the disease in the first place, continuing it after escalation adds cost, pill burden, and potential side effects without demonstrable benefit. The chemoprevention question is the main counterargument, and the guideline's dismissal of it (citing Rubin 2013 on inflammation as the true risk factor) may not satisfy all clinicians. In patients with extensive colitis and longstanding disease, many will still continue mesalamine for perceived chemopreventive benefit, guideline or not.
