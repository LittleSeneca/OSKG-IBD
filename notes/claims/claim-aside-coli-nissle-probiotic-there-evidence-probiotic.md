# steinhart-drug-10: Probiotics and FMT are unproven for IBD despite mechanistic plausibility and patient interest

**Source:** [[Steinhart 2018 - Ch7 Drug Therapy]] — Steinhart — Crohn's and Colitis: Understanding and Managing IBD, 3E (2018), Ch 7

## The Claim

Aside from the E. coli Nissle probiotic, there is no evidence that any probiotic, including VSL#3, is effective in improving outcomes in ulcerative colitis or Crohn's disease.

## Evidence

E. coli Nissle for UC maintenance (roughly equivalent to 5-ASA). VSL#3 for pouchitis prevention (some positive studies). FMT studies have "huge variation in how FMT is carried out" making results impossible to interpret. Steinhart notes donor selection, processing, delivery route, and timing all vary dramatically between studies.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high
**Rationale:** HIGH for the claim that probiotics/FMT are unproven for luminal IBD. The evidence has not substantially changed since 2018 -- FMT remains investigational for UC (some positive signals in selected tria

## Stakes

Patient interest in microbiome-directed therapies is high and will likely increase. The gap between patient expectation and evidence is wide. Unregulated probiotic products make claims that exceed the data.

## Assessment

Steinhart's skepticism is appropriate and evidence-based. His willingness to say "this appeals to many IBD sufferers and merits further study" while clearly stating the evidence limitations is the right tone for a patient-facing book.

**Strongest section:** The steroid section is the most detailed and clinically useful for patients, distinguishing common/reversible from uncommon/irreversible side effects with unusual honesty. The biologic sections are practical, addressing administration route, antibody formation, and safety in accessible terms.

**Weakest section:** The CAM section is relatively superficial for a patient-facing book. Given that "up to half of IBD sufferers have tried CAM," a more detailed evidence table for specific CAM therapies (aloe, curcumin, Boswellia, cannabis) would have been valuable. The cannabis section correctly notes that marijuana helps symptoms but not inflammation, but doesn't address CBD or the cannabinoid-derivative trials in sufficient detail.

**Overall quality:** This is an excellent patient-facing drug therapy overview. Steinhart writes with clinical authority and genuine respect for the patient's decision-making role. The case studies (Jonathan, Naveen, Rebecca) ground the pharmacology in real clinical decisions. The treatment philosophy sections (mucosal healing, step-up vs top-down, personalized medicine) give patients the conceptual framework to understand why specific drugs are recommended, not just what they do.
