# pimentel-ch1-3: The treatment landscape for IBS has transformed since Pimentel's 2006 book

**Source:** [[Pimentel 2022 - Intro Ch1 IBS and SIBO Overlap]] — Pimentel & Rezaie — The Microbiome Connection (2022), Intro + Ch1

## The Claim

When Dr. Mark Pimentel's book was published, there were few treatment options for IBS or SIBO; in fact, 10 years ago there were only a few drugs approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to treat IBS. Today there are eight drugs for IBS on the market: five drugs to treat IBS with constipation and three drugs to treat IBS with diarrhea.

## Evidence

The eight FDA-approved IBS drugs, including rifaximin (the antibiotic that "can eliminate or lessen bacteria in the gut") and motility-modifying agents. The authors also note the evolution of dietary approaches: from "avoid fiber and legumes" to gluten-free and low-FODMAP to their own Low-Fermentation Eating protocol.

**Rating:** high
**Rationale:** HIGH (the drug count is a verifiable fact). The more important claim -- that treatment is now more effective because it targets underlying mechanisms rather than symptoms -- is supported by rifaximin'

## Stakes

This contextualizes the book's argument: SIBO treatment is now evidence-based and FDA-supported, not experimental. The contrast between the 2006 book's limited options and 2022's expanded arsenal strengthens the case that SIBO medicine has matured.

## Assessment

The eight-drug count slightly overstates the case -- several are IBS-C drugs (lubiprostone, linaclotide, plecanatide, tegaserod, tenapanor) that don't target the SIBO mechanism Pimentel proposes. Only rifaximin directly targets gut bacteria. The three IBS-D drugs include eluxadoline (mixed opioid receptor modulator) and alosetron (5-HT3 antagonist), which are not SIBO-directed. Pimentel is correct that the landscape is better, but the drugs he lists don't all support his specific thesis.
