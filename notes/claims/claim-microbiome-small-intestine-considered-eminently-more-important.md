# pimentel-ch3-2: The small intestinal microbiome is more clinically important than the colonic microbiome for metabolic and functional GI diseases

**Source:** [[Pimentel 2022 - Ch3 Gut Microbiome]] — Pimentel & Rezaie — The Microbiome Connection (2022), Ch 3

## The Claim

The microbiome in the small intestine may be considered eminently more important than the microbiome in the stool. The interaction between the microbiome and the large absorbing surface area of the small intestine may indicate how some people become diabetic or obese. More importantly, for the purposes of this book, SIBO and IBS-related symptoms are directly related to how your microbiome reacts in the small intestine.

## Evidence

The small intestine's enormous surface area ("if you stretched out the small intestine and its villi, it would cover the surface area of a tennis court") and its role as the primary site of nutrient absorption and immune interaction. The methanogen-obesity hypothesis: methane slows transit, increasing caloric absorption. The REIMAGINE study's finding that small intestine composition is relatively consistent along its length.

## Stakes

If the small intestinal microbiome is indeed more clinically important, then research funding, diagnostic development, and therapeutic targeting should shift from stool to small intestinal sampling. This would require the kind of invasive sampling (endoscopy with sterile aspiration) that Pimentel's lab has developed.

## Assessment

This argument serves Pimentel's broader thesis (SIBO is a small intestine disease) but may overstate the case. The colon's microbiome has well-established roles in SCFA production, immune regulation, and drug metabolism that are clearly clinically important. The more defensible claim is that both the small intestinal AND colonic microbiomes matter, and they matter for different diseases.
