# pimentel-ch9-2: Antidepressants and antipsychotics have no FDA approval for IBS and are used primarily for their side effects (constipation), not their primary mechanism

**Source:** [[Pimentel 2022 - Ch9 Refractory SIBO]] — Pimentel & Rezaie — The Microbiome Connection (2022), Ch 9

## The Claim

There is no antidepressant or drug with antidepressant/antipsychotic property approved for treatment of IBS by the FDA or European or Asian drug agencies.

## Evidence

Pimentel's published harm analysis using "number needed to harm" (NNH). Meta-analysis problems: small negative studies unpublished (publication bias), different drugs pooled (apples and oranges), variable methods. Side effects of tricyclics: dry mouth, constipation, heart rhythm problems, impotence.

## Stakes

The antidepressant-for-IBS practice reinforces the psychological framing of IBS that Pimentel has spent his career fighting. If IBS is an organic autoimmune disease, treating it with antidepressants (for their constipating side effect) is both conceptually wrong and, per Pimentel's data, more harmful than SIBO-directed therapy.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Many gastroenterologists (and the ACG guideline) support low-dose tricyclic antidepressants for IBS, particularly for pain-predominant IBS. The American College of Gastroenterology's 2021 guideline gives tricyclics a "strong recommendation" for global IBS symptoms. The psychogastroenterology field would argue that neuromodulators work through central and peripheral pain modulation, not just through constipating side effects.

**Alternative reading:** Not documented.

## Assessment

Pimentel's antidepressant skepticism is consistent with his SIBO model but arguably too dismissive. Tricyclic antidepressants have a legitimate role in IBS management, particularly for visceral hypersensitivity, which is a component of IBS independent of SIBO. The NNH comparison (2.3 vs. 846) is dramatic but may not be comparing like with like (different trial designs, patient populations, outcome measures). Pimentel is right that antidepressants should not be first-line for SIBO-positive IBS, but his blanket dismissal goes further than the evidence supports.
