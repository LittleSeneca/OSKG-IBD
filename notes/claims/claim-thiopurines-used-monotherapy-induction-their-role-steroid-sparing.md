# ecco-cd2020-4: Thiopurines should NOT be used as monotherapy for induction; their role is in steroid-sparing maintenance (R1.4)

**Source:** [[ECCO Crohn's Medical 2020 - Torres]] — Torres et al. — ECCO Guidelines on Crohn's Disease: Medical Management (2020)

## The Claim

Thiopurines should NOT be used as monotherapy for induction; their role is in steroid-sparing maintenance (R1.4)

## Evidence

Pooled analysis of 5 trials (n=380): no difference for induction of remission between thiopurines and placebo (48% vs. 37%; RR 1.23; 95% CI 0.97-1.55). Clinical response data from 3 trials: 42.8% vs. 26.9% (RR 1.87; 95% CI 0.44-7.96) with serious heterogeneity (I²=69%) and very serious imprecision. Serious AEs: 13.5% for AZA vs. 3.8% for placebo (RR 2.57; 95% CI 0.92-7.13). Thiopurines have a slow onset of action (8-12 weeks) making them unsuitable for acute induction. No consistent improvement in biomarkers (CRP, ESR, calprotectin) was observed during induction.

**Cross-guideline comparison:**
G1 (ACG 2018, R19) gives a **strong** recommendation against thiopurines for induction — stronger than ECCO's "weak." Both agree on the core message: don't use thiopurines to induce remission. The difference in strength reflects ECCO's acknowledgment that thiopurines ARE commonly started alongside steroids (as a bridge to maintenance), blurring the "induction" vs. "steroid-sparing" distinction.

## Assessment

The clinical reality is that thiopurines ARE often started at the same time as steroids for induction in European practice — the slow onset means the steroid does the induction work while the thiopurine gradually takes over. ECCO's "weak" recommendation acknowledges this pragmatic reality better than ACG's more categorical "strong." However, the very low-quality evidence for the recommendation is honest: the trials are old, small, and heterogeneous.
