# pimentel-ch3-1: The stool microbiome does not represent the small intestinal microbiome -- stool-based testing is misleading for SIBO and IBS

**Source:** [[Pimentel 2022 - Ch3 Gut Microbiome]] — Pimentel & Rezaie — The Microbiome Connection (2022), Ch 3

## The Claim

We now know that the microbiome in the 20-foot-long small intestine is radically different from the microbiome in the stool. Declaring that the stool represents the microbiome in the gut is incomplete and incorrect. In a recent paper (2020), we published for the first time the bacterial profile of the entire small intestine and colon (represented by stool). Even at the highest levels, the small bowel's profile is completely different. We now know that there's no way to know what's happening in the longest part of the gut simply by testing stool.

## Evidence

The REIMAGINE study (Pimentel et al., 2020): "we examined aspirates from the small intestine to define for the first time the composition of the small intestine's microbiome." The technique: "an endoscope into the mouth and snaked down into the small intestine. Then, using a newly designed sterile aspiration catheter, we remove samples from different parts of the small intestine." Pimentel cites Dr. Brian Lacy (Mayo Clinic) showing that "20 percent of samples using [non-sterile] approaches are useless because they are contaminated with mouth bacteria."

**Rating:** high

## Stakes

If stool testing cannot characterize the small intestinal microbiome, then the entire commercial stool-testing industry (Viome, Thryve, uBiome, etc.) is measuring the wrong thing for SIBO/IBS. This undercuts the functional medicine approach of using stool tests to diagnose dysbiosis and guide probiotic therapy. It also explains why commercial stool tests often produce contradictory or unactionable results for IBS patients.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** Commercial stool testing companies argue that stool provides actionable information about large intestinal dysbiosis, even if it doesn't represent the small intestine. Some functional medicine practitioners argue that stool testing can detect SIBO indirectly through markers (e.g., elevated fecal fat suggesting malabsorption). The debate is about whether stool testing has ANY value for SIBO assessment, not about whether it accurately represents the small intestine.

## Assessment

Pimentel's critique of stool testing is methodologically sound and practically important. His specific claim (stool ≠ small intestine) is uncontroversial. The more aggressive implication (stool testing is useless for SIBO) is debatable but worth taking seriously given his unique access to paired small intestine/stool samples. The REIMAGINE study is the only dataset of its kind and represents the best available evidence.
