# steinhart-drug-8: JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib) represent the first effective oral small-molecule option for biologic-refractory UC

**Source:** [[Steinhart 2018 - Ch7 Drug Therapy]] — Steinhart — Crohn's and Colitis: Understanding and Managing IBD, 3E (2018), Ch 7

## The Claim

Tofacitinib "has been shown to be effective in treating ulcerative colitis. It has not been properly tested in Crohn's disease." As a small molecule, it is orally administered, does not provoke antibody formation, and has a different side effect profile from biologics -- notably an increased risk of shingles.

## Evidence

Clinical trial data for UC. Steinhart notes that other JAK inhibitors (filgotinib, upadacitinib) were in development for Crohn's at the time of writing. The shingles risk is highlighted: "One infection that has been seen more often than expected in people taking tofacitinib is shingles."

## Confidence

**Rating:** high
**Rationale:** HIGH for UC efficacy. The shingles signal is real and was confirmed in post-marketing surveillance. Since 2018, upadacitinib has received approval for both UC and Crohn's, validating the JAK inhibitor

## Stakes

JAK inhibitors are the first oral advanced therapy for IBD. For patients who prefer pills over injections/infusions, they represent a significant quality-of-life advance. The safety concerns (shingles, potential cardiovascular/thrombotic risk at higher doses seen in rheumatoid arthritis) require ongoing monitoring.

## Assessment

Steinhart's coverage of JAK inhibitors is appropriately cautious given the 2018 publication date -- tofacitinib had only recently been approved for UC. The class has expanded significantly since then, and the oral administration advantage Steinhart identifies has proven to be a major factor in patient preference.
