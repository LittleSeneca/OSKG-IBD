# acg-cd2018-13: Thiopurines are effective for steroid-sparing and maintenance but NOT for short-term induction; TPMT testing is recommended (R19-R22)

**Source:** [[ACG Crohn's 2018 - Lichtenstein]] — Lichtenstein et al. — ACG Clinical Guideline: Management of Crohn's Disease (2018)

## The Claim

Thiopurines are effective for steroid-sparing and maintenance but NOT for short-term induction; TPMT testing is recommended (R19-R22)

## Evidence

The slow onset of thiopurines (8-12 weeks to clinical response) makes them unsuitable for acute induction. Their role is in steroid-sparing and maintenance. Adverse effects include allergic reactions, pancreatitis, myelosuppression, nausea, infections, hepatotoxicity, and malignancy (non-melanoma skin cancer, lymphoma). TPMT testing identifies patients at risk for severe myelosuppression. Routine monitoring of WBC and liver chemistries is required.

**Cross-guideline comparison:**
G6 (AGA 2021) provides more detailed guidance on thiopurine monitoring and the role of TPMT vs. NUDT15 testing. G8 (ECCO 2020) discusses thiopurine use more extensively in the context of combination therapy. The key divergence point: G6 and G8 both reflect the trend toward earlier biologic use and away from thiopurine monotherapy, which is only beginning to emerge in this 2018 guideline.

## Assessment

This is a nuanced set of recommendations. R19 (don't use for induction) is critical: thiopurines take too long. R20-R21 (use for steroid-sparing and maintenance) reflect the established evidence base. R22 (TPMT testing) is "strong, low evidence" — the recommendation is strong because the consequences of missing TPMT deficiency are catastrophic, even though the evidence for universal testing is observational. The challenge for clinicians: this guideline was published just as the field was pivoting from thiopurine-first to biologic-first strategies.
