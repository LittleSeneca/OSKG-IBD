# yamada-ucdx63-4: Endoscopic diagnosis of UC requires continuous, circumferential inflammation beginning at the rectum, with specific mucosal features aiding distinction from Crohn's

**Source:** [[Yamada 2022 - Ch63 Ulcerative Colitis Diagnosis]] — Yamada's Textbook of Gastroenterology, 7E (2022), Ch 63

## The Claim

The disease presents as diffuse, continuous inflammation beginning in the rectum, with proximal extension that varies among individual patients. For initial evaluation, however, it is advisable to perform a full colonoscopy with ileal examination, to rule out Crohn's disease and to determine the proximal margin of disease.

## Evidence

Table 63.1 (adapted from Hogan et al. 1980) provides a specificity index for endoscopic features distinguishing UC from CD. Features favoring UC: contiguous distribution (+++), circumferential (+++), rectal involvement (+++), friability (+++), granularity (+++), overt colitis (+++). Features favoring CD: cobblestoned (+++), ileal involvement (++++), discrete lesions (+++), ulcers >1cm (+++), linear ulcers (+++), serpiginous ulcers (+++). In partially treated patients, UC mucosal changes become more focal, potentially mimicking CD. In pancolitis, the ileocecal valve is patulous, and backwash ileitis (distal terminal ileum involvement) can occur, which must be distinguished from CD ileitis. Periappendiceal or cecal patch inflammation may be present in patients with distal disease without indicating CD.

## Confidence

**Rating:** high
**Rationale:** HIGH. The endoscopic features described are the standard teaching in gastroenterology training.

## Stakes

The distinction between UC and CD has massive therapeutic implications (surgery is curative for UC but not CD; certain biologics are approved for one but not the other). Approximately 2% of initial UC diagnoses are reclassified as CD or indeterminate colitis. The most common diagnostic error: treating partially treated UC as CD because the resolving inflammation appears patchy.

## Disagreement

**Who disagrees:** The traditional teaching that UC always spares the anus and always involves the rectum has exceptions. Rectal sparing can occur in treated or pediatric UC. Backwash ileitis challenges the "UC never involves the small bowel" rule.

**Alternative reading:** Not documented.

## Assessment

Table 63.1, though based on a 1980 paper, remains clinically useful because it codifies what experienced endoscopists recognize intuitively. The key takeaway: no single feature is diagnostic; the overall pattern (continuous, circumferential, rectal, superficial) is what matters. The caution about partially treated UC mimicking CD is particularly important -- this is a common source of misdiagnosis.
