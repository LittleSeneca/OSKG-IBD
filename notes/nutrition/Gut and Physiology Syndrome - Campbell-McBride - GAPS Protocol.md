# Campbell-McBride — GAPS Nutritional Protocol: Diet, SIBO, and IBD

Natasha Campbell-McBride is a physician with postgraduate degrees in neurology and human nutrition. She developed the GAPS (Gut and Psychology Syndrome / Gut and Physiology Syndrome) protocol based on her clinical experience treating her own child's autism and thousands of patients with chronic conditions linked to gut dysbiosis. This book (2020, second edition) expands the protocol from its original focus on neurological/psychiatric conditions to include the full spectrum of physiological diseases, including IBD and SIBO. The book is structurally unusual — a mix of patient testimonials, clinical reasoning, A-Z condition summaries, detailed protocol instructions, and extended discussions of food preparation and traditional practices.

## Critical Assessment

### Strengths

1. **The staged dietary approach** is clinically sensible for patients with active gut inflammation. The principle of starting with easily digestible, nutrient-dense foods and gradually reintroducing more complex foods as tolerance improves mirrors medical nutritional management of IBD flares (though GAPS is more restrictive than standard low-residue diets).

2. **The emphasis on meat stock as a gut-healing food** has a mechanistic basis. Gelatin, glycine, proline, and glutamine ARE substrates for intestinal epithelial repair. The use of meat stock (as opposed to bone broth) for shorter cooking time to minimize histamine/glutamate is a nuanced distinction that may matter for histamine-sensitive patients.

3. **Fermented foods as probiotic delivery vehicles** is supported by traditional dietary practices across cultures and emerging evidence on the microbiome benefits of fermented food consumption.

4. **Recognition of the stomach acid-SIBO connection** addresses a mechanism that is underrecognized in standard SIBO management (though it is not the whole story).

5. **Patient empowerment.** The book gives patients a concrete, actionable protocol. For patients who feel failed by conventional medicine, this is psychologically significant even if the evidence base is thin.

### Weaknesses

1. **Universal causal claims exceed evidence.** "All autoimmunity is born in the gut" is an overstatement. Genetic factors, extra-intestinal immune triggers, and environmental exposures can all contribute independently of gut health.

2. **No controlled trial evidence for GAPS.** Twenty years of clinical experience is valuable but not equivalent to controlled outcome data. The specific claims about remission rates, recovery timelines, and protocol superiority over other dietary approaches are unverified.

3. **Dismissal of antibiotic treatment for SIBO.** The claim that antibiotics and antimicrobials are misguided because they "try to kill the microbes" ignores the evidence base for rifaximin and the clinical reality that severe SIBO often requires antimicrobial treatment before dietary and motility interventions can succeed.

4. **No discussion of prokinetics.** The omission of motility dysfunction and the migrating motor complex as the primary mechanism in SIBO is the book's biggest gap for SIBO management. The GAPS diet may heal the gut lining and restore stomach acid, but it does not directly address impaired small intestinal motility — the root cause of SIBO recurrence in most patients.

5. **Extreme restrictiveness.** The Introduction Diet (stages 1-2) is essentially meat stock, soup, fermented juice, and egg yolks. For a malnourished IBD patient, the risk of inadequate caloric and micronutrient intake on this protocol is significant. The book does not adequately address monitoring for nutritional deficiencies during the restrictive phases.

6. **Structural disorganization.** The book is difficult to navigate as a clinical reference. Recommendations for specific conditions are scattered across the A-Z section, the protocol chapters, the bowel management chapter, and the patient testimonials. Extracting a clear treatment algorithm requires synthesis across multiple sections.

## Relevance to IBD/SIBO Knowledge Graph

For the IBD/SIBO knowledge graph, this book provides:

1. **A detailed dietary protocol** (GAPS Introduction Diet through Full GAPS) that represents one end of the dietary intervention spectrum — more restrictive than SCD, comparable to AIP in its elimination scope, distinct in its emphasis on meat stock and fermented foods over solid foods in the early stages.

2. **A patient-driven perspective** on gut healing that complements the clinical canon. Campbell-McBride's framework makes the gut the central organ in chronic disease — a perspective that resonates with the microbiome literature but that she extends beyond what the evidence supports.

3. **Clinical observations** (the SIBO-celiac overlap, the stomach acid-SIBO connection, the role of fermented foods in gut restoration) that are valuable as hypotheses even where they are not proven.

4. **A caution about antibiotics.** While Campbell-McBride's dismissal of antibiotic treatment for SIBO goes too far, her emphasis on root-cause addressing (stomach acid, gut barrier, microbiome restoration) is a necessary corrective to antibiotic-only approaches that produce temporary improvement followed by relapse.

**Tier 2 cross-reference role:** Cite when discussing the GAPS diet as a dietary intervention option, the stomach acid-SIBO connection, the therapeutic use of meat stock and fermented foods for gut healing, and the patient-experience perspective on dietary management of IBD. Do NOT cite as an authoritative source for SIBO treatment protocols (where the ACG guideline, Pimentel, and Siebecker take precedence) or for claims of universal autoimmune causation by gut dysbiosis.
