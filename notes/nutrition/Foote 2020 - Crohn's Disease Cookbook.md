# Foote -- The Crohn's Disease Cookbook: Dietary Principles and Meal Plans

Amanda Foote is a registered dietitian specializing in food-related medical conditions. This 2020 cookbook provides practical, evidence-based dietary guidance for Crohn's disease management, organized around an anti-inflammatory framework rather than a named dietary protocol. Unlike Gottschall (1994) which prescribes a specific diet (SCD), Foote provides flexible principles that can be adapted to individual triggers and tolerances. The book represents the mainstream dietetic consensus circa 2020: anti-inflammatory eating, low-residue during flares, gradual reintroduction, and trigger identification.

## Ch1: The Crohn's Disease Diet Connection

### Claim 1: Crohn's disease is caused by an overactive immune system attacking harmless microorganisms, with genetic and environmental contributions

**Author's claim:** "One known cause of Crohn's disease is an overactive immune system. A normal immune system only attacks harmful microorganisms, but some people's immune systems attack harmless microorganisms as well. This is believed to be a cause of chronic inflammation in Crohn's disease." Additional factors: genetic risk (family history), and environmental factors (urban developed-country diets "higher in processed and high-fat foods and lack healthy bacteria").

**Evidence presented:** Standard medical consensus summary. No primary research cited in this chapter.

**Confidence:** HIGH for the immune dysregulation model (well-established in IBD research). LOW for the specific diet-microbiome causation claim -- this is Foote's framing, not a proven mechanism. The "hygiene hypothesis" and Western diet links are epidemiological associations, not proven causes.

**What's at stake:** Foote's framing places dietary intervention within a multi-factorial disease model (immune + genetic + environmental), which is more measured than Gottschall's primarily dietary model of pathogenesis.

**My assessment:** Standard introductory material. Competent but unoriginal. The value is in the dietitian's perspective: nutrition is positioned as a management tool, not a cure.

### Claim 2: Malabsorption in IBD affects specific nutrients depending on inflammation location

**Author's claim:** "Inflammation in the small intestine can cause malabsorption of nutrients, while inflammation in the large intestine can inhibit the absorption of water and electrolytes." Specific nutrient concerns: B vitamins (especially folate), vitamins C, D, K, calcium, selenium, zinc.

**Evidence presented:** Standard nutritional physiology. Signs of malnutrition listed: fatigue, light-headedness, paleness, numbness, tingling, bruising, bone/muscle pain, hair loss, muscle cramping, appetite loss, taste/smell changes.

**Confidence:** HIGH. Site-specific malabsorption is well-documented. Small intestinal Crohn's → fat-soluble vitamin and B12 malabsorption; colonic disease → water and electrolyte losses. The nutrient list is consistent with clinical guidelines.

**What's at stake:** This maps the nutritional consequences of IBD to specific dietary targets. Unlike SCD which attempts to correct malabsorption by carbohydrate restriction, Foote's approach is to supplement around the malabsorption while eating whatever foods can be tolerated.

**My assessment:** Solid applied nutrition. This site-specific framework is clinically useful and too often missing from patient-facing dietary protocols.

## Ch2: Food Guidance for Crohn's Disease

### Claim 3: An anti-inflammatory diet is the foundation for Crohn's management, with 10 specific "power foods"

**Author's claim:** "One of the best ways to fight inflammation is to adopt an anti-inflammatory diet." The 10 anti-inflammatory power foods: turmeric, ginger, cinnamon, olive oil, fatty fish, berries/citrus, tomato, creamy nut/seed butters, garlic, dark chocolate.

**Evidence presented:** General nutritional science consensus. No specific trials cited for this food list. The anti-inflammatory framing draws on Mediterranean diet research and individual food component studies (curcumin in turmeric, omega-3s in fatty fish, polyphenols in berries).

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. Individual anti-inflammatory foods have supporting evidence (turmeric/curcumin has RCTs in IBD; omega-3s have mixed results). The aggregate "anti-inflammatory diet" concept is supported by Mediterranean diet trials showing reduced inflammatory markers. But the specific list of 10 is Foote's curation, not a validated protocol.

**What's at stake:** This is the mainstream dietetic alternative to named protocols (SCD, low-FODMAP, CDED). It's more flexible and easier to follow but less specific -- "eat anti-inflammatory foods" is less actionable than "eliminate all grains."

**Who disagrees:** Some SCD advocates would note that several anti-inflammatory foods (garlic, tomato, citrus) are potentially problematic for some IBD patients. The low-FODMAP diet restricts garlic, onion, and some fruits. Foote's inclusive approach may overestimate tolerability.

**Alternative reading:** The anti-inflammatory diet may work primarily through caloric adequacy and nutritional repletion rather than specific anti-inflammatory compounds. Malnourished patients improving on ANY adequate diet could explain apparent anti-inflammatory effects.

**My assessment:** The anti-inflammatory framing is sensible as a remission maintenance strategy. It's less useful as a flare intervention (when many anti-inflammatory foods become poorly tolerated). Foote's strength is in providing a positive, additive framework ("eat more of these") rather than a restrictive one.

### Claim 4: During flares, a low-residue diet with specific healing foods should be followed

**Author's claim:** The 10 healing foods for flare-ups: hot/cold cereals (including oatmeal), white pasta, white rice, white bread, potatoes, bananas, avocado, poultry, fish, well-cooked skinless vegetables. The 10 foods to avoid during flares: whole nuts/seeds, popcorn, high-fiber foods (whole grains, brown rice, raw fruits/vegetables with skins), dairy, spicy foods, high-fat/fried foods, gas-causing foods (beans, legumes, cruciferous vegetables), red meat, added sugars/artificial sweeteners, caffeine/carbonation/alcohol.

**Evidence presented:** Clinical dietetics consensus. The low-residue/low-fiber approach during acute IBD flares is standard practice. The "10 healing foods" list overlaps substantially with the traditional "BRAT" diet (bananas, rice, applesauce, toast) expanded for nutritional adequacy.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM-HIGH for the general principle (low-residue during flares reduces mechanical irritation and diarrhea). LOW for the specific food lists being optimized -- no comparative trials of different flare diets exist.

**What's at stake:** The flare diet represents the practical minimum of dietary restriction. Unlike SCD (which restricts grains, potatoes, and many of Foote's "healing foods"), the low-residue approach prioritizes easily digested starches as energy sources during catabolic states.

**Who disagrees:** SCD would eliminate white pasta, white rice, white bread, cereals, oatmeal, and potatoes -- all of Foote's healing staples. The SCD model says these starches fuel bacterial fermentation and perpetuate the vicious cycle. The low-residue model says they provide needed calories without mechanical irritation. This is a fundamental disagreement about whether fermentable substrate or mechanical irritation is the primary dietary problem in IBD flares.

**Alternative reading:** Both approaches may work for different patients. Patients with stricturing Crohn's (narrowed intestinal lumen) genuinely need low-residue to prevent obstruction regardless of fermentation effects. Patients with inflammatory/microbial-driven disease may benefit more from SCD's fermentation reduction.

**My assessment:** This is the central tension in IBD dietary therapy: low-residue (feed the patient, minimize mechanical damage) vs. SCD/low-FODMAP (starve the microbes, reduce fermentation). The two approaches recommend opposite foods for some categories (grains, potatoes). There is no head-to-head trial. Clinical wisdom suggests low-residue for obstructive/stricturing disease and fermentable-carbohydrate restriction for diarrheal/inflammatory/microbial-driven disease, but this is expert opinion, not evidence.

### Claim 5: Food triggers should be identified through systematic single-food elimination, not blanket restriction

**Author's claim:** "I recommend that you only eliminate one food (or a food grouping, like dairy or wheat) at a time. Eliminating multiple foods at a time makes it difficult to determine which food is bothering you." Elimination trials may take up to six weeks to determine trigger status. Common triggers: gluten, dairy, red meat, particular fruits/vegetables.

**Evidence presented:** Clinical dietetics practice standard. No trials comparing elimination strategies for Crohn's.

**Confidence:** MEDIUM. The single-food elimination approach is methodologically sound for identifying triggers but assumes triggers are independent (not synergistic) and that symptoms respond on a timescale of days-to-weeks. In practice, IBD triggers may interact (e.g., a food only triggers when inflammation is already present) and responses may be delayed.

**What's at stake:** This is the pragmatic alternative to protocol diets. Instead of eliminating entire food categories based on a theory (SCD, low-FODMAP), Foote advocates empiric, individualized trigger identification. This is more tailored but requires more patient effort and discipline.

**Who disagrees:** Protocol diet advocates would argue that systematic elimination of all potential triggers simultaneously is more efficient and that individual trigger identification is confounded by delayed reactions and interaction effects. The SCD and low-FODMAP eliminate broad categories first, then reintroduce individually.

**My assessment:** Foote's approach is the dietitian's standard of care and is appropriate for patients who can tolerate structured self-experimentation. For patients in severe flares who cannot afford months of trial-and-error, a protocol diet that eliminates known triggers preemptively may be more practical. The approaches are complementary: start with a protocol diet, then use single-food reintroduction to personalize.

## Ch3: Meal Plans

### Claim 6: Structured meal planning for both flare and remission states improves dietary adherence and nutritional adequacy

**Author's claim:** Two distinct one-week meal plans are provided: a "Meal Plan for Flare-Ups" (low-fiber, dairy-free, gentle foods) and a "Meal Plan for Remission" (more variety, more fiber, more protein sources). The flare plan emphasizes: oatmeal, white rice, white pasta, potatoes, poultry, fish, melon, banana, avocado, well-cooked vegetables. The remission plan adds: berries, peanut butter, eggs, pork, beef, tofu, tortillas, pitas, a wider range of vegetables.

**Evidence presented:** Practical dietetics. No comparative trial data on meal planning vs. ad hoc eating in IBD.

**Confidence:** LOW for the specific meal plans being optimal. HIGH for the general principle that planned eating improves nutritional adequacy. The meal plans are illustrative, not prescriptive -- Foote explicitly states they should be modified to individual needs.

**What's at stake:** The meal plans operationalize the dietary principles from Ch2. They demonstrate that a Crohn's diet can be varied, appealing, and nutritionally complete -- countering the perception that IBD diets are necessarily bland or restrictive.

**My assessment:** The meal plans are pedagogically useful but not clinically validated. The flare plan provides ~2000 calories/day (estimated from recipe portions) and the remission plan ~2200-2400, which is appropriate for weight maintenance in adults. The key contribution is demonstrating feasibility: a Crohn's diet doesn't have to be miserable.

### Claim 7: Meal prepping reduces stress and improves dietary compliance during flares

**Author's claim:** "Having ready-to-eat items available takes the stress out of having to prepare something if you are not feeling well or are in a time crunch." Strategies: double recipes, freezer-safe storage, keep pantry and fridge stocked with staples.

**Evidence presented:** Practical experience, not research.

**Confidence:** LOW (no direct evidence in IBD). MEDIUM for general behavioral nutrition principles (advance preparation improves dietary adherence in multiple chronic disease populations).

**My assessment:** Sensible practical advice. The behavioral/operational aspects of dietary therapy (shopping, prepping, cooking while symptomatic) are underappreciated in most dietary protocols. Foote's attention to these is a strength of the book.

## Key Dietary Comparisons

### Foote (Crohn's Cookbook) vs. Gottschall (SCD)

| Dimension | Foote (2020) | Gottschall (1994) |
|-----------|-------------|-------------------|
| Framework | Anti-inflammatory, flexible | Specific Carbohydrate Diet, rigid |
| Grains | Allowed (white preferred during flares) | ALL forbidden |
| Potatoes | Allowed (healing food) | Forbidden |
| Dairy | Lactose-free OK; avoid if trigger | Only 24hr yoghurt and dry curd cottage cheese |
| Legumes | Avoid during flares (gas) | Some allowed after soaking (3 months in) |
| Sugar | Limit added sugar | All sucrose forbidden |
| Evidence basis | Dietetics consensus, general nutrition science | Historical clinical observation, biochemical model |
| Patient role | Active self-experimenter | Follower of protocol |
| Timeline | Weeks (elimination diet) | Years (2+ for Crohn's/UC) |

### Foote vs. Low-FODMAP

Foote's approach is more permissive than low-FODMAP. She allows garlic, onion, wheat (white bread/pasta), and some high-FODMAP fruits. Her "anti-inflammatory" framing is additive (eat more of X) while low-FODMAP is subtractive (eliminate these specific fermentable carbohydrates). The two approaches converge on: avoid legumes during flares, limit dairy if lactose intolerant, and prefer well-cooked vegetables.

### Foote vs. Low-Residue

Foote's flare diet IS essentially a low-residue diet: white grains, well-cooked skinless vegetables, tender proteins, avoidance of fiber, nuts, seeds, skins. This is the standard of care for stricturing Crohn's and acute flares. The key difference from protocols like SCD is that Foote doesn't attempt to reduce fermentation, only mechanical irritation.

## Cross-Cutting Assessment

| Claim | Confidence | Most Vulnerable To |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| Overactive immune system as cause | HIGH | Standard model |
| Site-specific malabsorption | HIGH | Established nutritional physiology |
| Anti-inflammatory power foods | MEDIUM | Aggregate, not individual food validation |
| Low-residue flare diet | MEDIUM-HIGH | No comparative flare diet trials |
| Single-food elimination for triggers | MEDIUM | Interaction effects, delayed reactions |
| Structured meal plans | LOW (specific plans) | Not validated against ad hoc eating |
| Meal prepping improves compliance | LOW (IBD-specific) | General behavioral principle |

**Strongest section:** Ch2's food guidance -- the "enjoy/avoid" framework is clear, evidence-aligned, and more flexible than protocol diets. The low-residue flare recommendations are standard of care.

**Weakest section:** The anti-inflammatory food list -- aggregating individual foods with anti-inflammatory properties into a "diet" assumes additive effects that may not exist. Garlic and tomato are anti-inflammatory in isolation but may trigger symptoms in IBD patients.

**Cross-reference note:** Foote's book represents the "mainstream dietetics" pole of the IBD dietary spectrum. The other poles are: SCD (Gottschall) at the restrictive/mechanistic end, low-FODMAP at the fermentation-targeted middle, elemental diet (Thompson) at the most restrictive/medicalized end, and CDED at the evidence-based middle. The lack of head-to-head comparisons between these approaches is the single largest evidence gap in IBD dietary therapy.
