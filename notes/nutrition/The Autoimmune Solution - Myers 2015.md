# The Autoimmune Solution — Myers (2015)

A functional medicine physician's protocol for reversing autoimmune disease through diet, gut healing, detoxification, and stress reduction. Amy Myers is an MD who developed Graves' disease (autoimmune hyperthyroidism) and found conventional medicine's drug-first approach inadequate. She now runs a functional medicine clinic in Austin, Texas. The book codifies "The Myers Way" — a four-pillar protocol she claims has helped thousands of patients reverse autoimmune conditions and discontinue immunosuppressive medications.

## Core Thesis

Autoimmune disease is not inevitable or irreversible. Genetic predisposition accounts for only 25% of risk; environmental factors — diet, gut health, toxins, infections, and stress — account for the other 75%. By addressing these environmental factors through a systematic protocol, patients can reverse autoimmune conditions, not merely manage symptoms with drugs. Leaky gut is the necessary precondition for autoimmunity; heal the gut and the autoimmune process can be arrested.

## Key Concepts

### 1. The Autoimmune Spectrum

Myers introduces the concept of an "autoimmune spectrum" — a progression from mild inflammation through increasing immune dysregulation to full-blown autoimmune diagnosis. People with symptoms like fatigue, brain fog, joint pain, skin issues, or digestive problems may be "on the spectrum" without having crossed the diagnostic threshold. The goal is to move people back down the spectrum before they develop diagnosable disease. This is a useful framework for understanding IBD prodrome and the progression from gut symptoms to diagnosed Crohn's/UC.

### 2. Leaky Gut as the Common Pathway

Drawing on Alessio Fasano's research at Harvard, Myers argues that leaky gut (increased intestinal permeability) is a prerequisite for autoimmunity. The tight junctions of the intestinal epithelium become compromised, allowing partially digested food proteins, toxins, and microbes into the bloodstream. The immune system mounts antibodies against these foreign entities, and through molecular mimicry, may begin attacking self-tissue.

Myers identifies multiple causes of leaky gut: gluten (universally problematic), dairy, grains, legumes, GMOs, NSAIDs, antibiotics, acid-blocking medications, birth control pills, stress, gut infections (SIBO, yeast overgrowth, parasites), and environmental toxins. This is a broader list than most gastroenterology sources would endorse, but the core mechanism is consistent with mainstream intestinal permeability research.

### 3. The Four Pillars of The Myers Way

**Pillar 1: Heal Your Gut.** Remove inflammatory foods, add gut-healing nutrients (L-glutamine, zinc, collagen, bone broth), restore beneficial bacteria with probiotics, and address infections (SIBO, yeast overgrowth, parasites). Myers explicitly recognizes SIBO as a contributor to leaky gut and autoimmunity — a connection less commonly made in mainstream autoimmune literature.

**Pillar 2: Get Rid of Gluten, Grains, and Legumes.** Gluten is treated as universally problematic for autoimmune patients, not just those with celiac disease, because it triggers zonulin release which opens tight junctions. All grains and legumes are eliminated because they contain lectins, phytates, and other compounds Myers views as gut-irritating. This is a more restrictive approach than most evidence-based dietary guidelines for IBD, and closer to the Paleo Autoimmune Protocol (AIP).

**Pillar 3: Tame the Toxins.** Environmental toxins (heavy metals, mycotoxins from mold, BPA, phthalates, pesticides) burden the liver and immune system. Myers recommends detoxification support including glutathione, milk thistle, and avoidance of common toxin sources. The toxicology evidence for this pillar is thinner than for the dietary pillars — the claims rest more on clinical experience than controlled studies.

**Pillar 4: Heal Your Infections and Relieve Your Stress.** Chronic infections (Epstein-Barr, Lyme, gut pathogens) and chronic stress both drive inflammation. Myers emphasizes that stress management is not optional — cortisol directly affects intestinal permeability and immune function. This pillar aligns with the psychoneuroimmunology literature on stress and autoimmune disease.

### 4. The Gut-Health Timeline

A distinctive feature of Myers' clinical approach is tracing autoimmune disease back to birth: C-section delivery (missed vaginal microbial inoculation), formula feeding (missed breast milk bacteria and immune factors), childhood ear infections treated with antibiotics (microbiome disruption), teenage acne treated with more antibiotics (further microbiome damage), and a high-sugar diet feeding pathogenic overgrowth. This timeline approach to gut health is clinically useful for understanding IBD etiology, even if the causal claims about specific events are retrospective and impossible to verify individually.

### 5. Food-as-Medicine Dietary Protocol

The Myers Way diet eliminates: gluten, all grains (including rice, quinoa, corn), legumes (including soy, peanuts, beans), dairy, eggs, nightshades (tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, potatoes), sugar, alcohol, and processed foods. What remains is effectively a paleo-AIP hybrid: grass-fed meat, wild fish, organic vegetables (except nightshades), limited fruit, healthy fats (coconut oil, olive oil, avocado), and bone broth. The 30-day protocol is followed by systematic reintroduction to identify individual trigger foods.

### 6. Supplements as Necessary Adjuncts

Myers argues that diet alone is insufficient in the modern environment because: (a) food is less nutrient-dense due to soil depletion, (b) environmental toxin exposure is higher, (c) gut damage impairs nutrient absorption, and (d) genetic variants (MTHFR, GSTM1, COMT) affect detoxification capacity. The supplement protocol includes: a multivitamin, L-glutamine (for gut repair), probiotics (100+ billion CFU), digestive enzymes, omega-3s, vitamin D, glutathione or NAC (for detox), and anti-microbial herbs for SIBO/yeast (berberine, oregano oil, caprylic acid).

## SIBO-Specific Content

Myers includes a SIBO symptom questionnaire and recognizes SIBO as a cause of leaky gut. Her SIBO protocol modifies the base diet by limiting starchy vegetables to 2 cups/day and fruit to 1 cup/day (starving the bacteria of fermentable carbohydrates), plus antimicrobial supplements. She does not discuss breath testing, the hydrogen/methane distinction, IMO, or prokinetics in any detail. Her SIBO approach is symptom-driven and supplement-based, not evidence-based by gastroenterology standards. It is functional medicine SIBO management — useful as a patient-driven approach but not a substitute for the Cedars-Sinai protocol (Pimentel) or ACG guideline-concordant care.

## Relevance to IBD/SIBO

**Direct IBD relevance:** Myers treats IBD as one of the autoimmune conditions her protocol addresses. The leaky gut-autoimmunity connection is directly applicable to Crohn's and UC pathogenesis. The gut-healing pillar (L-glutamine, bone broth, probiotics) aligns with the nutritional support many IBD patients seek during remission maintenance.

**SIBO relevance:** Myers is one of the few popular functional medicine authors who explicitly names SIBO and integrates it into an autoimmune protocol. However, her SIBO management is less sophisticated than dedicated SIBO sources (Pimentel, Siebecker, Sarna). She treats SIBO primarily through dietary carbohydrate restriction and herbal antimicrobials, without addressing the motility dysfunction that is now understood to be the primary underlying mechanism.

**Dietary relevance:** The Myers Way is more restrictive than standard IBD dietary recommendations (low-residue, low-FODMAP, SCD). Its elimination of all grains, legumes, and nightshades goes beyond what the evidence supports for most IBD patients. However, for patients who have not responded to less restrictive approaches, the Myers Way or AIP may be worth considering as an elimination diet trial.

**Practical value:** The 30-day meal plan, recipes, and supplement schedule make the protocol actionable. For a patient newly diagnosed with an autoimmune condition who wants a structured dietary intervention, this book provides a clear on-ramp. The emphasis on working with a physician to taper medications (rather than stopping abruptly) is responsible.

## Limitations and Omissions

1. **Evidence quality:** The book rests heavily on clinical experience and patient anecdotes. While many of the underlying mechanisms (leaky gut, gluten-zonulin, microbiome-immune interaction) have research support, the specific claim that the Myers Way protocol reverses diagnosed autoimmune disease is not supported by controlled trials. This is a patient-experience book, not a peer-reviewed treatment outcome study.

2. **Overgeneralization of gluten harm:** Myers treats gluten as universally harmful for all autoimmune patients. The evidence for non-celiac gluten sensitivity is real but less settled than the book suggests — many studies show that FODMAPs, not gluten, drive symptoms in self-identified gluten-sensitive patients without celiac disease.

3. **Nightshade elimination lacks evidence:** The exclusion of nightshade vegetables (tomatoes, peppers, eggplant) is based on the theoretical concern that alkaloids may increase intestinal permeability. The clinical evidence for this claim is weak. This restriction may unnecessarily limit vegetable intake in patients who already struggle with dietary variety during flares.

4. **No discussion of fiber paradox in active IBD:** Like The Good Gut, this book does not address the tension between fiber for microbiome health and fiber restriction during active IBD flares. The protocol effectively eliminates grains and legumes (major fiber sources) while including vegetables — but for a patient in a flare who cannot tolerate raw vegetables, the fiber gap could be significant.

5. **Supplement commercial interest:** Myers sells her own supplement line, creating an inherent conflict of interest. The supplement recommendations are extensive and expensive, and not all are evidence-supported for autoimmune disease specifically.

6. **SIBO management is superficial:** SIBO is acknowledged but not addressed in clinical depth. No breath testing protocol. No prokinetic strategy. No distinction between hydrogen and methane types. The dietary approach (carbohydrate restriction) is a generic anti-microbial strategy rather than SIBO-specific management.

## Assessment

The Autoimmune Solution is a well-structured functional medicine protocol that places gut health at the center of autoimmune disease reversal. Its strengths are: (a) the autoimmune spectrum concept, which is clinically useful for understanding disease progression; (b) the gut-health timeline approach, which connects childhood events to adult disease in a patient-empowering narrative; (c) explicit recognition of SIBO as a contributor to systemic autoimmunity; and (d) a practical, actionable 30-day protocol.

Its weaknesses are: (a) claims of disease reversal that exceed the evidence base; (b) overgeneralized dietary restrictions (nightshades, all grains) that may be unnecessary; (c) a supplement-heavy approach with a commercial conflict of interest; and (d) SIBO management that lacks the specificity found in dedicated SIBO sources.

For the IBD/SIBO knowledge graph, this book is a Tier 4 cross-reference source. It provides a patient-driven perspective on autoimmunity and gut healing that complements but does not replace the clinical canon (Sleisenger, Yamada, ACG guidelines). Its value is in the framework connecting gut permeability, diet, and immune function — concepts that recur across more authoritative sources but are rarely integrated into a single patient-facing protocol. Use it as a reference for the leaky gut-autoimmunity connection and the functional medicine approach to dietary intervention, but verify specific treatment claims against higher-tier sources.

**Tier 4 cross-reference role:** Link when discussing leaky gut as a mechanism in IBD pathogenesis, the autoimmune spectrum concept, or functional medicine dietary approaches to gut healing. Do NOT cite as an authoritative source for SIBO treatment protocols or claims of autoimmune disease reversal.
