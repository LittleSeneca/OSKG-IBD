SELECTED SUMMARIES
Vitamin D and Inﬂammatory Bowel Disease: Chicken or Egg?
Ananthakrishnan AN, Khalili H, Higuch LM, et al. Higher predicted vitamin D status is associated with
reduced risk of Crohn’s disease. Gastroenterology. 2012;143:482–489.
V
itamin D deﬁciency is described in patients with
inﬂammatory bowel disease (IBD), but it is unclear if
this is a consequence of the disease or a contributor to the
disease pathogenesis.
Ananthakrishnan et al1 conducted a prospective cohort
study of 72,719 women (age 40–73 years) enrolled in the
Nurses’ Health Study in which vitamin D status and new
diagnoses of Crohn’s disease (CD) or ulcerative colitis (UC)
were assessed over a 22-year period from 1986 to 2008. At
inception, women completed an assessment of diet and life-
style, from which 25-hydroxy vitamin D (25(OH)D) status
was predicted using a validated model. A single assessment
of 25(OH)D appears to be representative of long-term lev-
els.2 New cases of IBD were conﬁrmed until 2008.
For CD, they showed that prediagnosis predicted lev-
els of 25(OH)D were associated with a signiﬁcant reduc-
tion in risk of incident CD. The median interval between
assessment of predicted plasma 25(OH)D level and diagno-
sis of CD was 10 years. Compared with women who were
predicted to be vitamin D-deﬁcient (plasma 25(OH)D <20
ng/mL), the multivariate hazard ratio (HR) of CD was 0.38
(95% conﬁdence interval [CI], 0.15–0.97) for women pre-
dicted to be vitamin D-sufﬁcient deﬁned as a plasma
25(OH)D >30 ng/mL (Ptrend ¼ 0.048).
For UC, they showed that prediagnosis predicted lev-
els of 25(OH)D were associated with a reduction in risk of
incident UC, although this risk did not reach statistical sig-
niﬁcance. There was a signiﬁcant trend toward reduction in
UC risk with increasing vitamin D intake such that each
100 IU/day increase in total vitamin D intake resulted in a
10% reduction in UC risk.
The authors conclude that higher predicted plasma
levels of 25(OH)D signiﬁcantly reduces the risk for inci-
dent CD and nonsigniﬁcantly reduces the risk for UC and
suggest that vitamin D may be an important mediator in
the pathogenesis of CD and possibly UC.
COMMENT
There is compelling evidence from epidemiological
studies, observational studies, and animal models that vita-
min D plays a role in IBD pathogenesis. In particular, there
is an increased incidence of IBD among populations at
higher latitudes,3 and a seasonal variation in onset and exac-
erbations of IBD. Also a negative correlation has been found
between disease activity and serum 25(OH)D levels.4 Ani-
mals that lack the vitamin D receptor (VDR) are more sus-
ceptible to development of intestinal inﬂammation.5 A thera-
peutic effect of vitamin D is suggested by prevention and
amelioration of intestinal inﬂammation in animal models of
colitis such at IL-10KO or chemically induced trinitroben-
zene sulfonic acid (TNBS) colitis.5–7 A clinical trial in which
108 CD patients were randomized to 1200 IU vitamin D3 or
placebo demonstrated a reduced risk of relapse from 29% to
13% at 1 year in the vitamin D3-treated group, although the
result did not reach statistical signiﬁcance (P ¼ 0.06).8
However, although vitamin D deﬁciency is described
in patients with IBD and there is evidence for a role of
vitamin D in IBD pathogenesis, as discussed above, it has
been unclear in patients if this is a consequence of the dis-
ease or a contributor to the disease pathogenesis. The study
by Ananthakrishnan et al suggests that vitamin D may be
an important contributor to the disease pathogenesis.
The stronger association of 25(OH)D with CD (as
opposed to UC) is biologically plausible given the particu-
lar mechanistic links of vitamin D with NOD2 and autoph-
agy pathways, in that vitamin D induces NOD2 expression/
antimicrobial peptides9 and autophagy contributing to elim-
ination of intracellular mycobacteria in a cathelicidin-de-
pendent manner.10
Apart from stimulating further interest in the role of
vitamin D in IBD pathogenesis, the current work may suggest
targeted preventative therapy for at-risk patients such as those
with a positive family history of IBD. There is some evidence
of synergistic effects of vitamin D with immunomodulators
used in IBD and a role for vitamin D in restoring steroid-
responsiveness.11 However, larger trials of vitamin D as a
therapeutic strategy are required in IBD in prevention, main-
tenance of remission, and treatment of established disease.
