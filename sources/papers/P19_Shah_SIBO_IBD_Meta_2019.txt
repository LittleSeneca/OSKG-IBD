Summary
Background: Current data on small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) in patients
with inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) are controversial.
Aim: To conduct a systematic review and meta‐analysis to determine the preva-
lence of SIBO in patients with ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD).
Methods: Electronic databases were searched up to May 2018 for studies reporting
prevalence of SIBO in IBD patients. The prevalence rate of SIBO among IBD
patients and the odds ratio (OR) and 95% CI of SIBO in IBD patients compared with
controls were calculated.
Results: The final dataset included 11 studies (1175 adult patients with IBD and
407 controls), all utilising breath test for diagnosis of SIBO. The proportion of SIBO
in IBD patients was 22.3% (95% CI 19.92‐24.68). The OR for SIBO in IBD patients
was 9.51 (95% CI 3.39‐26.68) compared to non‐IBD controls, and high in both CD
(OR = 10.86; 95% CI 2.76‐42.69) and UC (OR = 7.96; 95% CI 1.66‐38.35). In
patients with CD, subgroup analysis showed the presence of fibrostenosing disease
(OR = 7.47; 95% CI 2.51‐22.20) and prior bowel surgery (OR = 2.38; 95% CI 1.65‐
3.44), especially resection of the ileocecal valve, increased the odds of SIBO. Individ-
ual studies suggest that combined small and large bowel disease but not disease
activity may be associated with SIBO.
Conclusions: Overall, there is a ninefold increase in the prevalence of SIBO in IBD
patients compared to controls. Prior surgery and the presence of fibrostenosing
disease are risk factors for SIBO in IBD.

INTRODUCTION
Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which includes Crohn's disease
(CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), are chronic and relapsing inflamma-
tory disorders of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract.1 The precise patho-
physiology of IBD is largely unknown but is thought to be related to
an inappropriate immune response to commensal bacteria in geneti-
cally susceptible hosts.2 Impaired intestinal barrier function, defects
in innate immunity, changes in microbial composition and the pres-
ence of mucosa‐associated and translocating bacteria have all been
described in IBD.3 There is growing evidence that gut microbial dys-
biosis, defined as the alteration in the composition, density and the
function of the intestinal microbes, is associated with both CD4–6
and UC7,8 as well as other intestinal and metabolic diseases.
Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) is a condition that is
also defined by excessive and/or abnormal microbiota in the small
bowel9–11 and thereby, is one manifestation of gut microbial dysbio-
sis. However, the potential pathogenic role of SIBO in GI conditions
is controversial. This stems from the lack of a universally accepted
and applied gold standard for diagnosis of SIBO.12 Presence of >105
colony forming units per millilitre (cfu/ml) of colonic‐type bacteria in
culture of jejunal aspirates is the traditionally accepted gold standard
for
diagnosis
SIBO.13,14
However,
concentrations
≥105 cfu
are
mostly seen in patients with blind loop syndrome and more recently
a bacterial concentration of >103 cfu/ml has been considered a clini-
cally relevant cut‐off criterion for diagnosing SIBO.15–17 However,
aspiration and culture of small intestine content is an invasive and
time‐consuming procedure and lacks universal acceptance in terms
of cut‐off values for diagnosing SIBO. In clinical practice, culture has
been largely replaced by breath tests,18 which are relatively quick,
simple, noninvasive surrogate tests for diagnosis of SIBO, but lack
sensitivity and specificity.15,19,20
The main protective factors preventing SIBO are gastric acid,
intact anatomical integrity of the small intestine21 and normal small
bowel motility.22 Other factors include the integrity of the intestinal
mucosa and its protective mucus layer, the enzymatic activities of
intestinal, pancreatic and biliary secretions, the protective effects of
some of the commensal microbiota, and the mechanical and physio-
logic properties of the ileocecal valve (IC)23,24 and local and systemic
immunity.25 For these reasons, patients with IBD, particularly those
with CD may exhibit a predisposition for SIBO.
Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth is frequently associated with
GI symptoms such as bloating, distension, flatulence, abdominal dis-
comfort, diarrhoea and weight loss26 and may even cause structural
changes such as atrophy of small intestinal villi27 with subsequent
alterations of small intestinal absorption. SIBO symptoms overlap
with those of IBD, often making it unclear if it is the cause, conse-
quence or an epiphenomenon in relation to the other disorder. Thus,
a SIBO diagnosis in patients with IBD may be challenging but may
have therapeutic implications. Antibiotic treatment of SIBO targets
dysbiosis and potentially improves symptoms28 and other conse-
quences of the altered mucosal or luminal bacterial colonisation.
However, there are only a few studies in the literature reporting the
efficacy of antibiotic treatment of bacterial overgrowth complicating
IBD.29–32
Several studies have reported an increased prevalence of SIBO
in patients with IBD.29,30,32–34 Many studies also report risk factors
that can predict the prevalence rate of SIBO in IBD patients, how-
ever, the results are conflicting. Thus, we performed a systematic
review and meta‐analysis and aimed to: (a) determine the prevalence
of SIBO in patients with IBD (and IBD subtypes, CD and UC); (b)
determine and compare the prevalence of SIBO in IBD patients and
control subjects; (c) identify potential risk factors for SIBO preva-
lence in IBD patients; and (d) assess the effect of antibiotic therapy
on symptom improvement in IBD patients with SIBO.

MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1
|
Search strategy
Electronic databases, including PUBMED, MEDLINE (OvidSP) and
EMBASE were searched from initiation up to May 2018 for all stud-
ies assessing the prevalence of SIBO in patients with IBD and/or UC
and/or CD. The search strategy included [‘SIBO’ OR ‘small intestinal
bacterial overgrowth’ OR ‘bacterial infections’ OR ‘breath tests’]
AND [(‘IBD’ OR ‘inflammatory bowel disease’ OR ‘UC’ OR ‘Ulcerative
colitis’ OR CD ‘Crohn's disease’) AND ‘prevalence’]. The initial search
was not limited to specific languages to capture all appropriate stud-
ies. ‘Snowball’ methods such as pursuing references of references
and electronic citation tracking to identify all the relevant articles
were employed. The detailed search strategy was developed in con-
sultation with a research librarian and the results of the literature
search are outlined in Figure 1.
2.2
|
Selection of studies
Two authors (GH and AS) independently conducted an initial screen
of identified abstracts and titles. Abstracts were eliminated in this
initial screening if they were case series, case reports, animal studies;
or if they did not investigate the association between SIBO and IBD
or UC or CD. Full texts of the remaining articles were retrieved and
reviewed. Studies that reported prevalence of SIBO in IBD patients,
compared proportion of SIBO in IBD vs non‐IBD and reported effi-
cacy data after antibiotic treatment of SIBO in IBD patients were all
eligible. Articles were considered for inclusion only if they reported
original data from prospective and retrospective cohort studies,
case‐control or nested case‐control studies of patients with clearly
diagnosed with IBD (including CD and UC), SIBO was diagnosed
using one of the clinically validated methods,15 sufficient information
to calculate the effect size was available, and the manuscript was
published in a peer‐reviewed journal as a full paper. Disagreements
between reviewers were resolved by mutual consensus after refer-
ence to the original published paper.

2.3
|
Data extraction and quality assessment
All data were extracted independently by two reviewers on to a
Microsoft Excel spreadsheet (XP professional edition; Microsoft
Corp, Redmond, Washington, USA), with disagreements resolved by
consensus. The following information was extracted from each study
independently by the two reviewers: author, year of publication,
journal, study design, country, source of controls, method of diagno-
sis of SIBO including test duration, quantity of substrate used and
the cut‐off criteria for diagnosis of SIBO, mean age, sex, concurrent
use of PPI and antibiotics, any significant co‐morbidities including
previous surgery for IBD patients and the control group. In addition,
for all patients with IBD, data regarding mode of diagnosis of IBD,
time since diagnosis, subtype (UC, CD or IBD‐Unclassified [IBD‐U]),
history of prior surgery (including the type, location and the number
of surgeries), disease location and behaviour, pharmacological treat-
ment, disease activity (utilising stool markers like faecal calprotectin
or composite indices like Crohn's disease activity index [CDAI]),
treatment of SIBO in IBD patients with antibiotics and objective and
subjective response post treatment. The current systematic review
and meta‐analysis were consistent with the proposals for the report-
ing
of

of

in

reviews and meta‐analysis statement requirements.36
2.4
|
Data analysis
In an initial step, case numbers of patients with IBD, UC, CD and
controls in the respective cohorts were determined. The number of
cases of SIBO diagnosed by breath tests (lactulose breath test [LBT],
glucose breath test [GBT]) for the cases and the control group was
calculated separately. In a second step, the pooled ORs and 95%
confidence intervals (CI) for the prevalence of SIBO in patients with
IBD and their respective controls were separately calculated. Meta‐
analyses according to IBD subtypes (UC and CD) and CD‐related risk
factors were also estimated. Further, we assessed methane positivity
on breath test in IBD patients and controls. Methane‐positive SIBO
on breath test was defined as rise over baseline in breath methane
concentrations of greater than or equal to 10 ppm.15 Lastly, we also
examined the pooled prevalence of SIBO in the IBD population and
subtypes (UC and CD).
Analyses for the association between SIBO and IBD were carried
out utilising the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS Version
23, Armonk NY: IBM Corporation) and comprehensive Meta‐analysis
(CMS) Version 3.3.070. Pooled estimates of disease prevalence were
calculated using a random effects model37 as all tests of between‐
study homogeneity indicated that variation between individual study
estimates was consistent with random sampling variation. Between‐
study variation was evaluated using Cochrane's test38 and was quan-
tified through the I2 index in which values close to 100 indicate sub-
stantial variation between studies while values close to zero indicate
minimal between‐study variation. Standard approaches (Egger Test39
and inspection of Funnel Plots) were applied to identify potential
publication biases. With a limited number of studies, the Funnel Plot
can be difficult to interpret, so we have primarily relied on Egger's
test to indicate the potential for publication bias. The statistical
package CMA utilised uses logit transformation of proportions and
the variance of the logit to estimate pooled event rates within
groups and to compare event rates between groups. If one or more
cells had a value of 0, then the CMA software automatically adds a
fixed value of 0.5 to the all the cells of the study results tables
where the problems occur for computation of log OR and variance.

RESULTS
3.1
|
Search results
The initial literature search revealed 232 publications. Of these, 26
published articles appeared to be relevant for the study question
and were retrieved for further evaluation. Fifteen articles were
excluded as ineligible leaving eleven appropriate studies (Figure 1).
Ten of these studies included patients with CD,29–34,40–,43 four
included patients with UC,33,34,43,44 while three included both UC
and CD patients (two case‐control studies33,43 and one cohort
study34). None of the studies included patients with unclassified
IBD. Five of the eleven studies were case control studies29,33,42–44
and the remaining six were cohort studies.30–32,40,41 All case‐control
studies included healthy volunteers in the non‐IBD control group;
however, one study45 included patients undergoing evaluation of
unexplained GI symptoms as a control group. We have used the
term ‘control’ to describe all in the ‘non‐IBD control group’. The
characteristics of all the studies in the current meta‐analysis includ-
ing the methodology pertaining to diagnosis of SIBO and patient
characteristics are outlined in Tables 1 and S1. Three of the eleven
studies were conducted in Italy,29–31 two in India43,44 and one each
in Canada,34 Belgium,32 Korea,33 Brazil,42 Spain41 and Germany.40

|
Prevalence of SIBO in IBD patients
In total, the eleven studies reported the prevalence of SIBO in 1175
IBD patients. Nine studies utilised GBT30,33,34,40–44 and only one
study each utilised LBT29 and
14C‐glycocholate breath test32 for
diagnosing SIBO in IBD patients (Table 1), hence we did not do a
subgroup analysis according to the type of breath test utilised to
diagnose SIBO in patients with IBD. Overall, 262/1175 (22.3%, 95%
CI 19.92‐24.68) IBD patients (Figure 2), 215/846 (25.4%, 95% CI
22.48‐28. 34) CD patients and 47/329 (14.3%, 95% CI 10.52‐18.08)
UC patients were positive for SIBO, (Figure S1). Heterogeneity was
low in the overall analysis (I2 = 33.94, P = 0.13, Figure 2) with no
clear evidence for publication bias (Figure 3).

3.3

Prevalence of SIBO in IBD patients and controls
Five of the of the eleven case‐control29,33,42–44 studies included 513
adult patients with IBD, 234 with CD, 279 patients with UC and
407 controls. The odds ratio (OR) for prevalence of SIBO in IBD
patients as compared to healthy controls was 9.51 (95% CI 3.39‐
26.68, Figure 4), with moderate heterogeneity detected between the
studies included I2 = 46.82, P = 0.11). Similarly, subgroup analysis by
IBD subtype showed increased prevalence of SIBO in CD (OR =
10.86; 95% CI 2.76‐42.69) and UC (OR = 7.99; 95% CI 1.66‐38.35).

presence of fibrostenosing disease (Figure S2) and prolonged orocae-
cal transit time (OCTT) in both in UC and CD patients. Breath
methane positivity was inversely associated with IBD (Figure S3) and
no association was
found between

Association between SIBO and CD behaviour,
location, duration since diagnosis and influence of
immunotherapy
How disease behaviour, location and duration since diagnosis poten-
tially impacted the prevalence of SIBO among individuals with CD
were also examined (Table 2). Eight studies29,31,33,34,40–43 examined
fibrostenosing disease as a predictor of SIBO among individuals with
CD. A subanalysis demonstrated increased odds of SIBO among CD
patients with fibrostenosing disease (OR = 7.47; 95% CI 2.51‐22.20,
Figure S2) compared to those with inflammatory type behaviour, but
with statistically significant heterogeneity (I2 = 70.93, P < 0.001).
Only one31 of four studies31,40–42 found a statistically significant
positive association between the prevalence of SIBO and disease
duration. Furthermore, two studies29,40 assessed the link between
SIBO and disease location. One of two studies found SIBO signifi-
cantly more prevalent in CD patients with involvement of both large
and small bowel, while the other failed to show any such association.
Finally, no association was found between thiopurine and/or biologic
treatment and prevalence of SIBO in CD patients.40,41
3.7
|
Association between SIBO and disease
activity, and symptoms
Five studies explored the link between disease activity (three
CDAI34,40,42 and two using faecal calprotectin33,42) and SIBO in CD
patients (Table 2). All three studies found no association between
positive breath test and CDAI. Only one of the two studies assessing
faecal calprotectin in SIBO patients found significantly higher faecal
calprotectin in SIBO‐positive CD patients as compared to controls
and SIBO‐negative CD patients. Four studies reported significantly
increased abdominal symptoms (bloating, flatus, satiety),33,40 mete-
orism (a swollen abdomen, generated by the increase in the amount
of intestinal gas),41 loose stools and increased stool frequency31,40
and low body weight40 in SIBO‐positive IBD patients.

there are no systematic reviews available in the literature. To the
best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta‐
analysis reporting the prevalence of SIBO in IBD patients. The final
selection included 11 studies conducted in eight countries world-
wide, with a total sample size of 1582 individuals. The overall preva-
lence of SIBO in IBD patients (22.30%) was significantly higher as
compared to controls (OR 9.5, 95% CI 3.39‐26.68). As expected, the
prevalence of SIBO was higher in CD (25.4%) as compared to UC
(14.3%), and despite UC being a colonic disease the SIBO prevalence
was also increased in UC compared to controls.
Multiple factors limit bacterial colonisation of the small intestine
that include low gastric pH, rapid transit of luminal contents towards
the ileum, the integrity of the intestinal mucosa and the protective
mucus layer46 as well as anti‐reflux properties of the ileocecal
valve24 combined with protective effects of commensal flora and
local and systemic immunity.25 Alterations of some of these factors
may contribute to the increased prevalence of SIBO in IBD patients,
particularly in patients who have had prior surgery or CD patients
with fibrostenosing disease. Notably, multiple bowel surgeries, espe-
cially those involving the resection of the ileocecal valve, and/or
presence of fibrostenosing disease, and/or involvement of both small
bowel and colon, appear to increase the risk for SIBO in CD
patients. However, SIBO is not only more prevalent in CD, but also
UC. While UC is primarily a colonic disease, it can be speculated that
alterations of immune function, alterations of motility and/or dysbio-
sis may contribute to the manifestation of SIBO in patients without
evident small bowel pathology. There was on the other hand no
association of SIBO with the use of immunotherapy (thiopurine and/
or biologic), disease activity or duration of disease. Such findings
suggest interventions that affect structural rather than immunologi-
cal characteristics of the large intestine increase the risk of SIBO in
CD patients.

clinical predictors of bacterial overgrowth. Similarly, in this system-
atic review, SIBO‐positive IBD patients had significantly increased
nonspecific abdominal symptoms (bloating, flatus, satiety), mete-
orism, weight loss and loose stools and increased stool frequency.
Across the various studies, we were unable to identify a single
symptom or cluster of symptoms that could reliably clinically predict
SIBO in IBD patients. However, there is now the notion that in some
patients with IBD, IBS‐type symptoms persist.48 It appears at least
possible that these symptoms in a subgroup of patients are due to
SIBO. It is also possible that SIBO‐related symptoms are more severe
due to alterations of structural and/or sensory function in patients
with IBD due to the effects of inflammatory mediators.49
Antibiotic treatment of SIBO targets dysbiosis or other conse-
quences of the altered mucosal or luminal bacterial colonisation and
potentially improves symptoms.28 A recent systematic review and
meta‐analysis by Gatta et al,50 of 32 studies involving 1331 patients
found rifaximin to be safe and effective for treatment of SIBO in adult
patients with unexplained GI symptoms, with an overall eradication
rate according to intention‐to‐treat analysis of 70.8% (95% CI: 61.4‐
78.2). Furthermore, in the literature, several randomised controlled tri-
als51,52 have reported the efficacy of antibiotic therapy in IBS patients
but none in patients with IBD. In this systematic review, sub‐analyses
showed that short course of broad‐spectrum antibiotic was effective
in treating SIBO in IBD patients. More than 90% patient had normali-
sation of positive breath test post antibiotic treatment and the major-
ity of SIBO‐positive IBD patients treated with antibiotics reported
improvement in overall GI symptoms. These results in IBD need con-
firming in a large placebo‐controlled randomised trial.
Methane and hydrogen concentrations in the exhaled breath after
a glucose challenge are now widely used as diagnostic measures to
identify patients with SIBO. Interestingly, methane positivity was sig-
nificantly less prevalent in IBD patients compared to controls (OR =
0.72; 95% CI 0.11‐4.51). Methane and hydrogen produced during
anaerobic fermentation in the distal large intestine (or in the small
intestine in SIBO) are excreted in flatus and expired breath.53 Although
H2 production appears more ubiquitous, approximately 35%‐50% of
healthy subjects predominantly produce methane (CH4 producers).54–
57 Thus, while our findings show the prevalence of methane producers
in the non‐IBD control group is substantially lower at 12.8% compared
to the general population, it is still threefold higher than IBD patients.
Researchers have focused on the striking association between
methane production and constipation.58 Indeed, a systematic review
and meta‐analysis of nine studies59 showed methane positivity is more
common in patients with functional constipation or constipation pre-
dominant irritable bowel syndrome compared to nonconstipated con-
trols. There is experimental60 and clinical evidence59,61 that methane
slows the intestinal transit implying that methane‐producing microor-
ganisms may be risk factors for constipation. However, human studies
have shown that slow intestinal transit may facilitate growth of metha-
nogenic bacteria.54,62 Thus, it remains to be determined if methane
produces constipation or if the latter is because of its effect on intesti-
nal transit. On the other hand, very few studies have examined the
association between methane excretion and other GI symptoms. An
initial study by Mckay et al55 showed a significantly lower concentra-
tion of breath methane excretion in patients with chronic diarrhoeal
states such as IBD and pneumatosis cystoides intestinalis as compared
to healthy controls. These results have been replicated in subsequent
studies in patients with IBD.43,63 Thus, different GI symptoms may be
attributed to the qualitative rather than quantitative modifications of
the intestinal gas composition, reflecting changes in the intestinal
microbiota. Methane gas slows intestinal transit but does not appear
to explain the slower gut transit in IBD.
The current meta‐analysis is not without limitations. Although the
majority of case‐control studies in this systematic review included
healthy volunteers in the non‐IBD control group, one study included
patients undergoing evaluation of unexplained GI symptoms, which
could introduce potential biases. There was moderately high hetero-
geneity among the case‐control studies included in our primary analy-
sis, suggesting that the prevalence of SIBO in IBD patients may have
been overestimated. On the other hand, there was only minimal
heterogeneity in the cohort studies reporting the prevalence of SIBO
in IBD patients. There was also a moderately high heterogeneity in
most of the studies included in the sub‐analyses. However, it is impor-
tant to note that the power of funnel plots and Egger's test is limited
to distinguish chance from real asymmetry when less than 10 studies
are included in a meta‐analysis.64 This met‐analysis looked at the vari-
ous risk factors for SIBO in IBD patients, but no effect sizes or ORs
could be calculated regarding the prevalence of SIBO and the use of
immunotherapies (thiopurine and/or biologic), disease activity or dura-
tion of disease. This information was outlined in a descriptive fashion
in the selected studies and did not allow data extraction to be per-
formed for the appropriate statistical analyses.
Interestingly, none of the studies included in this systematic
review have utilised small bowel culture and aspirate (direct test), the
current gold standard for diagnosing SIBO.12 All the studies have used
breath tests (indirect test), which are surrogate markers for diagnosing
bacterial overgrowth. This is mainly to overcome the limitations of
direct tests that are invasive, time consuming and require an endo-
scopy. However, compared with the direct aspiration method, the
GBT has a sensitivity of 62.5% and a specificity of 81.7% and the LBT
has a sensitivity of 52.4% and a specificity of 85.7%.65 Lastly, none of
the studies have evaluated the effect of proton pump inhibitor (PPI)
use on SIBO prevalence in IBD patients. Chronic acid suppression and
the resultant hypochlorhydria associated with PPI use have been
hypothesised to alter the intraluminal environment to promote coloni-
sation by large intestinal flora in the small intestine.66 Several studies
have explored PPI use as a risk factor for SIBO in various GI condi-
tions. However, the results have been conflicting with some finding a
positive67 and others finding a negative association.68
In summary, this is the first systematic review and meta‐analyses,
utilising the breath test as a diagnostic modality which demonstrates a
significantly increased prevalence of SIBO in IBD patients, as com-
pared to controls. The prevalence of SIBO is higher in CD patients
compared to UC patients but not significantly higher. A history of mul-
tiple gastrointestinal surgeries and fibrostenosing disease substantially
increase the risk of SIBO in CD patients. Methane positivity on breath
